The Gulf of Guinea is an inlet of the Atlantic Ocean off the west coast of Africa. In the UAP record it is known as the site of the 1902 Fort Salisbury encounter with an unidentified submerged object (USO), one of the most striking early-20th-century maritime sightings catalogued in the historical USO literature. Gerb cites it as compelling evidence that credible maritime witnesses were observing anomalous transmedium objects well before the modern UAP reporting era.

## The Fort Salisbury USO Sighting (1902)

In 1902, four crew members aboard the British vessel Fort Salisbury saw a massive cigar-shaped object, approximately 200 meters long, sinking into the ocean in the Gulf of Guinea. No submarine in existence at the time came anywhere close to that size. Submarine technology was still in its earliest stages, and the boats measured only tens of feet in length. An object that large lay well outside any conventional military or commercial explanation available in 1902.

Analyses of historical USO sightings cite the Fort Salisbury encounter as evidence that credible maritime witnesses were seeing transmedium craft of anomalous size well before modern UAP reporting frameworks existed. Gerb singled out the case as one of the more significant entries in the historical record from before Project Blue Book. He noted that, given the gap in size between the object and any known technology of the era, it cannot be dismissed as a misidentified known vessel. Gerb flagged the 200-meter length in particular. The largest warships of 1902 were battleships approximately 130–140 meters long, so the object was bigger than even those, and submarines of the period were measured in tens of feet rather than hundreds of meters.

## Significance in USO Research

The Gulf of Guinea sighting is one of a cluster of historical maritime encounters examined in Gerb's survey of USO cases, which runs from the era of Christopher Columbus through the late 20th century. The [[US Hydrographic Bureau]] documented USO cases during this period, and the [[British Admiralty|British Navy Admiralty]] kept records of anomalous maritime phenomena reported by ship crews in multiple ocean regions. The Fort Salisbury case fits a broader pattern of credible maritime sightings with multiple witnesses. Gerb argues that the prosaic explanations typically applied to these sightings (ball lightning, bioluminescence, or misidentification of known vessels) cannot adequately account for them.

The encounter also predates [[OPNAV 3820]], [[JANAP 146C]] and other reporting suppression mechanisms that limited the documentation of USO cases in the [[Project Blue Book]] era. This suggests that its preservation in maritime records gives an unfiltered account from before institutions suppressed such reports.
