Wright Field was a United States Army Air Forces installation near Dayton, Ohio, It was a major center for aeronautical research, development, and testing from 1917 until 1948, when it merged with Patterson Field to form Wright-Patterson Air Force Base. In the late 1940s it was home to the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center]] (ATIC), which analyzed foreign aircraft and advanced technologies.

According to testimony from [[Robert Sarbacher]] and [[Eric A. Walker]], Wright Field hosted classified meetings circa 1949–1950 at which US government scientists, including [[Vannevar Bush]], [[John Von Neumann]], and [[J. Robert Oppenheimer]], discussed recovered UAP craft, materials, and occupants. The meetings are known collectively as the [[Wright Field Crash Retrieval Meeting]].

## Historical Background

Wright Field was established in 1917 as Wilbur Wright Field, named after aviation pioneer Wilbur Wright. By World War II it was a major hub for military aviation research and development. It housed the Air Materiel Command and was the Army Air Forces' primary facility for testing and evaluating captured enemy aircraft and advanced technologies.

In 1948 Wright Field was combined with nearby Patterson Field to form Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, which is still one of the largest and most important installations in the US Air Force.

## The 1949–1950 Crash Retrieval Meetings

[[Robert Sarbacher]], a physicist and consultant to the DoD's [[Research and Development Board]], stated in his 1983 letter to researcher [[William Steinman]] and in his recorded conversation with [[Stanton Friedman]] that meetings concerning recovered UAP craft and materials took place at Wright Field around 1949–1950. Sarbacher did not attend them himself, but he was invited to take part in discussions about crash retrievals at the R&D Board. He named [[Vannevar Bush]], [[John Von Neumann]], and [[J. Robert Oppenheimer]] as definitively involved.

In his conversation with Friedman, Sarbacher described a meeting at Wright Field about a crash that had occurred "out west," likely referring to the 1947 Roswell incident or other contemporaneous New Mexico recoveries. He mentioned an attendee from Philadelphia who "acted very smug" about his involvement. That attendee was later identified as [[Eric A. Walker]], Executive Secretary of the Defense Research Board from 1950 to 1951.

In interviews with [[William Steinman]] between 1987 and 1990, [[Eric A. Walker]] independently confirmed that he attended the Wright Field meeting described by Sarbacher, and that it concerned crash retrievals and body recovery efforts.

## Connection to ATIC and Foreign Technology Division

Wright Field housed the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center]] (ATIC), which was responsible for analyzing foreign aircraft and technologies. Its work included assessing the capabilities of Soviet jet fighters, captured German aircraft, and other advanced systems. Because of this mission, Wright Field was a logical place for classified briefings on recovered UAP technology: it already had the infrastructure, security clearances, and technical expertise needed to analyze new kinds of aerospace systems.

ATIC later became the Foreign Technology Division, which kept operating from Wright-Patterson Air Force Base. It stayed involved in UAP intelligence analysis by overseeing the Air Force's official UFO investigation programs, including [[Project Sign]], [[Project Grudge]], and [[Project Blue Book]].

## Wright Field vs. Wright-Patterson Air Force Base

The name Wright Field belongs to the period before 1948, and Wright-Patterson Air Force Base to the period after 1948. The alleged crash retrieval meetings Sarbacher described took place around 1949–1950, just after the merger. Sarbacher still consistently called the location "Wright Field," which suggests the meetings were held in facilities or organizational units that kept the Wright Field identity after the formal merger.

Wright-Patterson Air Force Base has remained central to UAP research and speculation. Numerous witnesses allege that recovered craft, materials, and biologics have been stored in secure facilities on the base, most famously in the rumored "Blue Room" or Hangar 18.

## Significance

As the alleged site of the 1949–1950 crash retrieval meetings, Wright Field sits at the origin point of the US government's organized efforts to understand and reverse-engineer recovered non-human technology. The nation's top scientists, people who had built the atomic bomb and shaped postwar defense policy, gathered there, which shows how seriously the US government treated the UAP question during the early Cold War.

Multiple independent sources (Sarbacher, Walker, and [[Wilbert B. Smith]]'s contemporaneous notes) name Wright Field as a hub for UAP discussions. That lends the claim substantial credibility, even though no documentation has been publicly released.
