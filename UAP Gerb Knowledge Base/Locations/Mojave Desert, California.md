The Mojave Desert is a large arid region in southeastern California that also takes in portions of Nevada, Utah, and Arizona. In this wiki it matters as the place where oilman [[Silas Newton]] did magnetic research in 1949 and talked with a scientist he described as the top magnetic expert in the United States. That conversation became an important link in Newton's account of the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]].

## Newton's 1949 Magnetic Research

According to accounts examined in [[Videos/The 1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval/summary|The 1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]], [[Silas Newton]] was doing magnetic research in the Mojave Desert in 1949 when he met a scientist he would later call "Dr. G" and identify as the foremost U.S. authority on magnetics. Gerb and researcher William Steinman believe this scientist was [[Leo GeBauer]], a physical scientist based in Phoenix who co-invented the "doodlebug" oil-detecting device and specialized in magnetic anomaly detection.

In Newton's account, he first learned from this scientist, during or around this period, of a flying saucer that had landed 16 miles east of [[Aztec, New Mexico]] and that Air Force personnel secured within hours of its discovery on March 25, 1948. Newton consistently maintained that he did not originate the Aztec story and was repeating information Dr. G had told him. A wire recording dated March 3, 1950 is believed to document Newton recounting this story, and Gerb treats it as direct evidence that Newton relayed the crash account and did not originate it.

## Significance to the Aztec Case

The Mojave Desert meeting brings together Newton's oil industry work, his interest in magnetic anomaly detection, and the classified scientific community that Gerb argues had actual knowledge of recovered non-human technology. The doodlebug device operated on magnetic detection principles, and it was central to the fraud charges that eventually destroyed Newton's credibility. Newton's correspondence with Wilbert B. Smith's work on flying saucer magnetic propulsion suggests that his interest in the connection between magnetics and UAP was substantive and not merely commercial.

The Mojave Desert region is also close to several classified aerospace facilities in the Antelope Valley and the wider Southern California military test corridor, including [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale and the [[Edwards Air Force Base]] complex. Gerb identifies this regional cluster as significant to understanding how early UAP retrieval and reverse engineering activities were coordinated in the American Southwest during the late 1940s and 1950s.
