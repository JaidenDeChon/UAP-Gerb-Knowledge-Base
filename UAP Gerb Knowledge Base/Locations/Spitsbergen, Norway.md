Spitsbergen is the largest island of the Svalbard archipelago in the High Arctic and is administered by Norway. In the UAP context, it is the alleged site of a 1952 crash of a 47-meter metallic disc that drew CIA interest. It is also considered a possible geographic match for a set of snowy, high-latitude photographs associated with Project Blue Book crash retrieval records.

## The 1952 Alleged Crash

In 1952, reports circulated of a large disc-shaped craft coming down on or near Spitsbergen. They spread mainly through the European press and were later documented in UFO research literature. The object was described as metallic and about 47 meters in diameter. The incident drew interest from the CIA's technical intelligence infrastructure and was examined alongside other crash retrieval cases of the early 1950s.

Spitsbergen is remote, has an Arctic climate and lies entirely above the 74th parallel North, so any recovery effort there would have had significant operational security. Norwegian authorities have historical sovereignty over Svalbard under the 1920 Svalbard Treaty. The treaty also grants other signatory nations certain rights on the archipelago, and that legal complexity could complicate a unilateral US military recovery operation.

## Connection to Blue Book Photographs

Researchers have identified Spitsbergen as a possible location for a set of snowy, high-latitude photographs that appear in Project Blue Book–era documents and purport to show a downed craft. The stark, snow-covered landscape in the photographs is consistent with Svalbard's terrain. If authenticated, the images would be the earliest photographic evidence of a crash retrieval program.
