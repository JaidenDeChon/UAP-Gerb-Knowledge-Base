The Nazca Desert (Spanish: *Desierto de Nazca*) is a high arid plateau in southern Peru, part of the larger Sechura Desert. It is best known as the location of the [[Nazca Lines]] — vast geoglyphs created by removing the reddish iron-oxide pebbles covering the desert floor to expose lighter earth beneath, producing large patterns and figures visible only from elevated vantage points or from the air.

## The Nazca Lines

The geoglyphs at Nazca were created over centuries, with most dated to between 500 BC and 500 AD, by the Nazca culture. Designs include animals (hummingbirds, monkeys, spiders), plants, geometric shapes, and long straight lines extending for miles. The lines were made by making shallow depressions in the desert floor, exposing the paler subsoil. The designs have endured for two millennia due to the extreme aridity of the region and minimal wind erosion.

## Archaeological Interpretation

Archaeologists and anthropologists broadly agree the lines served ceremonial and religious purposes, possibly as offerings to deities observable from mountains or as pathways for ritual processions. Many of the figures are visible from nearby hills. The civilization's lack of a written language makes definitive interpretation difficult.

## Erich von Däniken and the Ancient Astronaut Hypothesis

[[Erich von Däniken]], in his 1968 book *Chariots of the Gods?*, proposed that the Nazca Lines were extraterrestrial landing strips or navigational markers created by aliens or ancient humans under extraterrestrial instruction. He cited the resemblance of the linear patterns to modern airport runways and the difficulty of creating large-scale designs without an aerial view.

Archaeologists reject this interpretation, noting that the designs are observable from nearby elevated terrain, that the lines would not function as practical runways (the desert surface is soft), and that the construction techniques are well understood and consistent with the capabilities of ancient Andean cultures.
