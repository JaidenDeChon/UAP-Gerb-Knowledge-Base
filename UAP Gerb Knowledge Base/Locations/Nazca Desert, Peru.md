The Nazca Desert (Spanish: *Desierto de Nazca*) is a high arid plateau in southern Peru, part of the larger Sechura Desert. It is best known as the site of the [[Nazca Lines]], vast geoglyphs made by removing the reddish iron-oxide pebbles that cover the desert floor to expose the lighter earth beneath. The resulting patterns and figures are visible only from elevated vantage points or from the air.

## The Nazca Lines

The Nazca culture created the geoglyphs over centuries, and most are dated to between 500 BC and 500 AD. The designs include animals (hummingbirds, monkeys, spiders), plants, geometric shapes and long straight lines that run for miles. Each line is a shallow depression in the desert floor that exposes the paler subsoil. The designs have lasted for two millennia because the region is extremely dry and has little wind erosion.

## Archaeological Interpretation

Archaeologists and anthropologists broadly agree that the lines had ceremonial and religious purposes. They may have been offerings to deities observable from mountains, or pathways for ritual processions. Many of the figures can be seen from nearby hills. Because the Nazca civilization had no written language, a definitive interpretation is difficult.

## Erich von Däniken and the Ancient Astronaut Hypothesis

In his 1968 book *Chariots of the Gods?*, [[Erich von Däniken]] proposed that the Nazca Lines were landing strips or navigational markers for extraterrestrials, made either by aliens or by ancient humans following extraterrestrial instruction. He pointed to how much the linear patterns resemble modern airport runways, and to how hard it would be to lay out designs this large without an aerial view.

Archaeologists reject this interpretation. They note that the designs can be seen from nearby high ground, and that the lines would not work as practical runways because the desert surface is soft. They also point out that the construction techniques are well understood and consistent with what ancient Andean cultures were capable of.
