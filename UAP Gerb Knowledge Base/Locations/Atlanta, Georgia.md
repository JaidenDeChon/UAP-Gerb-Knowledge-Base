Atlanta, Georgia is documented in the [[Denb Report]] as the final domestic destination of the **16-foot 5-inch silver disc** recovered by a CIA covert recovery team following the [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval|1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash]]. The transport of the Coyame disc to Atlanta represents one of the few alleged crash retrieval cases in which a specific continental US civilian city — rather than a military installation — is named as the object's post-recovery destination.

## The Coyame Recovery and Transport to Atlanta

In late August 1974, according to the Denb Report, a CIA-coordinated recovery team staged at [[Fort Bliss]], Texas covertly entered Mexican airspace, retrieved a silver disc from a halted Mexican military convoy (all convoy personnel found dead), sanitized the site with high explosives, and carried the object back to US territory slung beneath a Sea Stallion helicopter. The helicopters waited overnight in the Davis Mountains, approximately 25 miles northeast of [[Valentine, Texas]], then met a small convoy on the road near Van Horn and Kent, Texas, where the disc was transferred to a truck that could be sealed completely. The convoy then drove non-stop by back roads to Atlanta, Georgia.

The Denb Report — an anomalous document first published on electronic bulletin boards in 1992 and independently corroborated by researcher [[Leonard Stringfield]] as "authoritatively written using correct military terminology" — provides the Atlanta destination without explanation of which specific facility received the disc. UAP Gerb notes that [[Lockheed Martin]] operates a complex at Air Force Plant 6 in Marietta, less than 30 miles outside Atlanta, where Lockheed says it performs [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works|Skunk Works]] advanced development; the Lockheed company did C-130 work there from the 1950s. He also points to the Lockheed-run Georgia Nuclear Aircraft Laboratory (Air Force Plant 67), which studied the feasibility of nuclear-powered aircraft until it closed in 1971 and was acquired by the city of Atlanta. Unconfirmed reports say the disc later went on to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] or another unnamed base.

## Implications

[[Ryan S. Wood]], author of *Magic Eyes Only*, rates the Coyame case as medium to high authenticity based on the operational detail in the Denb Report. The use of a civilian city as the stated destination — rather than a named military base like [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] — is consistent with the alleged [[CIA Office of Global Access|CIA OGA]] model of moving recovered UAP materials "fairly quickly into private hands" after military retrieval, as described by journalist [[Christopher Sharp]]'s sources regarding OGA operations.
