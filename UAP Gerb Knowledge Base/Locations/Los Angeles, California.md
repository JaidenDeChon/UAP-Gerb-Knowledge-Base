Los Angeles is the largest city in California and the second-most populous city in the United States. In UAP research and in this wiki, it appears in two contexts. It was the site of the 1942 [[Battle of Los Angeles]], one of the earliest wartime UAP-adjacent incidents on U.S. soil, and it is a secondary location in the story of [[Silas Newton]]'s 1953 fraud prosecution, which is connected to the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]].

## The Battle of Los Angeles (1942)

On the night of February 24–25, 1942, in the anxious months after the Attack on Pearl Harbor, anti-aircraft artillery batteries across Los Angeles opened fire after unidentified radar signatures were detected over the city. The barrage lasted several hours and killed five civilians: three in car accidents and two from heart attacks caused by the alarm. U.S. military officials later attributed the incident to either war nerves or a drifting meteorological balloon.

A photograph published in the *Los Angeles Times* on February 26, 1942 appeared to show searchlight beams converging on a saucer-shaped object in the sky, though the image is widely believed to have been heavily retouched for print. Gerb covered the Battle of Los Angeles in [[Videos/The Alien and UFO Obscure Oddities Iceberg (Level 1)/summary|The Alien and UFO Obscure Oddities Iceberg (Level 1)]]. He describes it as an early personal introduction to UAP cases, but he is skeptical that the incident reflects genuine anomalous aerial phenomena and says the available evidence likely has a prosaic explanation. Even so, the case is historically significant as a documented instance of the U.S. military responding to unidentified aerial signatures above a major population center.

## Newton's Arrest and the Aztec Case

Los Angeles also appears in the documentary trail of the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]] case. Oilman [[Silas Newton]] was arrested there in October 1952, and alleged fraud victim [[Herman Flader]] deposited checks there in transactions connected to the doodlebug oil-detecting device. Newton and his associate [[Leo GeBauer]] were prosecuted for fraud after a campaign by *San Francisco Chronicle* journalist [[J. P. Cahn]], whose 1952 exposé discredited Newton and GeBauer and in effect labeled the Aztec story a hoax.

In [[Videos/The 1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval/summary|The 1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]], Gerb argues that Newton's prosecution was not a straightforward fraud case and may have been targeted retaliation for Newton's role in spreading information about the Aztec crash. The arrest in Los Angeles is one part of a broader pattern of federal pursuit that Gerb examines critically. He notes, for instance, that the FBI could not find a single federal judge in Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, or Utah willing to entertain a federal criminal case, and that 32 of Newton's 33 investors remained satisfied with their investments. Flader was the sole complainant.
