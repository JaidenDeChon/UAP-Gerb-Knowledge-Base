Nitinol is a near-equiatomic alloy of nickel and titanium. Dr. William J. Buehler first formally documented it in the public scientific record in 1961, at the U.S. Naval Ordnance Laboratory. Its commercial importance rests on two extraordinary material properties. The first is superelasticity: the alloy can sustain extreme mechanical deformation and return to its original shape once the stress is released, with no permanent deformation. The second is the shape memory effect: after cold deformation, it recovers a pre-set shape when heated. Nitinol is also elastocaloric, meaning it heats up when mechanically stressed and super-cools when the stress is removed, exchanging heat with the environment. These properties make nitinol exceptionally difficult to manufacture. Production requires vacuum arc remelting or vacuum induction remelting, and because titanium is highly reactive, the composition has to be controlled extremely tightly.

In UAP research, nitinol is central to claims that [[Battelle Memorial Institute]] analyzed material recovered from the [[Roswell Crash|1947 Roswell crash]] more than a decade before the alloy's official scientific discovery.

## Roswell Connection

Intelligence officer [[Jesse Marcel]] inspected the Roswell crash debris field with rancher [[Mac Brazel]] in July 1947. Among the recovered material, he described an exceedingly light metallic foil that could not be permanently deformed: it returned to its original shape even after being crumpled or bent. This description is functionally identical to nitinol's superelastic properties. Marcel later stated that General Roger Ramey ordered him to pose with substitute weather balloon debris for press photographs, while the actual material was transported to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]].

In 1949, twelve years before nitinol's official discovery, Wright-Patterson Air Force Base contracted [[Battelle Memorial Institute]] to perform material analysis on shape-memory titanium alloys. The work ran under contract number 33-38-3736, from September 1 through October 21, 1949. It was classified under the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]'s "restricted" standard, a private-sector classification tier that even holders of Special Access Program clearance could not access. The 1949 Battelle research was not declassified until 2010.

[[EJ Center]], a senior chemical engineer at Battelle, co-authored the nitinol analysis subsection of the 1949 reports. He later disclosed privately that he had studied "Parts retrieved from a flying saucer" during his time at Battelle. He made this disclosure around 1957–1958. The [[MUFON|Mutual UFO Network]] documented it in 1992, and Dr. [[Irena Scott]] published it in 1994, more than fifteen years before the underlying contract documents were declassified.

## Significance to UAP Material Research

UAP researchers point to a sequence of events: the Roswell crash in July 1947, the transport of the material to Wright-Patterson, Wright-Patterson's contract with Battelle for nitinol analysis in 1949, and the official "discovery" of nitinol in 1961. They cite it as evidence that nitinol was first identified by analyzing crash-retrieved non-human material, and not through indigenous metallurgical research.

Battelle's 1955 Special Report 14, the comprehensive ATIC analysis of 3,200 UFO sightings, concluded that no physical matter had ever been recovered from any UFO sighting. UAP researchers argue that this conclusion was a deliberate falsehood, given that Battelle's own 1949 contract was almost certainly performed on recovered material.

The nitinol case also shows how the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]'s "restricted" classification standard let private contractors hold classified UAP-related research outside the reach of congressional oversight and standard SAP clearance frameworks. [[David Grusch]] cited this feature of the system in explaining how crash retrieval programs have been concealed.

## Shape Memory Alloy

Nitinol is the most widely known shape memory alloy. Shape memory alloys are metals that return to a pre-programmed form in response to a change in temperature or a mechanical release, and the class as a whole has been associated with multiple UAP crash retrieval accounts. Jesse Marcel's description of the [[Roswell Crash|1947 Roswell crash]] is among the earliest military witness accounts of material with these properties.
