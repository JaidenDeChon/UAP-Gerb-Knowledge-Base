Panspermia is the hypothesis that life exists throughout the universe in microbial or cellular form and is carried between worlds by space dust, meteoroids, asteroids, comets and planetoids. If it is true, life on Earth need not have begun on Earth: it could have arrived, already formed, from somewhere else. The idea is old (the word goes back to the Greek philosopher Anaxagoras), was revived in modern form by the Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius in the early twentieth century, and remains a speculative but scientifically framed alternative to a purely terrestrial origin of life.

## Types

Three variants are usually distinguished:

- **Lithopanspermia**: life carried inside rock from one solar system to another.
- **Ballistic panspermia**: life carried from one planet to another within the same solar system, typically in material blasted off one body by an impact and later falling on another.
- **Directed panspermia**: the deliberate seeding of life on other worlds by a civilization. The idea was put forward in 1973 by Francis Crick and Leslie Orgel; Gerb gives the example of humans one day sending life to other planets.

## Mars as a possible source

The example Gerb uses in the first episode of his iceberg series is Mars. If early Mars carried microbial or multicellular life, a large asteroid or meteor impact could have thrown Martian soil and dust, microbes included, into space, and some of that material could have reached Earth, carrying "the seeds of life" from one planet to the other. That is ballistic panspermia.

## Relevance to the UAP topic

Panspermia is not itself a claim about visitors or craft. It appears in UFO discussions because it bears on how common life, and therefore intelligent life, might be in the universe, and because directed panspermia allows for an intelligence that deliberately started life elsewhere. It sits alongside the [[Kardashev Scale]], the [[Fermi Paradox]] and the [[Great Filter]] among the scientific frameworks the UFO iceberg's upper layer introduces.
