The **Wilson-Davis Memo** is a 15-page document that surfaced in 2018 from the estate of Apollo 14 astronaut [[Edgar Mitchell]]. It purports to record notes from an October 16, 2002 meeting between astrophysicist Dr. [[Eric Davis]] and Vice Admiral [[Thomas Wilson]], former Deputy Director and Director of the Defense Intelligence Agency. The memo gives an account of Wilson's alleged 1997 discovery of a hidden crash retrieval and reverse engineering program inside an aerospace contractor. As one of the most senior intelligence officials in the U.S. military, he tried to gain access to it, and program managers and Pentagon officials then stonewalled him. If authentic, the memo would be smoking-gun evidence supporting decades of claims that the United States government is actively trying to reverse engineer craft of non-human origin.

## Provenance and Public Release

The memo came to public attention through [[James Rigny]], who obtained it while helping disperse Edgar Mitchell's estate in 2016. Around 2013, Rigny had formed a close relationship with a confidential source who had relationships with many Apollo astronauts, including Mitchell. When Mitchell died in February 2016, his family invited Rigny's source to help disperse the estate, much of which was marked for destruction.

Once he had access to the materials, Rigny copied numerous documents, including the Wilson-Davis memo. His name became public when he appeared on [[Richard Dolan]]'s podcast to discuss where the memo came from. The document later became one of the most significant pieces of evidence in the UAP disclosure debate, and in 2022 it was entered into the official U.S. Congressional Record.

Edgar Mitchell, the sixth man to walk on the moon, had long taken a public interest in UFOs. He began speaking publicly about his beliefs in 1973, two years after his moon landing, when he claimed: "I happen to have been privileged enough to be in on the fact that we've been visited on this planet and the UFO phenomenon is real."

## The 1997 Pentagon Briefing and Wilson's Investigation

The events in the memo began in April 1997, when [[Steven Greer]] briefed Vice Admiral Wilson, retired Navy Commander [[Will Miller]], Edgar Mitchell and DIA Director [[Patrick M. Hughes]] in a Pentagon conference room about UFOs, Roswell and crashed UFOs. Afterward, Miller and Wilson spoke privately, and Wilson came away wanting to investigate further.

From April to June 1997, Wilson carried out a 45-day investigation. General [[Marshal Ward]] and Secretary of Defense [[Bill Perry]] advised him to examine records in [[OUSD(AT) (Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Technology)|OUSD(AT)]], the Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Technology. Both Ward and Perry told Wilson about a "special project records group not belonging to usual special access programs". This was a special subset of unacknowledged carve-outs and waived programs that Perry himself had organized in 1994.

Wilson found the unusual records group in the index abstracts and called seven program managers. Four of them referred him to the same program, run by three people who called themselves the "[[Watch Committee]]": a security director (former NSA), a program director and a corporate attorney. An aerospace technology contractor active in defense and intelligence, described as "the best one of them," coordinated the program. Based on later whistleblower accounts, the contractor was likely [[Lockheed Martin]].

## Wilson's Meeting with the Watch Committee

At the end of May 1997, Wilson demanded a formal briefing and flew out to meet the watch committee in their conference room, inside a secure vault. The committee told Wilson it had formed after a near-disaster, when a past audit investigation almost exposed the program. After that incident, a formal agreement was struck with [[SAPOC (Special Access Program Oversight Committee)|SAPOC]] to prevent future discovery, and special criteria were set up to control all access.

The committee showed Wilson pages of a [[Bigot List]] dated 1990 to 1993. Every name on it was a civilian (scientists, technicians, engineers and managers), and it included no politicians, White House officials, presidents or congressional members. The committee told Wilson the program was a reverse engineering effort focused on recovered technological hardware.

According to the memo, the program manager stated they had "a craft, an intact craft they believed could fly" that was "not of this Earth, not made by man, not by human hands." The program had spent years trying to understand and exploit the technology, with agonizingly slow progress and little success, partly because it could not collaborate with the wider scientific community. Only 400 to 800 workers in total had been involved since the program began.

## Denial of Access and Career Threats

As Deputy Director of the DIA, Wilson had statutory oversight and regulatory authority over all DoD special access programs. The watch committee still denied him access, because he was not on the bigot list and did not meet their undisclosed special criteria.

Wilson complained to the SAPOC senior review group (SRG) at the Pentagon, but the SRG upheld the contractor's access denial and ordered Wilson to drop the matter. SRG chairman [[John Deutsch]] (former CIA Director) threatened Wilson that if he did not comply, "he would not see the Director of DIA promotion, he would get an early retirement, and lose one to two stars."

Wilson apparently heeded the threat, and he later served as Director of the DIA from 1999 to 2002. In January 1998, he spoke to [[Jacques Gansler]] (Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Technology), who had been read into the program. Gansler told Wilson: "UFOs are real, so-called alien abductions not real," and refused to discuss it further.

## The October 16, 2002 Meeting

On October 16, 2002, after Wilson had retired from the DIA, he met with physicist Eric Davis in a car outside defense contractor EG&G. [[Oak Shannon]], former manager of special projects at [[Los Alamos National Laboratories]], arranged the meeting. Davis had been investigating crash retrieval programs and wanted to hear Wilson's account firsthand.

Wilson recounted the entire 1997 investigation and how he was then denied access. He stressed how sensitive the information was, telling Davis that if Davis ever violated his trust, Wilson would "deny the meeting, deny everything said in the meeting, and would never seek to know more or talk more on this topic without clearance." Wilson called the program "absurdly close held subject matter" and said he'd "never seen anything like this program in the black programs community."

At the end of the meeting, Davis promised to keep it private, for his personal use only.

## Confirmations and Denials

Admiral Wilson flatly denied the memo in a 2020 interview with Billy Cox: "It's all fiction. I wouldn't know Eric Davis if he walked in right now." He did admit to meeting with Greer and Mitchell, but claimed he didn't follow up with any investigation. Researchers point out that in the memo itself, Wilson said he would deny the meeting if his trust was broken.

Eric Davis refused to comment directly on whether the meeting occurred, citing security clearances. In a deleted segment of The Basement Office, however, he confirmed: "They were leaked out of Ed Mitchell's estate." He said he could not discuss any aspect of the notes because of DoJ policies on prosecuting security clearance violations for discussing classified information leaked into the public.

Edgar Mitchell confirmed the 1997 meeting on CNN's Larry King Live in 2008, before his death. He described how Wilson discovered black budget funding for a UFO program and was then denied access: "He was told 'I'm sorry Admiral, you do not have need to know here, and so goodbye.'"

Will Miller confirmed the 1997 Pentagon meeting when interviewed by [[Joe Murgia]]. He also expressed his "firm belief" that the U.S. possesses "entire operational craft" including "transluminal velocity craft that can take us anywhere in space and time."

Oak Shannon confirmed the memo's authenticity in a Jay Anderson Project Unity interview.

Lou Elizondo said: "That Wilson memo, let me tell you something, that thing isn't going to die. That thing is now out for the public and that is going to start a firestorm."

Chris Mellon confirmed the memo's authenticity in a website post. He wrote that the memo "provided specific information lending credence to sensational reports that an official US government program is actively seeking to exploit recovered technology that was fashioned by some other species or perhaps advanced AI machines."

## UAP Gerb's Assessment of Scope

In the "80 Years of UFO Crash Retrieval and Reverse Engineering" timeline video, Gerb offers a specific theory about the scope of what Wilson actually encountered. In this assessment, Wilson came "toe-to-toe" with one specific legacy program activity rather than the entire UFO legacy program portfolio. That activity likely fell under the cognizant authority of the modern-day Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment (USD A&S), and probably involved the Air Force and an unnamed aerospace contractor, speculated to be [[TRW]].

The assessment rests on the memo's own wording. The Watch Committee allegedly told Wilson the activity involved "something recovered years ago in the past" with "technological hardware recovered". Gerb notes that this phrasing is singular, so it does not fit [[David Grusch]]'s later claim of 25 to 100 recovered craft.

The video also repeats several points drawn from the memo. The programs existed in a special subset of carve-out waived unacknowledged SAPs, and the "Watch Committee" and the SAPOC Senior Review Group shielded them from access by insiders. At the time, the Bigot List had no White House, presidential or Congressional names, consistent with the 1994 Perry-era reorganization.

## Significance to UAP Disclosure

The Wilson-Davis Memo is frequently cited as evidence of:
- The existence of crash retrieval programs managed in the private sector outside traditional military oversight
- Institutional gatekeeping mechanisms that prevent even senior intelligence officials from accessing UAP-related compartmented programs
- The transfer of recovered non-human technology to defense contractors for reverse engineering
- The nested compartmentalization structure described by whistleblower [[David Grusch]] in 2023
- The existence of intact recovered craft that defy conventional explanations

The memo's entry into the U.S. Congressional Record in 2022 gave it added institutional weight in UAP discourse.
