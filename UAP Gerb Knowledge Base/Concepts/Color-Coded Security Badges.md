Color-coded security badges are a compartmentalized system of physical access control: the color of a person's badge determines which sections of a secured facility or object they may enter or approach. The idea comes up in the account of "[[RB]]", an anonymous Marine whistleblower, who describes the security around a recovered disc-shaped craft at an undisclosed military base in December 1963.

## RB's Account

According to RB's account, the building that held the craft used a strict badge system in which each color allowed access to designated sections of the craft. A red badge gave total access to the entire vehicle. Engineers and scientists could work only on the portions of the craft assigned to them. This put compartments inside an already compartmentalized facility, a layered arrangement meant to keep any single person from understanding all of the craft's systems.

The badges worked alongside a physical perimeter, a circle of white tape around the craft that no unauthorized person could cross, including senior military officers and the Secretary of the Navy. Together, these measures suggest several layers of access control, consistent with the protocols of the most sensitive Special Access Programs.

## Broader Significance

Color-coded compartments inside UAP-related facilities resemble the "bigoted" access lists and compartmented programs used more widely in the U.S. intelligence community, where even high-ranking officials may be refused access to a program for which they have no "need to know." A parallel example at the institutional level is [[Barry Goldwater]], who was denied access to the [[Blue Room]] at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] despite his senior rank and clearances.
