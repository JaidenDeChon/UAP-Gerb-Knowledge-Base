Restricted Data is a category of classified information established by the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]] (and its predecessor, the Atomic Energy Act of 1946). It covers nuclear weapons design, production processes, and special nuclear materials. It is the only classification in the United States that applies to information automatically because of its subject matter, with no formal classification review, designation, or stamping, so such information is "born classified." The automatic approach began as an informal wartime practice by [[Leslie Groves]] during the Manhattan Project, when the science of nuclear fission was treated as secret by nature, whatever its formal designation.

## Legal Basis and Distinction from Executive Order Classification

Standard US classified information (confidential, secret, top secret) is governed by presidential executive orders, a line of orders that includes Eisenhower's EO 10501, Reagan's EO 12356, and Obama's EO 13526. A president can change or revoke these classifications by executive order.

Restricted Data, by contrast, is established by statute, the Atomic Energy Act passed by Congress. As a result:

- An executive order cannot declassify Restricted Data. Removing the designation takes specific congressional action or a formal Department of Energy (DOE) determination.
- Restricted Data is not subject to the standard FOIA requests that apply to other federal records.
- The [[Department of Energy]] (and before it, the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]) administers Restricted Data classification, rather than the DoD or the executive branch classification system.
- Restricted Data has its own clearances, separate from DoD clearances: Q clearance (equivalent to top secret) and L clearance (equivalent to secret).

## Related Category: Transclassified Foreign Nuclear Information (TFNI)

Section 142 of the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]] created Transclassified Foreign Nuclear Information (TFNI), a designation for information about nuclear programs that a foreign government originally classified and that was later incorporated into US classified holdings. TFNI is protected under two frameworks at once, the Atomic Energy Act and the executive order system, and is explicitly exempt from declassification.

The Schumer UAP disclosure amendment (UAPDA) explicitly states that UFO legacy programs have misclassified UAP materials, data, files, programs, and discoveries as TFNI. That lets non-nuclear materials and programs benefit from the most restrictive statutory classification framework in the US government.

## "Special Nuclear Material" as a Classification Vehicle

Section 51 of the 1954 Act defines "special nuclear material" as anything that gives off a sizable amount of atomic energy, a definition that is broad whether by intent or in effect. [[David Grusch]] has noted that it is vague enough to cover recovered non-human materials with radiological properties, which allows them to be classified under DOE statutory authority: "They're basically treating this as nuclear secrets because it gives off nuclear radiation... what legal gymnastics are you saying this stuff is a US nuclear secret? You're transclassifying it into a nuclear secret."

## Practical Significance for UAP Disclosure

Because Restricted Data and TFNI are statutory classifications and not executive ones, a presidential executive order to release UFO-related files has no legal force over materials in these categories, whichever president issues it. Executive action alone therefore faces a structural barrier to UAP disclosure. Materials protected under DOE's Restricted Data and TFNI designations sit in a legal framework separate from standard national security information, and releasing them requires either congressional action or a formal DOE declassification determination.

By classifying recovered non-human materials and programs under DOE statutory authority from the late 1940s onward, the program created a legal protection that has held up against every later attempt at executive-branch transparency.
