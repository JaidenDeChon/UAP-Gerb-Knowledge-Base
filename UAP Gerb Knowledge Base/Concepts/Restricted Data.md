Restricted Data is a category of classified information established by the Atomic Energy Act of 1946 and carried into the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]], which defines it today. It covers nuclear weapons design, production processes, and special nuclear materials. It is the only classification in the United States that applies to information automatically because of its subject matter, with no formal classification review, designation, or stamping, so such information is "born classified." The automatic approach began as an informal wartime practice by [[Leslie Groves]] during the Manhattan Project, when the science of nuclear fission was treated as secret by nature, whatever its formal designation.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said the 1954 Act established Restricted Data, with the 1946 Act as its predecessor. The category was created by the 1946 Act; the 1954 Act kept it and defines it in section 11 r.

## Legal Basis and Distinction from Executive Order Classification

Standard US classified information (confidential, secret, top secret) is governed by presidential executive orders, a line of orders that includes Eisenhower's EO 10501, Reagan's EO 12356, and Obama's EO 13526. A president can change or revoke these classifications by executive order.

Restricted Data, by contrast, is established by statute, the Atomic Energy Act passed by Congress. As a result:

- An executive order cannot declassify Restricted Data. Removing the designation takes specific congressional action or a formal Department of Energy (DOE) determination.
- Restricted Data is not subject to the standard FOIA requests that apply to other federal records.
- The [[Department of Energy]] (and before it, the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]) administers Restricted Data classification, rather than the DoD or the executive branch classification system.
- Restricted Data has its own clearances, separate from DoD clearances: Q clearance (equivalent to top secret) and L clearance (equivalent to secret).

## Related Category: Transclassified Foreign Nuclear Information (TFNI)

Gerb counts Transclassified Foreign Nuclear Information (TFNI) among the classification standards that came with the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]]. The Act's basis for it is section 142 e, which has the Commission and the Director of Central Intelligence remove information about the atomic energy programs of other nations from the Restricted Data category so that it can be "adequately safeguarded as defense information". The name TFNI came later, and the Act says nothing about it being exempt from declassification. TFNI is protected under two frameworks at once, the Atomic Energy Act and the executive order system, and the Schumer amendment describes it as "also exempt from mandatory declassification".

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said section 142 "created" TFNI, defined it as information a foreign government had classified, and said it was "explicitly exempt from declassification". Section 142 e only provides for removing other nations' atomic energy information from Restricted Data; the name is later, and the exemption language comes from the Schumer amendment's finding 4, not the Act.

Gerb reads the Schumer UAP disclosure amendment (UAPDA) as saying that UFO legacy programs hid UAP materials, data, files, programs and discoveries as TFNI. The amendment's own finding 4 is narrower: it says UAP records have not been declassified "due in part to exemptions under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954" and "an over-broad interpretation" of TFNI. In Gerb's view, that lets non-nuclear materials and programs benefit from the most restrictive statutory classification framework in the US government.

## "Special Nuclear Material" as a Classification Vehicle

Section 51 of the 1954 Act lets the Atomic Energy Commission declare further material to be "special nuclear material" if it finds the material "capable of releasing substantial quantities of atomic energy" and the President assents in writing. [[David Grusch]] paraphrases this as "anything that gives off a sizable amount of atomic energy" and has noted that it is vague enough to cover recovered non-human materials with radiological properties, which allows them to be classified under DOE statutory authority: "They're basically treating this as nuclear secrets because it gives off nuclear radiation... what legal gymnastics are you saying this stuff is a US nuclear secret? You're transclassifying it into a nuclear secret."

> [!edit] Attribution clarified 17 October 2026
> This paragraph used to give "anything that gives off a sizable amount of atomic energy" as the Act's definition. That is Grusch's paraphrase; section 51's words are "capable of releasing substantial quantities of atomic energy".

## Practical Significance for UAP Disclosure

Because Restricted Data and TFNI are statutory classifications and not executive ones, a presidential executive order to release UFO-related files has no legal force over materials in these categories, whichever president issues it. Executive action alone therefore faces a structural barrier to UAP disclosure. Materials protected under DOE's Restricted Data and TFNI designations sit in a legal framework separate from standard national security information, and releasing them requires either congressional action or a formal DOE declassification determination.

Gerb identifies this dual-track classification system as a feature of the UFO legacy program's security architecture, whether deliberate or fortuitously useful. By classifying recovered non-human materials and programs under DOE statutory authority from the late 1940s onward, the program created a legal protection that has held up against every later attempt at executive-branch transparency.
