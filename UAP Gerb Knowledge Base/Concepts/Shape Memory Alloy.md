A shape memory alloy (SMA) is a metal alloy capable of "remembering" a pre-set shape and returning to it after it has been deformed. It recovers the shape either when mechanical stress is released (superelasticity) or when heat triggers the change (the shape memory effect). The most commercially significant shape memory alloy is [[Nitinol (Nickel-Titanium Alloy)]], a nickel-titanium compound. Dr. William J. Buehler at the Naval Ordnance Laboratory first formally documented its properties in the public scientific record in 1961.

## UAP Crash Retrieval Context

Shape memory alloys come up in several UAP crash retrieval accounts, most prominently the [[Roswell Crash|1947 Roswell Crash]]. Intelligence officer [[Jesse Marcel]] was the first military officer to inspect the Roswell debris field, which he did alongside rancher [[Mac Brazel]]. Among the recovered material, Marcel described a light metallic foil that could not be permanently deformed: after it was crumpled or bent, it returned to its original shape. That description is functionally the same as the superelastic behavior of nitinol and other shape memory alloys.

UAP researchers, Gerb among them, argue that the formal scientific study of this Roswell-derived material was [[Battelle Memorial Institute]]'s 1949 classified contract with [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] to analyze shape-memory titanium alloys. The research was classified under the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]'s restricted standard and was not declassified until 2010. Battelle's analysis predates the official public discovery of nitinol by more than a decade, and it is central to the argument that non-human materials recovered from crashes drove the development of what later became one of the most commercially significant categories of advanced material in modern engineering.
