Ballistic missile early warning systems are networks of sensors, in space and on the ground, that detect and track ballistic missile launches anywhere in the world and warn military commanders and national leaders. The main US system in space is the [[Defense Support Program (DSP)]]. Its infrared sensors, carried on [[Geosynchronous Satellites|geosynchronous satellites]], pick up the heat of a missile launch within seconds of liftoff.

## Defense Support Program

The DSP satellites have been the United States' main ballistic missile early warning satellites since the first one, DSP-1, was launched in 1970. From geosynchronous orbit, about 22,000 miles above Earth, they watch the whole globe continuously for:
- Intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) launches
- Submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) launches
- Intermediate and short-range missile launches
- Nuclear detonations
- Space launches

## Dual Function: Missile Detection and Fast Walker Tracking

The main job of ballistic missile early warning satellites is detecting missiles. Since at least 1972, they have also been detecting and cataloging [[Fast Walkers]], unexplained objects entering or leaving Earth's atmosphere.

[[Richard P. Osedacz]]'s 1989 paper states directly that Fast Walker data "has been recorded since 1972 by **certain ballistic missile early warning Satellite Systems** located at various geostationary locations." [[Bradley R. Townsend]]'s 2008 study also referred to "**ballistic missile warning satellites**" when discussing Fast Walker detections.

This second use suggests that infrared sensors built to detect missiles are sensitive enough to track much smaller, faster objects that don't follow ballistic paths.

## Technical Explanation for Fast Walker Detection

Ballistic missile early warning systems detect:
1. Heat: the intense infrared glow of rocket plumes
2. Reflected light: sunlight bouncing off objects that pass through the sensor's field of view
3. Rapid movement: objects moving faster than any atmospheric phenomenon would

The sensors that track missile launches can also detect Fast Walkers, because both give off heat or light the sensors can see and both move at extreme speeds. Fast Walkers often change course, though, which a ballistic object cannot do, and this sets them apart from missiles and space debris.

## Data Integration with NORAD

Data from ballistic missile early warning satellites goes straight to [[NORAD]] (North American Aerospace Defense Command), which analyzes it in real time and assesses threats. NORAD therefore gets missile warnings and Fast Walker detections through the same feed, which explains why [[J. Allen Hynek]] stated in 1967 that "all continental US military UFO cases in [[Project Blue Book]] files are designated as NORAD cases."

## Classification of Fast Walker Data

The existence and general capabilities of ballistic missile early warning systems are publicly acknowledged, but the detection records themselves, Fast Walker detections in particular, remain highly classified. [[John Greenewald]]'s FOIA requests were denied again and again under Executive Order 13526, which indicates that Fast Walker data is treated as some of the most sensitive information these systems collect.

## Significance

Because ballistic missile early warning satellites also track Fast Walkers, UAP detection is built into the most critical national security systems. The objects are monitored and recorded continuously, and the data stays classified through TS/SCI compartmentation.

Systems built to detect missile threats also routinely detect objects from deep space that appear to fly under control. What Fast Walkers are and where they come from remain open questions, and classification keeps them unanswered.
