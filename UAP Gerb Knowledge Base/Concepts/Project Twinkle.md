Project Twinkle was a US Air Force program that ran from 1949 to 1951. Its task was to observe and document, by scientific means, the "green fireball" phenomenon and other unidentified aerial objects appearing over sensitive military installations in New Mexico, particularly near atomic weapons facilities. It was run together with [[Project Grudge]], the Air Force's official UFO investigation at the time, and it tried to determine whether the phenomena were natural atmospheric events, foreign surveillance technology, or something else entirely.

## Background and Establishment

Beginning in late 1948, military and scientific personnel in New Mexico began reporting unusual green fireballs and other anomalous aerial phenomena, particularly around [[Los Alamos National Laboratory]], [[Sandia National Laboratories]] and [[White Sands Missile Range]]. The objects behaved differently from meteors. They appeared at low altitude, traveled horizontally and followed controlled flight paths, and they were often seen during or near weapons tests and atomic research.

The Air Force was concerned that the phenomena might be Soviet surveillance or reconnaissance technology. It set up Project Twinkle to deploy observation equipment, including cameras, spectrographs and triangulation stations, and to capture scientific data about the objects.

## Operations and Observations

Project Twinkle's observation network was centered in New Mexico. On August 31, 1950, the program documented an incident in which the UFO phenomenon was seen following a V-2 rocket launch from [[White Sands Missile Range]]. This fit other reports from the period, including those of radar operator [[Lloyd Eugene Camp]], who saw silver disc-shaped objects circling V-2 rockets on several occasions.

It was hard to predict when and where the phenomena would appear, which limited what the program could find. Even with instrumented observation posts in place, Project Twinkle struggled to capture definitive data, because the green fireballs and UAP often appeared without warning and vanished quickly.

## Connection to Atomic Facilities

Because so many green fireball sightings clustered near Los Alamos, Sandia and other atomic research sites, some speculated that the phenomena were intelligently controlled and specifically interested in US nuclear weapons development. This pattern was later cited as early evidence of the now well-documented connection between UAP and nuclear facilities, a theme that still appears in modern UAP research and testimony.

## Findings and Closure

Project Twinkle was officially closed in late 1951. Its final report concluded that the green fireball phenomenon remained unexplained, with no definitive identification as natural meteors, atmospheric plasma or foreign technology. Without conclusive scientific evidence, the question stayed unresolved, and green fireball sightings continued now and then after the project ended.

## Relationship to Other Programs

Project Twinkle ran alongside [[Project Grudge]] and came before [[Project Blue Book]]. It set up instruments at observation sites and tried to collect rigorous scientific data, whereas Grudge and Blue Book ran broader investigations that relied mainly on witness testimony and analysis after the fact. The program was an early attempt by the US military to apply scientific method to UFO phenomena, though it had limited success.

## Significance

Project Twinkle is notable for several reasons:

1. It documents official US Air Force concern about UAP activity near the nation's most sensitive atomic facilities during the early Cold War.
2. It corroborates witness accounts, such as those of [[Lloyd Eugene Camp]], that UAP were seen near ballistic missile tests and weapons development work.
3. It was an early scientific effort to study UAP with instruments, and it predates wider public awareness of the phenomenon.
4. Its findings, or lack of them, show how hard it was for the military to study systematically a phenomenon that appeared unpredictably and showed characteristics beyond known technology.

Project Twinkle's documentation of UAP activity at [[White Sands Missile Range]] during V-2 rocket launches gives historical context to an account from [[Robert Sarbacher]]'s son. According to the son, Dr. Sarbacher was tasked with developing camera-equipped missiles to track and photograph UAP, because conventional aircraft were too slow to pursue them.
