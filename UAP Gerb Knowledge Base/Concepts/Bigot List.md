A **bigot list** is a highly restricted list of the people authorized to be read into a specific [[Special Access Program (SAP)]] or [[Unacknowledged Special Access Program (USAP)]]. The term comes from British operations in World War II during the invasion of German-occupied territory. Today it means the roster of people cleared for access to especially sensitive compartmented programs.

## Origin and Usage

According to retired Navy Commander [[Will Miller]], the term dates to "World War II, British invasion of German-occupied territory." In modern usage, a bigot list is "just a list of SAP/USAP authorization individuals. Lists vary according to the program in question, the types of individuals needed to run the program, and how limited the access needs to be depending on the sensitivity of the program."

Unlike a standard security clearance, a place on a bigot list applies to one program only. Program managers decide who goes on it, using strict and often undisclosed criteria. A person left off the list can be refused access even as a senior official with the right clearance level and [[Need to Know|need-to-know]] authority.

## The Wilson-Davis Memo Context

In the [[Wilson-Davis Memo]], Vice Admiral [[Thomas Wilson]], then Deputy Director of the Defense Intelligence Agency, was allegedly shown pages of a bigot list dated 1990 to 1993 for a crash retrieval and reverse engineering program run by an aerospace contractor. According to the memo:

- All names on the list were civilians: scientists, technicians, engineers, and managers
- No politicians, White House officials, presidents, congressional members, or their staffers appeared on the list
- No senior Bush or Clinton administration officials were listed
- Wilson did recognize the names of people from [[OUSD(AT) (Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Technology)|OUSD(AT)]], the [[National Security Council]], and other Pentagon departments

As Deputy Director of DIA, Wilson had statutory oversight and regulatory authority over all DoD special access programs, both acknowledged and unacknowledged. He was still denied access because he was not on the bigot list. The [[Watch Committee]] that oversaw the program told Wilson that membership of the list was decided by special criteria set after a near-exposure incident. Wilson did not meet those criteria, which were not disclosed.

## "Bigoted" as a SAP Control Marking

UAP Gerb's *Special Access Required Vol.2* cites [[David Grusch]]'s description, from his Judicial Watch interview, of a "bigoted, waived Special Access Program" as one of the most restrictive SAP configurations possible. Such a program is waived, meaning congressional reporting on it is limited, and it also carries the informal control marking "bigoted," meaning access is restricted "by name" through a bigot list. That marking works much like NOFORN (not releasable to foreign nationals), a control marking used on other legacy-related programs.
