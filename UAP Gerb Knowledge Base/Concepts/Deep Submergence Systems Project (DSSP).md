The Deep Submergence Systems Project (DSSP) was a 1964 United States Navy initiative established by [[John P. Craraven]], chief scientist of the Navy Special Projects Office, to dramatically expand the Navy's deep ocean engineering, diving, and recovery capabilities. The DSSP was initiated in direct response to the 1963 loss of the USS Thresher and the classified requirements identified by [[Sand Dollar]], a clandestine Navy program tasked with recovering objects of national security interest from the seafloor. The DSSP produced the Navy's Deep Submergence Rescue Vehicles (DSRVs) and clandestine nuclear submarines used for undersea intelligence collection.

## Origins

The loss of the USS Thresher in 1963 provided the public justification for the DSSP, but [[John P. Craraven]] identified a second, classified driver: the requirements of Project Sand Dollar, which maintained a global inventory of objects on the seafloor warranting recovery. Craraven was tasked in 1964 to determine whether he could "organize and manage a program to carry out this mission using the assets of the DSSP" — and whether he could do so with such secrecy that even his closest associates would have no idea of his involvement.

The DSSP was launched out of the Navy Special Program Office. Its timing coincides closely with — and predates by five years — the founding of the [[National Underwater Reconnaissance Office]] (NURO) in 1969, suggesting that the engineering capability development and the intelligence collection agency were being built in parallel.

## Products and Capabilities

The DSSP produced several deep-submergence platforms:

- **DSRV-1 (Mystic) and DSRV-2 (Avalon)**: The two publicly acknowledged Deep Submergence Rescue Vehicles, built by Lockheed Missiles and Space Company in Sunnyvale, California, and operational from approximately 1970–1972. Officially capable of deploying anywhere in the world within 72 hours (transported by C-5 Galaxy, truck, or mother submarine), hovering in strong currents, and mating with disabled submarines at depths exceeding 5,000 feet. Both operated within the US Navy until 2008. No official rescue missions were ever conducted; no American submarines sank after 1971.
- **NR-1**: A small nuclear-powered deep submergence vessel, launched in 1969 and retired in 2008. Conducted numerous classified missions including search, object recovery, geological survey, and oceanographic research. The sole publicly acknowledged NR-1 mission in 1976 involved recovering an F-14 lost from an aircraft carrier.
- **Proposed DSSVs (Deep Submergence Search Vehicles)**: Six units were proposed in the original DSSP plan, with 20,000-foot operational depth, specifically designed for seafloor exploration and object recovery and capable of clandestine operations. UAP Gerb argues that at least some of these units may have been secretly built, citing the 1991 North Atlantic retrieval case in which a DSRV reportedly operated at nearly 1.5 miles — beyond Mystic and Avalon's acknowledged operational ceiling of approximately one mile.

## Dual-Use Design Philosophy

[[John P. Craraven]] was explicit that the DSRV was not solely a rescue vehicle. He wrote: "A DSRV designed, constructed, and deployed for every conceivable rescue mission would also be available for the intelligence 'mission impossible' that were sure to occur." He also noted that NURO vessels like the USS Halibut and USS Seawolf "only transported top secret equipment and teams of industry specialists who would carry out unknown missions in unknown parts of the sea."

## Relationship to UFO/USO Retrieval

UAP Gerb argues the DSSP represents the Navy's systematic engineering response to the problem of recovering non-human craft from the ocean floor. Key supporting observations include:

- The DSSP was launched two years after the alleged October 1962 Bluegill Triple Prime nuclear test, during which a UFO was reportedly disabled and fell into the Pacific Ocean
- The DSRV's deployment capability and mechanical arm systems are described by Craraven as purpose-built for "classified operations associated with national security that could not be accomplished without a DSRV system"
- The 1991 North Atlantic retrieval described by witness Mark employed a DSRV-type vessel with mechanical rigging arms for object recovery — precisely the capability the DSSP was designed to provide
