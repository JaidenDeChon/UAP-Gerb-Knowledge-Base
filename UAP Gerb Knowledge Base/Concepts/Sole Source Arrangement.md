A sole source arrangement, in the context of alleged UAP legacy programs, is a contractual structure in which a single private defense contractor is granted exclusive access to recovered non-human materials held by the government. The contractor then develops technological insights or derivative systems from those materials and sells the resulting intellectual property or capabilities back to the government for profit. Critics call sole source arrangements deeply unethical because private corporations profit from materials that government personnel recovered with taxpayer money, and that belong under public stewardship.

## Mechanism

In a sole source arrangement, the government entity that controls recovered UAP materials gives a single cleared contractor access to them under an Unacknowledged Special Access Program (USAP) or a comparable classification structure. That entity is theorized to include elements of the [[NRO (National Reconnaissance Office)]], the successor agencies of the [[Atomic Energy Commission]], or Air Force sensitive activities offices. There is no competitive bidding. The contractor funds its own research through [[Independent Research and Development (IRAD)]] charges or other mechanisms and develops technology or knowledge from the materials. It then sells the resulting products or services back to the same government channels through classified procurement, often at substantial profit margins.

## Criticisms

Critics object to the arrangement on several grounds:

- The profit is circular. The government pays to recover the materials, pays the contractor to study them through IRAD reimbursement, and then pays again to buy what the contractor develops, while the contractor takes a profit at each stage.
- Control is concentrated. Knowledge of what was recovered, and of what was developed from it, stays inside a single corporate entity that has no public accountability and that classification shields from oversight.
- There is no competitive pressure. Without competition, contractors have no incentive to conduct research efficiently, share results across the government, or advance program objectives beyond what is profitable.
- National assets pass into private hands. Recovered non-human materials arguably belong to the public, and sole source arrangements effectively transfer their scientific benefit to private shareholders.

## Alleged Examples

In his 2023 congressional testimony, [[David Grusch]] described UAP materials held by contractors and kept outside normal government oversight, which is consistent with a sole source structure. The [[Kona Blue]] technology transfer failure is cited as evidence that specific contractors hold UAP materials under arrangements that resist any move to shared or competitive access. In that episode, CIA DS&T Deputy Director [[Glenn Gaffney]] blocked an attempt to move materials held by Lockheed Martin into a government-visible AATIP research program.

UAP legacy program research often cites [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works]] as a contractor alleged to hold non-human materials under a sole source or equivalent arrangement. Late in his life, former Skunk Works director [[Ben Rich]] made statements suggesting his division had developed technology to take ET home. Researchers widely interpreted those statements as confirming that the program's technology base was derived from UAP.
