Special Access Programs (SAPs) are U.S. government classified programs with security controls and access restrictions beyond those of standard classified programs. To get access to a SAP, a person needs an appropriate security clearance and also a specific "need-to-know" determination. Access is typically granted through a formal indoctrination process and tracked on strict access rosters. SAPs are further divided into Acknowledged SAPs (ASAPs), Unacknowledged SAPs (USAPs), and Waived Unacknowledged SAPs. Waived Unacknowledged SAPs are the most compartmentalized tier: even the program's existence may be withheld from congressional oversight committees, apart from select gang-of-eight notifications. The Air Force Special Access Program Central Office (SAPCO) manages the formal registry of Air Force SAPs, including those under the authority of the Office of the Administrative Assistant to the Secretary of the Air Force (SAF/AA).

Under it, UFO [[Legacy Programs]] were reconstituted as outside activities under SAF/AA, which shielded them from the standard SAP oversight structure under SAF/AAZ. ## Historical Development

The formal SAP framework recognized today took shape gradually. In the 1950s, 1960s, and 1970s, highly classified programs relied on informal, ad hoc security protocols, mainly top-secret codeword read-on access, that were SAPs in all but name. The Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency's SAP training course states directly that SAPs were first publicly acknowledged in the early 1980s.

Legislative and executive milestones in SAP development include:

- Eisenhower's EO 10501 (1953) standardized the modern three-tier classification system (confidential, secret, top secret) and eliminated a fourth "restricted" tier. In response, program managers introduced informal, ad hoc codeword access controls.
- Nixon's EO 11652 (1972) formally legitimized enhanced access controls for programs involving severe national security concerns, moving from informal practice to structured policy. A 1973 House Committee on Government Operations report, however, documented widespread uncontrolled growth of special access labels across DOD and expressed concern that dozens of agencies were applying access controls without proper authority.
- Reagan's EO 12356 (1982) standardized and expanded the SAP framework and tightened oversight. It effectively ended the "wild wild west" era, in which legacy programs freely exploited top-secret codeword access with minimal accountability. The DCSA's SAP training course cites EO 12356 as the formal acknowledgment of SAPs.
- In the Yellow Fruit case (1983), an internal DoD audit exposed an unacknowledged SAP run out of the Army's Special Operations Division. The audit triggered significant SAP oversight reforms and nearly exposed connected activities.
- In the Clinton/Perry SAPOC Reorganization (1994), Deputy Secretary of Defense William Perry greatly expanded SAPCO (Special Access Program Central Office) and SAPOC (Special Access Program Oversight Committee) and established the Senior Review Group (SRG). The most sensitive legacy activities moved into this quasi-government and industry oversight panel of approximately two dozen individuals. The Wilson-Davis notes describe this structure as the gatekeeping body that denied Admiral Thomas Wilson access to the crash retrieval portfolio. Gerb's "80 Years" timeline video attributes the reorganization to fear of President Bill Clinton's appetite for disclosure. It also cites [[David Grusch]]'s comment on the Judicial Watch podcast that the CIA and other agencies held a "tremendous burnback and shred party" in 1994 to destroy physical records connected to the programs.

## Types of SAPs

Gerb's video *Special Access Required Vol.2* sorts SAPs governed under Executive Order 13526 and DoD Instructions 5205.11/5205.07 into three functional categories:

- Acquisition SAPs make up roughly 75–80% of all DoD Special Access Programs. They protect sensitive research, development, test, and evaluation (RDT&E), modification, and procurement efforts, and are overseen by the Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment (USD A&S).
- Intelligence SAPs protect the planning and execution of sensitive intelligence and counterintelligence operations. The Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security (USD I&S) oversees them.
- Operations and Support SAPs (OSAPs) protect the planning, execution, and logistical support of sensitive military operations whose operational details must stay hidden.

The video illustrates the difference with a hypothetical triangular airframe that incorporates reverse-engineered, non-human-derived technology. An acquisition SAP would protect how the craft is built. An intelligence SAP would protect how the underlying recovery and its exploitability were determined, and an operations and support SAP would protect how and where the craft is used in operations.

## Governance Hierarchy

SAP governance runs from the top down. At the base are component-level SAP Central Offices (SAPCOs) for each of the armed services, the Joint Chiefs of Staff, [[DARPA]], and the Missile Defense Agency; each oversees the SAPs within its own department. Above these are OSD-level SAPCOs under USD(I&S), USD(A&S), and USD(R&E). Above those is the DoD-wide SAPCO, which governs SAP policy across the DoD and is the main contact and access-approval authority for members of Congress. At the top are the Special Access Program Oversight Committee (SAPOC), established in 1994, and its Senior Review Group (SRG). The SRG is a body of senior executive service-level officials, "both in and out of government" according to [[David Grusch]]. It is tasked with preventing duplication of SAPs across the DoD and is the main gatekeeper for access to the most sensitive programs. Gerb explicitly distinguishes the SAPOC/SRG governance structure from the alleged quasi-government-and-industry "control group" that descends from the Majestic 12/NSC 5412 Committee lineage described in [[Manhattan Project 2.0]]. He argues that the SAPOC/SRG is the operational gatekeeper that approves access, while the control group is a separate, higher-level body focused on overall program direction.

## Content-Only SAPs and the Non-Covert Action Loophole

*Special Access Required Vol.2* argues that the single most important loophole legacy programs exploit combines two designations. The first is the non-covert action designation, which converts a covert-action program under [[Covert Action Program (50 U.S. Code § 3093)|50 U.S. Code § 3093]] into a White House SAP with no Title 50 reporting requirement. The second is the [[Content-Only Special Access Program|content-only]] budgetary and administrative designation. A content-only SAP holds only critical program information (CPI) and has no dedicated funding line. Its costs are absorbed into ordinary DoD or armed-service budgets, which waives Title 10 reporting requirements as well. See [[Content-Only Special Access Program]] for full detail.

## Department of Energy SAPs

DOE SAPs draw authority from Executive Order 13526, as DoD SAPs do, and independently from the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]], a statutory basis that is not subject to unilateral presidential declassification. Gerb argues this makes DOE SAPs an especially airtight way to keep programs secret. He cites the DOE's [[Office of Intelligence and Counterintelligence|Office of Intelligence and Counterintelligence]] (OICI) as the agency's internal program-protection and counterintelligence apparatus for such programs.
