The Foreign Material Program (FMP; in official usage the Foreign Materiel Program) is the US Department of Defense program for obtaining and studying foreign weapons, aircraft, equipment and technology. It has two halves: foreign materiel acquisition (FMA), gaining physical possession of or access to an item, and foreign materiel exploitation (FME), the analysis, testing and evaluation of its scientific and technical characteristics. The Director of the [[Defense Intelligence Agency]] is the DoD Executive Agent for the program, under the oversight of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence (today the [[Office of the Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security (USDINS)|Under Secretary of Defense for Intelligence and Security]]), as set out in [[DoD Directive C-3325.01E]] of 2006; its successor, DoD Directive S-3325.01, is classified. Gerb argues that an "off-books" part of the program is one of the places where the [[UFO Legacy Program Framework|UFO legacy programs]] hide.

## Structure

As Gerb lays it out in [[Videos/NASIC - Air Force UFO Crash Retrieval & Reverse Engineering/summary|NASIC - Air Force UFO Crash Retrieval & Reverse Engineering]]:

- **Formal framework from 1961.** Foreign materiel work goes back at least to the Foreign Data Section of 1917, but 1961 marked the program's formal framework.
- **Oversight and executive agent.** The under secretary for intelligence oversees the program; the DIA's Directorate of Science and Technology manages it for the whole DoD. CIA reading-room records show the [[CIA Directorate of Science and Technology]] as a close partner of the DIA's directorate for decades. Gerb suggests the CIA contributes technical support and, through its Directorate of Operations and probably the [[CIA Office of Global Access]] (also established in 2003), clandestine acquisition.
- **Exploitation by domain.** FME is led by each service's science and technical intelligence center: the Army's National Ground Intelligence Center for ground systems; the [[Office of Naval Intelligence]]'s Farragut Technical Analysis Center for sea systems; and, for air and space, the [[National Air and Space Intelligence Center (NASIC)|National Air and Space Intelligence Center]] and, since 2022, the National Space Intelligence Center.
- **Acquisition.** Materiel comes by battlefield capture or by clandestine, deliberate acquisition. The intelligence centers are not direct-action units, so physically securing an item often needs special forces, ground troops or, in the most covert cases, US Special Operations Command or the Joint Special Operations Command.
- **Air Force test and evaluation.** Gerb notes that the Air Force's instruction on the program, AFI 99-114, sits in the 99-series test and evaluation publications: Air Force FME is not treated as purely an intelligence activity, so NASIC is the exploitation center but [[Air Force Test and Evaluation (AF-TE)|Air Force test and evaluation]] owns the policy.

## The off-books program

Gerb's case that part of the UFO legacy effort sits inside the program draws on [[David Grusch]], who has said he found "an off-books foreign material acquisition and exploitation program akin to us recovering a Russian or Chinese military asset", and that the FMA/FME program "as it relates to the UAP subject was essentially hidden in plain sight", with a black program's official "placemat chart" covering its day job while it does other, off-books work. Gerb adds [[James Lacatski]], the DIA scientist who has said he worked on a program in which the US gained access to the interior of a craft of unknown origin, which Gerb places within the DIA's purview. He argues that the program's mission, the agencies involved and its logistics make it a natural home for a UFO effort, and criticizes accounts such as [[The Age of Disclosure]] and [[Jay Stratton]]'s book for naming only the CIA, the Air Force, prime contractors and the Department of Energy while leaving out the DIA, the under secretary for intelligence, the Navy and the [[National Security Council]]. He also notes that [[James Clapper]], whom he calls the National Security Council's legacy program head after [[Dick Cheney]] left office, served as under secretary for intelligence before becoming Director of National Intelligence in 2010.
