The **Denb Report** is an anomalous document of unknown origin and the primary source for the [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]] case. Titled "Research Findings on Chihuahua Disc Crash," it first appeared on an electronic bulletin board in 1992. Copies were later mailed anonymously to a few UFO researchers, among them [[Elaine Douglas]] and [[Nick Redfern]].

## Authorship and Provenance

The report is signed by a person or group identified only as "[[JS (Denb Report Author)|JS]]" and is dated March 23, 1992. It is addressed "to all Denb team members" and labelled "File UFO 3263," which suggests it was one of many UAP cases the [[DENB Team]] studied. According to the report, its facts were "gathered from two eyewitness accounts, documentation illegally copied, and a partially destroyed document" by a person, now dead, in 1978. The DENB group received these notes and documents in February 1992.

There are several ideas about who wrote it:
- JS may be an acronym for Joint Staff, Joint Services, or another government or military department.
- JS may be a single person with access to classified crash retrieval documents.
- The DENB Team may have been an internal US government investigation group, similar to the [[UAP Task Force]], that worked through early internet message boards during the "UAP Dark Ages" (1969–2007).

## Content and Detail

The Denb Report gives a minute-by-minute account of the Coyame incident. It covers:

- Radar tracking data (speed, altitude, bearing, time stamps)
- Communications intercepts between Mexican military units
- CIA recovery team assembly timeline and staging at [[Fort Bliss]]
- Helicopter flight paths and arrival times
- A description of the recovered disc (16 feet 5 inches in diameter, silver, with convex surfaces and two damaged areas, weighing about 1,500 lbs)
- Destruction of the Mexican convoy and bodies with high explosives
- The transport route to Atlanta, Georgia, by way of a rendezvous in the Davis Mountains

The report separates confirmed facts from speculation and notes where its information is incomplete or unconfirmed. It says, for example, that the cause of death of the Mexican recovery team "is not known" and that speculation "ranges from a chemical released from the disc as a result of the damage to a microbiological agent."

## Authenticity Assessment

[[Leonard Stringfield]] reviewed the Denb Report and concluded that it was "authoritatively written using correct military terminology" and that it kept hard evidence clearly apart from speculation, traits inconsistent with a hoax. Stringfield had heard of the Chihuahua case on his own in the late 1970s or early 1980s. He said, "the only detail I vaguely recall is that the US military team had covertly crossed into Mexico to retrieve the object."

[[Ryan S. Wood]], author of *Magic Eyes Only*, rates the Coyame case, which rests on the Denb Report, as medium to high in authenticity. His reasons are:
- Correct military terminology and operational detail
- Consistency with known crash retrieval protocols
- Clear distinction between hard evidence and speculation
- Corroboration from independent researcher testimony (Stringfield)

Several problems still stand in the way of authenticating it:
- Anonymous provenance via internet message boards
- Unknown identity of JS and the DENB Team
- Lack of government documentation (FAA, NORAD, DoD) corroborating the incident
- Loss of Elaine Douglas's original document scans upon her death in 2014

## Distribution Method

The Denb Report first appeared on an early internet bulletin board in 1992 and was then mailed anonymously to researchers in 1993. The [[MJ-12 Documents]] reached researchers the same way in the 1980s, also by anonymous mail. This raises the question of whether the report came from a similar insider source, was a deliberate leak, or was part of a disinformation campaign.

The DENB Team used early internet message boards to distribute the report. It was later revealed that the [[UAP Task Force]] discussed UAP cases on Top Secret online forums, so the DENB Team may have been an earlier version of the same practice: a group of intelligence community professionals using the young internet to pass compartmented information outside official channels.

## Current Status

The original scans of the Denb Report were hosted on [[Elaine Douglas]]'s website (www.elainedouglas.com). Since her death in 2014 they have been missing from the Web Archive copy of the site. [[Ryan S. Wood]] has no copies of the original document for forensic analysis, which is a serious setback for anyone trying to authenticate it.

Inquiries to the FAA turned up no documents about the civilian aircraft crash, likely because it involved no US aircraft and did not happen over US airspace. NORAD was suggested as a possible holder of space tracking records if the object came in from orbital altitude, but no records have been released.

The Denb Report is still the most detailed primary source for the Coyame case, and researchers looking into alleged cross-border UAP crash retrieval operations continue to cite it.
