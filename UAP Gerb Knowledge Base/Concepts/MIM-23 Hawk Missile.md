The MIM-23 Hawk (Homing All the Way Killer) is an American medium-range surface-to-air missile system designed to target low-to-medium altitude aircraft. It is unique among surface-to-air missile systems in that it can fire both conventional warheads and air burst fragmentation rounds. An air burst round explodes near a target instead of striking it, and damages enemy aircraft or vehicles with heavy shrapnel.

## Service History

Hawk missile systems were phased out of general US military use in 1994, though the United States Marine Corps kept using them through 2002. The system was widely exported and used by allied nations, including the [[Peruvian Air Force]] (FAP).

## Significance in UAP Research

[[Jonathan Weygandt]] theorized that the catastrophic damage observed on the crashed [[Egg-shaped UAP|egg-shaped craft]] during the 1997 [[Peru UFO Crash Incident]] was caused by Hawk MIM-23 air burst fragmentation rounds, likely fired by Peruvian Air Force batteries. Weygandt spent a long time studying MIM-23 ballistics. He believed the fragmentation pattern visible on the craft matched a Hawk missile: an enormous gash in the rear, with damage consistent with shrapnel that pierced the hull and bounced around inside.

The US Marine Corps (USMC) had withdrawn its Hawk batteries from [[Operation Laser Strike]] in early 1997, but Peruvian forces still operated the system. If a conventional air burst round could damage a highly advanced non-human craft, it would suggest that a maneuvering UAP might be impossible to hit directly, yet an air burst round detonating nearby might still produce enough fragmentation to damage it at close range. Gerb discussed a similar "one-in-a-million" targeting scenario in his analysis of a 1973 incident in which a naval destroyer's weapons targeted a teardrop-shaped UFO.
