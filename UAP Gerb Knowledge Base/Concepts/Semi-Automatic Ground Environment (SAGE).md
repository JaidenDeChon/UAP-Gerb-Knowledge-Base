The Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) was the United States' first computer-based national air defense system. Conceived in 1951 and deployed by 1963, it was designed to detect and track hostile aircraft entering North American airspace and to coordinate a unified response to them. A network of massive IBM-built computers linked hundreds of radar sites, 24 direction centers and three combat centers into a single, continuously updated picture of U.S. airspace. From its inception through the 1980s, SAGE was the technological backbone of the [[NORAD|North American Aerospace Defense Command]] (NORAD). SAGE is the founding project of the [[MITRE Corporation]], which was spun off from [[MIT Lincoln Laboratory]] in 1958 specifically to provide systems-engineering leadership for the program.

## Development and Scale

SAGE began under the name "Project Lincoln" at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory, a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC) created in 1951 as the direct successor to [[Vannevar Bush]]'s MIT Radiation Laboratory. The project was larger than the Manhattan Project in both cost and personnel. Military, civilian and contractor staff worked on high-speed interception, low-altitude radar coverage, air traffic handling, and real-time data processing and display. SAGE computers held an expected picture of scheduled air traffic, recorded ahead of time on IBM punch cards and continuously updated, and compared it with the real-time picture built from radar returns. Any flying object that did not match a known flight plan appeared on operators' display screens as an unidentified "blip," which prompted an Air Force investigation.

## Technical and Strategic Limitations

SAGE was a genuine technological milestone as the first large-scale real-time computer network, but its performance was mixed. It struggled to keep pace with the emerging threat of Soviet intercontinental ballistic missiles, advancing hardware soon outpaced it, and its systems could be jammed relatively easily.
