The Semi-Automatic Ground Environment (SAGE) was the United States' first computer-based national air defense system, conceived in 1951 and deployed by 1963, designed to detect, track, and coordinate a unified response to hostile aircraft entering North American airspace. SAGE linked hundreds of radar sites, 24 direction centers, and three combat centers through a network of massive IBM-built computers into a single, continuously updated picture of U.S. airspace, forming the technological backbone of the [[NORAD|North American Aerospace Defense Command]] (NORAD) from the system's inception through the 1980s. SAGE is the founding project of the [[MITRE Corporation]], which was spun off in 1958 from [[MIT Lincoln Laboratory]] specifically to provide systems-engineering leadership for the program.

## Development and Scale

SAGE began under the name "Project Lincoln" at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory, an FFRDC created in 1951 as the direct successor to [[Vannevar Bush]]'s MIT Radiation Laboratory. The project's scale exceeded that of the Manhattan Project in both cost and personnel, drawing on military, civilian, and contractor staff to solve problems of high-speed interception, low-altitude radar coverage, air traffic handling, and real-time data processing and display. SAGE computers compared a continuously updated expected picture of scheduled air traffic — recorded ahead of time on IBM punch cards — against the real-time picture generated by radar returns; any flying object that did not match a known flight plan appeared on operator display screens as an unidentified "blip," prompting Air Force investigation.

## Technical and Strategic Limitations

SAGE achieved a genuine technological milestone as the first large-scale real-time computer network, but its performance was mixed. The system struggled to keep pace with emerging Soviet intercontinental ballistic missile threats, was quickly outpaced by advancing hardware, and featured systems that could be jammed relatively easily.

This claim is connected to documented direct acknowledgments made during the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) between the United States and Soviet Union in the 1970s, in which both sides agreed to alert one another if unidentified objects were detected in airspace that could otherwise be mistaken for a hostile incursion — an arrangement that reportedly extended to the Moscow–Washington hotline (the "Molink"), with specific nuclear flash codes ("Atom 222" or "Atom 333") allegedly designated to communicate UFO detections between the two superpowers.
