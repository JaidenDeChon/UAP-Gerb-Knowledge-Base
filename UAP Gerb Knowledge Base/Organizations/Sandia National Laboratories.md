Sandia National Laboratories is a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC) and one of the three multi-mission national laboratories of the [[National Nuclear Security Administration]] (NNSA), a semi-autonomous agency within the [[Department of Energy]]. Its main site shares land with [[Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico|Kirtland Air Force Base]] in Albuquerque, New Mexico, and it has further sites at Livermore, California, the [[Tonopah Test Range (TTR)|Tonopah Test Range]] in Nevada, Kauai in Hawaii and Minnesota, as well as a long presence on the [[Nevada National Security Site (Nevada Test Site)|Nevada Test Site]]. Sandia's core mission is the engineering of nuclear weapons and national security science and technology. Since 2017 it has been managed by National Technology and Engineering Solutions of Sandia (NTESS), a subsidiary of [[Honeywell]]. In UAP research, and above all in the work of UAP Gerb, Sandia is cited as one of the most important institutions in the alleged [[UFO Legacy Program Framework|UFO legacy programs]].

## History

Sandia traces its origins to **Z Division**, the ordnance design, testing and assembly arm of [[Los Alamos National Laboratory]] during the [[Manhattan Project]]. On 12 July 1945, the week of the Trinity test, Z Division selected Sandia Base at the Albuquerque Army Airfield, less than 60 miles south of Los Alamos, as an operating location. By 1 January 1947 its lands and equipment had passed to the [[Atomic Energy Commission]], while Sandia Base remained an Army installation. On 29 January 1947 the [[Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP)|Armed Forces Special Weapons Project]] was created and also set up at Sandia Base, where it began building Manzano Base (Site Able), a nuclear weapons storage site with two underground installations. Z Division became Sandia Laboratory, a branch of Los Alamos, in 1948.

On 13 May 1949 President [[Harry Truman]] asked [[AT&T]] president LeRoy Wilson to manage the laboratory as a separate entity, writing that AT&T had "an opportunity to render an exceptional service in the national interest." From 1 November 1949 Sandia Corporation, a wholly owned subsidiary of AT&T's [[Western Electric]], ran the laboratory for the AEC on the model of [[Bell Labs]], a structure that set a precedent for private-sector management of sensitive government research.

Under AT&T, Sandia opened its Livermore site next to [[Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory]] in 1956, made its first test drop at Tonopah on 8 February 1957, developed the permissive action link required on NATO nuclear weapons from 1962, and hosted President [[John F. Kennedy]] on 7 December 1962. Sandia Base merged into Kirtland Air Force Base in 1971, and in 1979 Congress formally designated Sandia a Department of Energy FFRDC. Management passed to [[Martin Marietta]] in 1993 and, after the 1995 merger, to [[Lockheed Martin]], which ran Sandia and the Tonopah Test Range until 2017, when Honeywell's NTESS took over.

During the Cold War, Sandia was the prime weapons engineering laboratory, turning warhead designs from Los Alamos and Lawrence Livermore into deliverable weapons and ensuring the safety and reliability of the stockpile. It supported underground, atmospheric and ground-level tests at the Nevada Test Site and tests abroad such as Operation Castle and Starfish Prime. For its 75th anniversary it listed 75 technologies, including payloads and ground systems for 23 Defense Support Program and 51 GPS satellites, hypersonic weapons systems, quantum computing platforms and asteroid tracking.

## Role in the Nuclear Security Enterprise

Sandia is one of the 16 DOE-sponsored FFRDCs and one of the laboratories of the [[Nuclear Security Enterprise (NSE)]], the complex of laboratories, production plants and test sites that designs, produces and tests US nuclear weapons. Alongside Los Alamos and Lawrence Livermore, it is one of the three NNSA multi-mission laboratories, and with them it sponsors the experimental programs at the Nevada National Security Site. Unlike Los Alamos and Livermore, which focus on warhead physics, Sandia specializes in engineering and systems integration: the non-nuclear components, delivery systems, safety mechanisms and arming, fuzing and firing systems that make a weapon usable. Its New Mexico campus has five acknowledged technical areas (TA-1 to TA-5) and the roughly 11-square-mile Coyote Test Field, much of it built by AFSWP in the late 1940s. Its personnel with access to DOE special access programs hold Q clearances, the DOE's highest.

## Alleged UAP Legacy Program Connections

Within UAP research, Sandia is cited as a central node in the alleged DOE tier of UAP legacy programs. UAP Gerb's dedicated investigation argues that it has been "one of the most critical institutions" in those programs since its inception. The main claims are:

- **Early crash retrievals.** Former New Mexico state representative [[Andrew Kissner]]'s 1994 sources said recovered discs from 1947 were analyzed for [[Vannevar Bush]] with the help of Los Alamos, Z Division at Sandia Base and AFSWP, and that a classified 1948 executive order vested custody of the discs in AEC laboratories such as Los Alamos and Sandia. The disputed [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] report says Sandia and AFSWP secured the July 1947 New Mexico crash sites under Colonel [[Sherman V. Hasbrouck]], and that three Sandia technicians died of contamination.
- **Reverse engineering.** [[Edgar Fouche]] claimed that Sandia and Lawrence Livermore reverse-engineered the [[Magnetic Field Disruptor (MFD)|magnetic field disruptor]] that propels the TR-3B, an alleged [[Alien Reproduction Vehicle (ARV)|alien reproduction vehicle]]. UAP Gerb reads this as the two FFRDCs handling the most sensitive component while contractors built the airframe. The department of researcher [[Ting Chao Wang]] is alleged to have been transferred to Sandia in 1956.
- **Custody.** UAP Gerb theorizes that FFRDCs such as Sandia and [[MITRE Corporation|MITRE]], being government-owned, are the intended custodians of recovered material. Other researchers allege that recovered materials are held "under Atomic Energy Act protection" at Sandia, where contractors such as Lockheed Martin send staff to work on them. This keeps the hardware out of contractor inventory, and so out of SEC filings and corporate audits.
- **Classification.** The DOE's [[Office of Intelligence and Counterintelligence]] (OICI) is alleged to run legacy-program operations at DOE FFRDCs, using categories under the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]], such as special nuclear material, transclassified foreign nuclear information and NOCONTRACT foreign intelligence information, to hide material from ordinary oversight and from contractors.
- **Recovery logistics.** Sandia is tied to all three NNSA elements UAP Gerb proposes as a domestic recovery arm: its staff have historically served in the [[NEST (Nuclear Emergency Support Team)|Nuclear Emergency Support Team]], whose personnel he identifies at [[Jonathan Weygandt]]'s 1997 Peru crash; it has supplied the [[Office of Secure Transportation (OST)|Office of Secure Transportation]]'s Mobile Guardian Transporters; and [[DOE Special Response Teams (SRTs)|Special Response Teams]] guard its sites. The same video cites the [[2008 Needles UFO Crash Retrieval|2008 Needles crash]], where witnesses tied the recovery convoys to the NNSA.
- **Nevada.** Sandia is said to have run classified programs at [[Area 51]] since at least 1959 and manages the Tonopah Test Range, where [[Richard Dolan]]'s 2021 source placed DARPA-run programs using recovered technology. EG&G photographs of Sandia's work from 1972 to 1992 show its compound and control rooms at [[Area 6 (Nevada Test Site)|Area 6]], a drone-testing site UAP Gerb suspects of legacy-program work. He also proposes that the recovered disc [[Alfred O'Donnell]] said was moved from [[Indian Springs Air Force Base]] in 1955 went to test-site installations run by Sandia.
- **Builders and managers.** Sandia's facilities were designed in part by the firm of [[Max Flatow]], the Manhattan Project's superintendent of construction at Los Alamos. Its managers have included AT&T, whose board [[Vannevar Bush]] sat on from 1947 to 1962, and Honeywell, which inherited part of Sperry Rand, a contractor [[Philip J. Corso]] tied to the exploitation of recovered technology.

The laboratory's dual status as a government-owned yet privately managed FFRDC is viewed by researchers as providing institutional and legal ambiguity that is structurally useful for maintaining compartmented programs outside traditional oversight channels.
