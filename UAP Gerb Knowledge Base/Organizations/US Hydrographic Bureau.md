The US Hydrographic Bureau (officially the Hydrographic Office of the US Navy, later redesignated the Defense Mapping Agency Hydrographic Center and eventually the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency) was the main American government body that collected, analyzed and published nautical charts, oceanographic data and maritime observations sent in by the crews of naval and merchant ships. In UAP and USO research, the Bureau is notable because it received and documented reports of anomalous sightings at sea in the late nineteenth century, when no standard federal system for reporting UAP existed. The best known of these is the [[SS Siberian USO Encounter]] of 1887.

## Role in Early USO Documentation

As part of its routine maritime intelligence work, the Bureau collected reports from ship captains about navigational hazards and unusual phenomena. When Captain RF Moore of the SS Siberian reported a fireball rising from the sea off Cape Race, Newfoundland on November 12, 1887, the Bureau documented the case and characterized it as "one of the most rare and most difficult to explain electrical phenomena." The description accepts that the event happened while admitting that nothing explained it satisfactorily. It is one of the earliest written records by an American government body of what would today be classified as a USO encounter.

The Bureau also compiled the maritime data cited in Gerb's survey of historical USO sightings, including the distribution of anomalous sighting reports from ships before the formal Project Blue Book era. The figures on UAP sightings from ships (258 cases in Blue Book, 104 in the Atlantic and 155 in the Pacific) draw on the historical system of maritime reporting that the Hydrographic Bureau helped establish.

## Relationship to Later USO Reporting Mechanisms

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the Hydrographic Bureau collected reports of anomalous sightings at sea informally, before [[OPNAV 3820]] (1952/1954) and [[JANAP 146C]] (1954) imposed formal rules to suppress them. While the Bureau was operating, no legal prohibition kept witnesses from discussing or reporting unusual things they saw at sea. After World War II, a formal system of military classification emerged, and it turned USO reporting from an open question of maritime safety into a classified intelligence category.
