The National Air and Space Intelligence Center (NASIC) is a US Air Force intelligence organization headquartered in Building 856 at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]], Ohio. It produces assessments of foreign air, space, missile, and cyberspace threats for the Department of Defense and the national intelligence community. It was formally established in February 2003 as the successor to the Air Force's Foreign Technology Division, which had operated under Air Force Systems Command from 1961. It is the Air Force's service intelligence center and its foreign materiel exploitation center, with some 4,100 military, civilian, reserve, Guard and contract personnel in four intelligence analysis groups, four support directorates and 18 squadrons. Since October 2014 it has been a field operating agency reporting to the Air Staff through the Deputy Chief of Staff for Intelligence, Surveillance, Reconnaissance and Cyber Effects Operations (HAF A2/6). Gerb, who devotes a whole video to it, calls it possibly "the most impactful singular organization in all of UFO legacy program history" outside the [[National Security Council]].

## Mission

NASIC's product is scientific and technical intelligence (S&TI): engineering-level assessments of how foreign aircraft, missiles, sensors and space systems work. It draws on several collection disciplines, which Gerb sets out because he argues each has a UFO application:

- **MASINT** (measurement and signature intelligence): identifying targets by their physical fingerprints (engine heat, radar emissions, sound, chemicals, radiation) against libraries of known signatures.
- **GEOINT** (geospatial intelligence): imagery and geospatial information combined into a picture of what is happening at a given place and time.
- **SIGINT** (signals intelligence): intercepted signals.
- **TECHINT** (technical intelligence): in NASIC's case, its role in the [[Foreign Material Program (FMP)|Foreign Materiel Program]], acquiring and reverse-engineering captured or procured hardware such as aircraft parts, missiles or crashed technology.

NASIC owns no aircraft or satellites. It states what intelligence it needs and the owners of the collection platforms decide how to gather it; NASIC owns the deep analysis. In 2022 the [[US Space Force]]'s National Space Intelligence Center took over much of its space mission.

## Lineage

NASIC's line runs back to 1917, through a long series of names and parent commands. Gerb stresses that through all of them the core mission stayed the same: foreign materiel acquisition and exploitation, around which the all-source analysis mission later grew.

| Years | Name | Parent |
|---|---|---|
| 1917 | Foreign Data Section, Airplane Engineering Department | Army Signal Corps, McCook Field, Dayton |
| 1927–1944 | Technical Data Branch / Laboratory | Materiel Division, Wright Field |
| World War II | T-2 Intelligence | Air Technical Service Command |
| 1947–1951 | Technical Intelligence Division | [[Air Materiel Command]] |
| 1951–1961 | [[Air Technical Intelligence Center (ATIC)]] | Air Force headquarters, Director of Intelligence |
| 1961–1991 | [[Air Force Foreign Technology Division|Foreign Technology Division]] | [[Air Force Systems Command]] |
| 1991–1993 | Air Force Foreign Technology Center, then Foreign Aerospace Science and Technology Center | Air Force Intelligence Command |
| 1993–2003 | National Air Intelligence Center | Air Intelligence Agency |
| 2003– | National Air and Space Intelligence Center | Air Force ISR Agency (later 25th Air Force); from 2014 the Air Staff |

Along the way the unit ran [[Project Sign]] and [[Project Grudge]] (as the Technical Intelligence Division) and [[Project Blue Book]] (as ATIC and FTD); captured German jets in Operation Lusty (as T-2); exploited MiG-15s and, as FTD, flew captured Soviet aircraft in programs such as Have Doughnut and Have Drill at [[Area 51]] and the [[Tonopah Test Range (TTR)|Tonopah Test Range]], with pilots from the Air Force Test Center at [[Edwards Air Force Base]]; and in 1971 obtained Pyotr Ufimtsev's paper on edge waves in the physical theory of diffraction, which [[Ben Rich]] of [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works|Skunk Works]] called the "Rosetta Stone" of stealth and which led to the F-117A. Gerb argues that FTD set up the flight-test infrastructure for foreign materiel that the [[Hidden Wing]] later used for recovered and reproduced craft.

## Organization

Today NASIC's four analysis groups are the Air and Cyberspace Intelligence Group, the Geospatial and Signatures Intelligence Group (GS), the Global Exploitation Intelligence Group (GX) and the Space, Missiles and Forces Intelligence Group. Gerb traces two squadrons through its reorganizations, using a late-2000s organization chart reproduced in [[Jeffrey T. Richelson]]'s *The US Intelligence Community*: in the 2000s both the Foreign Materiel Exploitation Squadron and a GEOINT/MASINT analysis squadron sat in a since-dissolved Data Analysis Group. In 2012 the exploitation squadron went to GX and the GEOINT squadron to GS, where it is the Geospatial Intelligence Analysis Squadron (GSI).

- **Foreign Materiel Exploitation Squadron.** An 87-member squadron of engineers, scientists, contractors and airmen from more than ten specialties, many in "one deep" positions. Gerb does not treat it as a legacy-program hub, which would be too obvious a security risk, but thinks individual specialists in it likely hold unacknowledged second roles.
- **Geospatial Intelligence Analysis Squadron (GSI).** In 2015 the [[NGA|National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency]] embedded a NGA Support Team (NST) at Wright-Patterson to act as NASIC's GEOINT functional manager, with image analysts, image scientists, geospatial analysts and computer-aided design specialists, supporting GSI. Gerb calls GSI "NASIC's most critical piece" of the off-books UFO acquisition and exploitation program and "one of the crown jewels" of the legacy programs, because a crash retrieval team needs a full GEOINT picture to find a downed craft and plan its recovery anywhere in the world.
