**Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)** was an American oil-exploration company founded in 1930 by J. Clarence Karcher, inventor of the reflection seismograph, and geophysicist Eugene McDermott. It became Texas Instruments in 1951. In UAP Gerb's investigation of the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery|1948 Aztec crash]], GSI is presented as the kind of company whose scientists moved from oil-finding instruments into [[Vannevar Bush]]'s wartime submarine-detection work, the milieu the video believes produced the anonymous "Dr. G" scientists.

## History

GSI was founded to offer the oil industry the first reflection-seismograph exploration services, the method by which, in the company's telling, most of the world's oil reserves came to be found. In 1939 its founders set up a parent company so that GSI could stay in seismic exploration while the parent branched into new ventures, and in 1941 McDermott and several key employees bought GSI outright. After Pearl Harbor its long experience building sophisticated electronics won it engineering work for the Army Signal Corps and a contract for airborne magnetic submarine detectors for the Navy. By 1942 it was a Navy war-production contractor working under the Office of Scientific Research and Development, which Vannevar Bush headed. In 1951 GSI became Texas Instruments. The video notes that Texas Instruments still honours its early seismic crews as "doodlebuggers."

## Relevance to the Aztec Case

The video argues that GSI is the missing link between the oil-finding "doodlebug" at the centre of the 1953 fraud trial of [[Silas Newton]] and [[Leo GeBauer]] and the wartime scientists [[Frank Scully]] described. Scully wrote that some of the Dr. G scientists had worked out how Japanese submarines were detected magnetically. Newton's manuscript says he knew pre-war oil scientists who moved into secret wartime work for Bush. Gerb suggests GSI would have sought out successful Southwest oilmen such as Newton to test its products. He names GSI's co-founders as possible Dr. G candidates, noting that McDermott's early patents earned Bush's respect and that GSI's later president Cecil Green said McDermott could reverse-engineer almost anything. He also points to Denver geophysicist [[Carl A. Heiland]] and wartime anti-submarine physicist [[John Torrence Tate]] as candidates working in the same field. Gerb adds that Texas Instruments has itself been accused of housing UFO legacy program work.
