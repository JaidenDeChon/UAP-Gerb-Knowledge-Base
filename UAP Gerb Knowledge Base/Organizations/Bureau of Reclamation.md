The Bureau of Reclamation is a US federal agency in the Department of the Interior that manages and develops water resources in the western United States. Founded in 1902, it has built and managed dams, hydroelectric power plants and water conveyance infrastructure across the American West, including hundreds of miles of underground tunnels. Research into deep underground military bases (DUMBs) identifies it as a possible cover organization for clandestine tunnel construction.

## Tunneling Operations

During the 20th century the Bureau excavated hundreds of miles of tunnels, some up to 28.5 feet in diameter, at depths of hundreds to thousands of feet below the surface. It regularly used tunnel boring machines (TBMs) and other excavation technologies to build aqueducts, water diversion systems and penstocks.

## Alleged Clandestine Tunnel Use

Multiple informants have indicated to [[Richard Sauder]] that some Bureau of Reclamation tunnels in the American Southwest have been used as cover for building clandestine secondary tunnels. Sauder describes the hypothetical mechanism this way: a tunnel boring machine working legitimately inside a Bureau aqueduct tunnel deep underground could, in theory, branch off and bore a secondary clandestine tunnel without being detected. A TBM needs a crew of approximately 10 men and can bore up to 10–15 miles a year in good conditions, so such secondary tunnels could connect distant classified facilities while staying invisible to anyone on the surface.

The theory draws support from geography. The Bureau of Reclamation's network of long-distance, deep tunnels in the American Southwest overlaps with the alleged network of underground facilities (DUMBs) in the same region, including sites near Dugway Proving Ground, Kirtland Air Force Base, Los Alamos and China Lake.

## Context in DUMB Construction Theory

Gerb presents the Bureau of Reclamation hypothesis as one of the more plausible explanations for how a large network of covert tunnels could be built without the visible surface disruption, such as spoil disposal and equipment movement, that such work would otherwise cause. Post-WWII Allied examinations of Nazi underground facilities noted a precedent for this kind of concealment: excavated rock had been "carefully scattered into hills miles away," leaving no visible surface evidence of major underground construction.
