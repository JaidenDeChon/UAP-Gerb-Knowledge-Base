Northrop Grumman is one of the largest U.S. defense prime contractors, specializing in aerospace, defense electronics, and information systems. The company was formed in 1994 when Northrop Corporation merged with Grumman Corporation, and it later grew considerably by buying TRW Inc. in 2002 (for approximately $7.8 billion) and Orbital ATK in 2018. These acquisitions created new divisions: Mission Systems, Space Technologies and, later, Northrop Grumman Innovative Systems (NGIS). They gave the company an unmatched spread of work across national security space, missile defense, airborne surveillance, and advanced airframe design. Northrop Grumman has classified research, development, test, and evaluation (RDT&E) access to [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, California. With [[Lockheed Martin]] and [[Boeing]], it is one of only three contractors operating at that government-owned facility.

## Alleged UAP Involvement

[[David Grusch]] and other sources allege that Northrop Grumman has been the primary corporate custodian of UAP legacy programs since its 2002 acquisition of [[TRW]]. TRW is alleged to have held UAP crash retrieval and reverse engineering programs dating back to the 1950s. The company's radar cross-section facility at [[Tejon Ranch]] is alleged to conceal a massive underground complex used for testing recovered non-human vehicles. [[Terry Phillips]] and associates allegedly run a dedicated security infrastructure inside Northrop Grumman to keep these activities from being disclosed. After the TRW acquisition, the company's [[Independent Research and Development (IRAD)]] spending rose from approximately $331 million to over $500 million, and this money allegedly provided cover funding for clandestine UAP-related research.

Gerb's video *80 Years of UFO Crash Retrieval and Reverse Engineering* dates a specific shift to 2004–2005. As defense funds were reallocated during the Global War on Terror, prime contractors such as Northrop Grumman allegedly began using IRAD to self-fund their own UAP retrieval and exploitation efforts. The video describes this self-funded work as only loosely accountable to the prime contractor's government handler at the time, and argues it is even less accountable today. That, it says, raises concerns about private companies holding non-human technology and reverse-engineered derivative vehicles with little government compliance oversight.

## Sectors and TRW Heritage

Northrop Grumman today has four sectors: Aeronautic Systems, Mission Systems, Space Systems and Defense Systems. The 2002 TRW purchase created Mission Systems, first led by TRW's [[Donald C. Winter]], and Space Technologies. Orbital ATK, bought in 2018, became Northrop Grumman Innovative Systems, and in 2020 that sector and Space Technologies merged into today's Space Systems. On that basis, Gerb argues in *Northrop Grumman & TRW* that two of the company's four sectors are TRW's direct heirs.

## The Acquisition Paper Trail

Gerb's first video on the company, *UFO Legacy Programs - Northrop Grumman* (January 2025), argues that Northrop has stayed out of the spotlight that falls on Lockheed Martin, yet is "always alongside in the shadows". Gerb builds his case mainly from what the company bought. After the 1994 merger, Northrop bought [[Teledyne Ryan]] in 1999, a maker of unmanned aircraft and of aerial targets for the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI). TRW followed in 2002, and Northrop called it its "capstone acquisition". Through TRW, Northrop also took in [[BDM International]], which TRW had bought in 1997. BDM employed [[Albert Stubblebine]] as a vice president after he left [[Army INSCOM]] in 1984. In 1989 its board member [[Sumar Shapiro|Rear Admiral Sumner Shapiro]] told [[Bob Echler|Bob Oechsler]] he had studied an extraterrestrial craft at close quarters. The video also says Northrop inherited divisions of [[Sperry Rand]], a company [[Philip J. Corso]] linked to the Army's golden age of reverse engineering. It adds that Ford Aerospace, which appears on [[Steve Wilson|Colonel Steve Wilson]]'s "Star Wars City" chart, is now part of the company. Northrop was also a leading [[C4ISR]] contractor for the [[Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)|Strategic Defense Initiative]] and built the first laser radar used in space.

Gerb ties the 2003 TRW settlement to the 1990s audit that, according to the [[Wilson-Davis Memo|Wilson-Davis notes]], almost exposed the legacy programs and led to their reorganization under the [[SAPOC (Special Access Program Oversight Committee)|SAPOC]] Senior Review Group. In his view, the overcharging auditors found on TRW's early-1990s space contracts was how hidden program money looked to them.

## Whistleblower Allegations

Several witnesses name Northrop directly:

- [[Karl Nell]], who says there is "zero doubt" non-human intelligence exists, spent 13 years as Northrop Grumman's deputy CTO and director of systems engineering and integration. While deputy CTO he was also command representative to US Central Command for the Army's foreign materiel program.
- The Norair paper and the B-2: a 1968 Northrop Norair paper by M. S. Cahn and G. M. Andrew on electro-aerodynamics in supersonic flow studied how an electrostatic field could cut drag. According to [[Nick Cook]]'s *The Hunt for Zero Point*, the Pentagon pulled it in 2002. Some researchers claim the B-2's charged wing leading edge and exhaust point to [[Electrogravitics|electrogravitic]] technology.
- [[Bill Hamilton|William Hamilton]], [[Bill McDonald]] and [[Richard Sauder]] each point to Northrop's radar cross-section range at [[Tejon Ranch]] and its alleged underground levels, rumored to number as many as 42.
- [[Edgar Fouche|Edgar Fouché]] named Northrop, with Lockheed, Boeing and Teledyne Ryan, among the primary contractors of the [[TR-3B]].
- Colonel Steve Wilson said Northrop built the "Great Pumpkin", an anti-gravity disc that glowed orange-gold in flight, at its secret facility northeast of Lancaster, California.
- [[Arnold House]] relayed the account of a Boeing colleague who said he was paid directly by Northrop while working on reverse engineering at [[Area 51]] from 1980 to 1997. The colleague said the program was called [[Project Redlight|Project Red Light]] and that 35 to 40 percent of Area 51's work moved to Utah, likely [[Dugway Proving Ground]]. On that basis Gerb suggests Northrop may have been Redlight's project manager.

Gerb ranks Northrop with Lockheed Martin, [[Boeing]], Raytheon and [[SAIC]] on his "Mount Rushmore" of legacy contractors, which he says work across Army, Navy and Air Force programs that are otherwise kept separate. Lockheed offered in 2008 to divest recovered material to [[AAWSAP]], but Northrop never offers to give material up, and Gerb reads that silence as a sign of deeper secrecy rather than a smaller role.
