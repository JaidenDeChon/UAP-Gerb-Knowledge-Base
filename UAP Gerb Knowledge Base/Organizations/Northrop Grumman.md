Northrop Grumman is one of the largest U.S. defense prime contractors, specializing in aerospace, defense electronics, and information systems. The company was formed through the 1994 merger of Northrop Corporation and Grumman Corporation, then significantly expanded through a series of strategic acquisitions: TRW Inc. in 2002 (for approximately $7.8 billion), and Orbital ATK in 2018. These acquisitions created new divisions — Mission Systems, Space Technologies, and later Northrop Grumman Innovative Systems (NGIS) — giving the company unparalleled breadth across national security space, missile defense, airborne surveillance, and advanced airframe design. Northrop Grumman holds classified research, development, test, and evaluation (RDT&E) access to [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, California, alongside [[Lockheed Martin]] and [[Boeing]], making it one of only three contractors with operational presence at that government-owned facility.

## Sectors and TRW Heritage

Northrop Grumman today has four sectors: Aeronautic Systems, Mission Systems, Space Systems and Defense Systems. The 2002 TRW purchase created Mission Systems, first led by TRW's [[Donald C. Winter]], and Space Technologies. Orbital ATK, bought in 2018, became Northrop Grumman Innovative Systems, and in 2020 that sector and Space Technologies merged into today's Space Systems. UAP Gerb's *Northrop Grumman & TRW* therefore argues that two of the company's four sectors are TRW's direct heirs.

## The Acquisition Paper Trail

UAP Gerb's first video on the company, *UFO Legacy Programs - Northrop Grumman* (January 2025), argues that Northrop has kept out of the spotlight that falls on Lockheed Martin, yet is "always alongside in the shadows". Through TRW, Northrop also took in [[BDM International]], which TRW had bought in 1997. BDM employed [[Albert Stubblebine]] as a vice president after he left [[Army INSCOM]] in 1984. The video also says Northrop inherited divisions of [[Sperry Rand]], which [[Philip J. Corso]] linked to the Army's golden age of reverse engineering, and that Ford Aerospace, listed on [[Steve Wilson|Colonel Steve Wilson]]'s "Star Wars City" chart, is now part of the company. Northrop was also a premier [[C4ISR]] contractor for the [[Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)|Strategic Defense Initiative]] and built the first laser radar used in space.

Gerb ties the 2003 TRW settlement to the 1990s audit that, according to the [[Wilson-Davis Memo|Wilson-Davis notes]], almost exposed the legacy programs and led to their reorganization under the [[SAPOC (Special Access Program Oversight Committee)|SAPOC]] Senior Review Group. In his view, TRW's overcharging on early-1990s space contracts was hidden program money as it appeared to auditors.

## Whistleblower Allegations

Several witnesses name Northrop directly:

- **[[Karl Nell]]**, who says there is "zero doubt" non-human intelligence exists, spent 13 years as Northrop Grumman's deputy CTO and director of systems engineering and integration. While deputy CTO he was also command representative to US Central Command for the Army's foreign materiel program.

According to [[Nick Cook]]'s *The Hunt for Zero Point*, the Pentagon pulled it in 2002. Some researchers claim the B-2's charged wing leading edge and exhaust point to [[Electrogravitics|electrogravitic]] technology.
- **[[Tejon Ranch]].** [[Bill Hamilton|William Hamilton]], [[Bill McDonald]] and [[Richard Sauder]] each point to Northrop's radar cross-section range at Tejon Ranch and its alleged underground levels, rumored to number as many as 42.

- **[[Arnold House]]** relayed the account of a Boeing colleague who said he was paid directly by Northrop while working on reverse engineering at [[Area 51]] from 1980 to 1997. The colleague said the program was called [[Project Redlight|Project Red Light]] and that 35 to 40 percent of Area 51's work moved to Utah, likely [[Dugway Proving Ground]]. On that basis Gerb suggests Northrop may have been Redlight's project manager.

Gerb ranks Northrop with Lockheed Martin, [[Boeing]], Raytheon and [[SAIC]] on his "Mount Rushmore" of legacy contractors, which he says work across the otherwise stove-piped Army, Navy and Air Force programs. Unlike Lockheed, which offered in 2008 to divest recovered material to [[AAWSAP]], Northrop never offers to give material up. He reads its silence as a sign of deeper secrecy rather than a smaller role.
