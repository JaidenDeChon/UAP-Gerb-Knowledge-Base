The Nuclear Emergency Support Team (NEST), formerly the Nuclear Emergency Search Team, is the [[Department of Energy]]'s lead rapid response organization for nuclear and radiological emergencies. It was created in 1974 under Executive Order 12656 and operates under the [[National Nuclear Security Administration]] (NNSA). NEST sends teams of scientists, engineers, nuclear physicists, health physicists, chemists, analysts, and logistics personnel to incidents involving nuclear materials anywhere in the world. Its stated mission is to be "prepared to respond immediately to any type of radiological accident or incident anywhere in the world."

## Organization and Capabilities

NEST personnel have historically come from DOE national laboratories and contractors rather than from Department of Defense forces. Around the mid-1990s, its corporate partners included [[EG&G]], [[Los Alamos National Laboratory]], [[Sandia National Laboratories]], [[Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory]], and Raytheon. When deployed, NEST restricts access to incident sites, performs diagnostics and threat mitigation, conducts agency planning and coordination, and handles disablement and cleanup. It is an umbrella organization that handles all nuclear and radiological emergencies for the Department of Energy.

NEST has its own fleet of vehicles, including high-tech vans, MBB BO-105 helicopters, and Cessna Citation 2 jets with a top speed of 464 mph. Under its authority within the Department of Defense, it can commandeer military aircraft for emergency deployment. It also works alongside the US Naval Explosive Ordnance Disposal Technology Division, the US Army 52nd Ordnance Group, and US Special Operations forces.

In February 1997, NEST introduced the Joint Technical Operations (JTO) team. The team was designed to provide deployable technical operational support even faster than the existing teams, and it operates 365 days a year, 24 hours a day.

## Primary Authority

NEST's primary authority comes from the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]]. The Schumer Rounds legislation identified the same act as the framework used to misclassify UAP data and materials as transclassified foreign nuclear information (TFNI). A 1995 NEST assessment team report noted that NEST laboratory program managers "enjoy a relatively high degree of autonomy and independence" in directing research priorities, a management style rooted in the laboratories' heavy focus on R&D during their formative years.

## Alleged UAP Involvement

NEST is theorized to be the primary scientific and radiological assessment component of UAP crash retrieval task forces. In the 1997 [[Peru UFO Crash Incident]] described by [[Jonathan Weygandt]], approximately 30 DOE personnel in biological containment suits, rain jackets marked "DOE," gas masks, and varying levels of MOPP gear were flown to the crash site of an [[Egg-shaped UAP|egg-shaped craft]] in US Army CH-47 Chinook helicopters within approximately 8–9 hours of the crash. That timeline is consistent with a NEST team leaving [[Sandia National Laboratories]] on Cessna Citation 2 jets (approximately 7.5 hours of flight time over 3,446 miles) and then commandeering CH-47s from [[1st Battalion 228th Aviation Regiment|1-228th Aviation]] under [[Joint Task Force Bravo]] for local transport.

Gerb's analysis demonstrated that in the same year as Weygandt's encounter, NEST considered deploying to Bolivia and Colombia to recover debris from the Russian Mars 96 probe, which carried plutonium-238 generators. That plan was based on intelligence from US Space Command and [[Defense Support Program (DSP)]] satellites. This establishes a provable pattern of NEST deploying to South America on the basis of satellite intelligence, the same mechanism theorized for the Peru crash retrieval.

Taken together, the presence of DOE radiological response assets at alleged UAP crash sites, NEST's authority under the Atomic Energy Act, and its established partnerships with the same national laboratories accused of hosting UAP reverse engineering programs make NEST a central node in theories of organized crash retrieval operations.
