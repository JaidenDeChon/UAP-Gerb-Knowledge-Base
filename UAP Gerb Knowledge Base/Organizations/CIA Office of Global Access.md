The **Office of Global Access (OGA)** is part of the Central Intelligence Agency's Directorate of Science and Technology and was established in 2003. Its stated mission is to facilitate access to denied areas and targets for intelligence collection.

## Alleged UAP Involvement

### Cross-Border Retrieval Operations

In a September 2024 article, journalist [[Christopher Sharp]] accused the Office of Global Access of taking part directly in recovering crashed UAP in foreign countries. According to Sharp's reporting, OGA provides the operational logistics and coordination for retrieval missions, while intelligence agencies such as the [[NRO (National Reconnaissance Office)|NRO]] supply various kinds of satellite and signals intelligence to locate downed craft. [[JSOC]] (Joint Special Operations Command) is a subordinate command within Special Operations Command made up of Delta Force, SEAL Team 6, the 24th STS, Regimental Reconnaissance Company and the Intelligence Support Activity. It is allegedly the force on the ground for these retrievals.

Sharp's source stated the OGA acts as "a facilitator for people to get in and out of countries" and is "very clever at being able to get anywhere in the world they want to." The source added that although the military does the physical retrieval, objects are moved out of military control "fairly quickly into private hands" because the military "has to keep too many records."

### 1974 Coyame Precedent

The [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]] took place nearly three decades before the OGA was created. The CIA's alleged role in it suggests that, before 2003, other CIA departments or ad hoc teams carried out the functions later consolidated under the OGA. According to the [[Denb Report]], the CIA received radio intercepts of the Mexican military's discovery of a crashed disc on August 25, 1974, and "immediately began forming a recovery team." The report notes, "The speed with which this team and equipment was assembled suggests that this was either a well-rehearsed exercise or one that had been performed prior to this event."

By 2100 hours on August 26, 1974, a CIA recovery team with unmarked helicopters had assembled and staged at [[Fort Bliss]], Texas. The team covertly entered Mexican airspace, retrieved the disc from a stopped Mexican military convoy whose personnel were all dead, destroyed the site with high explosives, and transported the object to Atlanta, Georgia. This pattern is consistent with OGA operations as Sharp's sources describe them.

If so, the CIA has coordinated cross-border UAP crash retrievals since at least the 1970s, and after 2003 the OGA gave these longstanding practices a formal institutional home.

## Operational Model

The operational model that Sharp describes, corroborated by alleged historical cases, runs in these stages:

1. Intelligence: satellite and reconnaissance assets (NRO, reconnaissance aircraft) locate and track crash sites.
2. Logistics and coordination: the CIA (via OGA or predecessor units) facilitates access, coordinates with foreign governments or bypasses them, and manages operational security.
3. Retrieval: JSOC or specialized military units carry out the physical recovery under hazmat protocols.
4. Transfer: objects are moved "fairly quickly into private hands" (aerospace contractors, national laboratories) to minimize military documentation.

This model matches the patterns seen in the [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]] and the [[Peru UFO Crash Incident|1997 Peru UFO Crash Retrieval]].
