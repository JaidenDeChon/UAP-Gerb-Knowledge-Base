The Atomic Energy Commission (AEC) was a United States government agency established in 1946 to manage the nation's nuclear weapons program and promote peaceful uses of atomic energy. For UFO investigations, it was one of three bodies required to receive every [[Project Sign]] UFO investigation report, along with the [[Army and Navy Research and Development Board]] and the [[USAF Scientific Advisory Board]].

## Role in Early UFO Investigations

In 1948 the Atomic Energy Commission was made a mandatory recipient of Project Sign reports. That choice indicates high-level government concern that UFO phenomena might be connected to atomic or nuclear capabilities. It suggests that UFO sightings were being evaluated as possible security threats involving nuclear weapons, nuclear facilities or advanced propulsion technologies, as well as aviation anomalies.

The AEC may have been included because of UFO sightings near nuclear installations, worries about foreign reconnaissance of atomic sites, or interest in advanced propulsion systems that could bear on nuclear technology. Sending all Project Sign findings to the AEC shows how UFO investigation overlapped with national security priorities in the early Cold War.

## Additional Context

The AEC was linked to [[Avery Technical Center]] through laboratory studies of radiobiological warfare. This indicates that its work extended into classified military research programs beyond its public nuclear energy mission.

## Restricted Classification and UAP Material Concealment

The AEC had a "restricted" classification standard, a private-sector tier separate from the standard government Secret or Special Access Program (SAP) designations. It was used to classify the 1949 research that [[Battelle Memorial Institute]] did on [[Nitinol (Nickel-Titanium Alloy)]] for [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]]. Under this standard, only personnel inside the contractor with a need to know had access, and even people holding government SAP clearances were shut out. The 1949 Battelle nitinol research was not declassified until 2010.

[[David Grusch]] has cited the AEC's 1954 Atomic Energy Act, together with related trans-classified "nuclear foreign material" designations, as one of the primary legal mechanisms used to keep UAP-related materials and programs outside the reach of congressional oversight. Battelle's use of the AEC restricted classification for UAP-related material research is an early documented example of this practice.
