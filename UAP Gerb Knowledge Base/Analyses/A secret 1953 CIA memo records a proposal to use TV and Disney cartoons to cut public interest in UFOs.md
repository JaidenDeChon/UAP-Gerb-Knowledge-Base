In January 1953 the [[CIA]] brought five scientists together for four days to look at the Air Force's UFO reports. This was the [[Robertson Panel]], and Gerb treats it as the moment the [[UFO Stigma]] was made on purpose. His evidence for that intent is mostly later testimony: the astronomer [[Thornton Page]], one of the five, recalling that the chairman told members their job was to debunk, and [[J. Allen Hynek]]'s 1979 account of an unwritten rule not to "rock the boat". The CIA's own record of the meetings is more direct than either. Written for the agency a month later and stamped SECRET, it records the panel proposing a "debunking" campaign on television, in films and in popular magazines. It names the people and outlets that might help, from a Princeton psychologist to Walt Disney's studio.

## The finding

The [[Robertson Panel Report]] archived in this vault is the "Durant Report": a memorandum dated 16 February 1953 from Frederick C. Durant, an associate member of the panel, to the head of the CIA's Office of Scientific Intelligence. It has two parts and three attachments. The panel's own two-page report, signed by the five members, is attached as Tab A. The debunking proposal isn't in that formal report. It's in Part II of Durant's memo, which he describes as an "unofficial supplement" holding the members' comments that "they believed were inappropriate for inclusion in the formal report".

That section lays out a public "educational program" with two aims. One is training military personnel to recognise balloons, aircraft and meteors. The other, in the memo's own quotation marks, is "debunking": reducing public interest in flying saucers through the mass media. The members suggest advisers and channels by name, and expect the program to run for at least a year and a half.

This is a contemporaneous official record that the panel wanted to shape public opinion about UFOs, and how it thought that could be done. It's independent of anything Page or Hynek remembered later. It doesn't show that the campaign was ever carried out, and its stated reason is a Cold War one, not the hiding of anything.

## The evidence

1. **The purpose of Part II.** Page 3 of the memo: "b. An unofficial supplement to the official Panel Report to AD/SI setting forth comments and suggestions of the Panel Members which they believed were inappropriate for inclusion in the formal report (Part II)." Page 8 adds that at the last session the members restated "opinions and suggestions on details that were felt inappropriate for inclusion in the formal report. It was agreed that the writer would incorporate these comments in an internal report to the AD/SI."
2. **The two aims.** Page 21, under "Educational Program": "The Panel's concept of a broad educational program integrating efforts of all concerned agencies was that it should have two major aims: training and "debunking"."
3. **What debunking was for.** Page 22: "The "debunking" aim would result in reduction in public interest in "flying saucers" which today evokes a strong psychological reaction. This education could be accomplished by mass media such as television, motion pictures, and popular articles. Basis of such education would be actual case histories which had been puzzling at first but later explained. As in the case of conjuring tricks, there is much less stimulation if the "secret" is known. Such a program should tend to reduce the current gullibility of the public and consequently their susceptibility to clever hostile propaganda."
4. **Who might help.** Pages 22 and 23: "psychologists familiar with mass psychology should advise on the nature and extent of the program. In this connection, Dr. Hadley Cantril (Princeton University) was suggested." The memo notes that Cantril wrote the study of the panic over Orson Welles's 1938 *War of the Worlds* broadcast. It goes on: "Also, someone familiar with mass communication techniques, perhaps an advertising expert, would be helpful. Arthur Godfrey was mentioned as possibly a valuable channel of communication reaching a mass audience of certain levels." And: "The Jam Handy Co. which made World War II training films (motion picture and slide strips) was also suggested, as well as Walt Disney, Inc. animated cartoons. Dr. Hynek suggested that the amateur astronomers in the U. S. might be a potential source of enthusiastic talent "to spread the gospel"." The panel thought that true cases "showing first the "mystery" and then the "explanation" would be forceful."
5. **How long.** Page 24: "The Panel believed that, with ATIC's support, the educational program of "training and debunking" outlined above might be required for a minimum of one and one-half to two years." ATIC was the Air Technical Intelligence Center, which ran [[Project Blue Book]].
6. **Civilian groups.** Pages 25 and 26, under "Unofficial Investigating Groups": the panel named two civilian UFO groups and said "such organizations should be watched". The rest of that passage is on a faint page and the transcription marks several words as uncertain, so this post doesn't rely on it.
7. **What the videos use instead.** In [[Videos/The Origin of the UFO Stigma/summary|The Origin of the UFO Stigma]], Gerb quotes the formal report's call to strip UFOs of "special status", then turns to Page: "signatory to the report thoron L page admits on the 3rd of October 1993 that the chairman told members ahead of the meeting to debunk all UFO reports presented to them and to quote reduce public concern". In [[Videos/The Alien and UFO Obscure Oddities Iceberg (Level 2)/summary|Iceberg Level 2]] he quotes Hynek: the panel was "handed down the unwritten law of the Air Force Don't Rock the Boat play with it cool it don't get the public excited". No video quotes Part II, or mentions Disney, Godfrey, Cantril or the mass-media plan. The [[Videos/National Reconnaissance Office - UFO Crash Retrievals, Surveillance, and Legacy Program Gatekeepers/summary|NRO video]] shows only the memo's list of evidence.

## How it fits together

Gerb's argument has two steps: the panel was told to debunk, and debunking became policy. For the first step, Page's and Hynek's recollections came decades after the event. The formal report (Tab A, pages 27 and 28) is more guarded. It recommends that the security agencies "strip the Unidentified Flying Objects of the special status they have been given and the aura of mystery they have unfortunately acquired", through a program "designed to reassure the public of the total lack of evidence of inimical forces behind the phenomena".

Part II fills in what that wording leaves out. It uses the word "debunking" itself and says the aim is less public interest. It lists television, films and magazines as the tools, and names the people who could make the case persuasive. The panel members chose to leave these details out of the report they signed, and the memo says so. A viewer who has only heard the later recollections can see from this record that the idea was written down by the CIA in February 1953.

## What cuts against it

- **The stated reason is national security, not concealment.** The memo argues that a flood of poor reports could clog air-defence channels, and that a public primed to panic was open to "clever hostile propaganda" (page 17 lists both dangers, and page 22 has that phrase). The panel concluded "unanimously that there was no evidence of a direct threat to national security in the objects sighted" (page 10). Nothing in the memo says anything was being hidden. Read on its own terms, it's a plan to calm the public and cut false alarms during the Cold War.
- **These are suggestions, not decisions.** Names were "mentioned" and "suggested". Nothing shows that anyone approached Disney, Godfrey, Jam Handy or Cantril. The CIA's own historian, Gerald Haines, writes that after the panel "CIA officials said no further consideration of the subject appeared warranted". Whether any of this was ever carried out is a separate question. Some researchers, among them [[Leslie Kean]], point to possible later echoes, such as a 1966 CBS special on UFOs; this memo doesn't settle that.
- **"Debunking" here also means explaining.** The memo's idea was to publicise cases that had been solved, the way revealing a conjuring trick takes the magic out of it. That's an aim of reducing interest, but it's built on explaining real reports, not on inventing explanations.
- **It isn't a secret any more.** The memo has been public for decades. See below.

## Already known?

Yes. Haines's 1997 history of the CIA and UFOs, published in the agency's journal *Studies in Intelligence*, says the panel "suggested using the mass media, advertising, business clubs, schools, and even the Disney corporation". He writes that the Condon Committee was allowed to publish the Durant memo with minor deletions in 1969, and that the CIA gave a copy to the civilian group Ground Saucer Watch in 1975. History.com also mentions the Disney suggestion, and Wikipedia quotes the mass-media line. The copy in this vault is the CIA's 2013 release. Researchers know the passage well; Gerb's videos just never show it.

## What would settle it

Whether the campaign happened would take records from after February 1953: CIA, Air Force or National Security Council papers adopting the educational program, or any approach to Disney, Godfrey, the Jam Handy Organization or Cantril about UFOs. Page's original letter would also show what he said about the panel's instructions, in his own words.
