[[Edgar Fouche|Edgar Fouché]], the former Air Force sergeant behind the TR-3B story, said one of his sources, a man he called Gerald, belonged to [[TREAT]]. Fouché said this stood for the "Tactical Reconnaissance Engineering Assessment Team", a secret unit like the [[Men in Black]]. Gerb says he could find only one other public mention of TREAT: an anonymous letter to [[Steven Greer]], dated 31 August 1997. It accuses Prince [[Hans Adam II]] of Liechtenstein of funding TREAT and someone in it who was married to a former head of Army intelligence. Gerb works out that the general is [[Albert Stubblebine]], and builds on that across several videos. But another TREAT matches every detail in the letter. It was a series of conferences on alien-abduction trauma, run from 1989 by Stubblebine's future wife, and the Prince is reported to have helped pay for them.

## The finding

The 1997 letter is probably about Rima Laibow's TREAT, short for "Treatment and Research of Experienced Anomalous Trauma", not about Fouché's covert team. If so, the letter offers no independent support for Fouché's TREAT, and no link between that team and Stubblebine or the Army's intelligence command.

This doesn't show that Fouché's TREAT didn't exist, or that Gerald was invented. It removes the one outside document Gerb gives for it. The letter itself isn't available online, so its exact words are known here only as Gerb reads them.

## The evidence

1. In [[Videos/Alien Reproduction Vehicle - TR-3B and the Flying Triangles/summary|the TR-3B video]] (December 2024), Gerb introduces the team: "treat stands for the Tactical reconnaissance engineering assessment team AKA what we might call the Men in Black I can find only one publicly available mention of treat and this is from Lenin's Crown Prince Hans Adams in a letter to Dr Steven Greer dated August 31st 1997 this letter from an unknown sender".
2. He then reads the key line: "quote Adam's funding should be focused on his support for treat and it's gr D who is wed to a former US Army General ex- head of Army intelligence and security command end quote". The captions garble one word ("gr D"). It may be "Dr", which would mean a doctor attached to TREAT was married to the general.
3. Gerb identifies the general as Stubblebine, who commanded the US Army Intelligence and Security Command ([[Army INSCOM]]) from 1981 to 1984. He concludes: "what we can learn here is that quite possibly treat is the real life Men In Black housed within US Army inscom".
4. A month later, in [[Videos/Philip J. Corso - US Army UFO Technology Research & Development/summary|the Corso video]], the link has firmed up. Gerb calls Stubblebine "the alleged founder of the nsa's Men in Black treat tactical reconnaissance engineering assessment team".
5. In [[Videos/Dugway Proving Ground - UFO Legacy Programs/summary|the Dugway video]] Gerb suggests a witness's interrogators "could also be the infamous alleged NSA Menin black treat". In [[Videos/UFO Legacy Programs - Science Applications International Corporation (SAIC)/summary|the SAIC video]] he says, "I have implicated the NSA and UFO legacy program operations from enforcing gatekeeping in the form of Treat", and lists among the accusations against Stubblebine "funding clandestine men in blacktype entities".
6. Outside the channel: the *Baltimore Sun* reported in April 1992 on "TREAT (Treatment and Research of Experienced Anomalous Trauma)", "a group founded by New York psychiatrist Rima Laibow" to deal with post-traumatic stress in people who said they had been abducted by aliens, with its first meeting in May 1989. A 2021 research blog on Laibow lists seven TREAT conferences from 1989 to 1994. The ISGP study of Greer's Disclosure Project gives the same founding year.
7. Laibow married Stubblebine. Wikipedia, citing the Southern Poverty Law Center, says he remarried "psychiatrist Rima E. Laibow" shortly after his 1994 divorce, and the two later set up the Natural Solutions Foundation together. So by August 1997 the doctor who founded TREAT was "wed to" a former head of INSCOM.
8. The Prince and TREAT: Robert Durant's 1992 profile of the Senate aide C.B. "Scott" Jones, as quoted by ISGP, says Jones "acted as liaison with the Prince of Liechtenstein (who was the major benefactor of the conference)", meaning Laibow's TREAT II meeting in January 1990. The 2021 blog quotes a conference report with the same words, and an unauthorised biography of Jones lists "Rima Laibow's TREAT conferences" among the projects Hans-Adam funded.

## How it fits together

Line the letter up against Laibow's TREAT, one detail at a time:

- **The acronym.** The letter names TREAT, and Laibow's TREAT was a known name in UFO circles from 1989.
- **The doctor.** The letter refers to TREAT's "Dr" (if that's the garbled word), and Laibow was a medical doctor who founded it.
- **The marriage.** The letter says that person is married to an ex-head of INSCOM, and Laibow had married Stubblebine by about 1995.
- **The money.** The letter says the Prince funded TREAT, and the Prince is reported as the main backer of at least one TREAT conference.

Read as a description of Fouché's covert team, the letter needs a secret NSA unit that happened to share its acronym with a Prince-funded abduction group, and whose doctor happened to be married to the same general as that group's founder. Read as a description of Laibow's conferences, nothing in it needs explaining.

The tone of the letter still matters. Gerb says the writer accused the Prince of supporting "counterintelligence operations against the American people on the subject of UFOs". Greer himself was suspicious of the Stubblebine circle. In his writing he describes a "slippery shadowy-op psychiatrist" working alongside a general that ISGP identifies as Stubblebine, and ISGP thinks, without being sure, that the psychiatrist is Laibow. So the writer may well have believed TREAT was an intelligence front. But that would be a suspicion about Laibow's conferences, not a sighting of a team called the Tactical Reconnaissance Engineering Assessment Team.

## What cuts against it

- **Nobody here has seen the letter.** Its text is known only from Gerb's reading. If the full letter names the Tactical Reconnaissance Engineering Assessment Team, or describes field work rather than conferences, this reading fails.
- **The writer is unknown.** Gerb calls the sender unknown, and no copy turned up online, so the writer's intent can't be checked.
- **The funding evidence is thin.** The Prince's funding of TREAT is reported in secondary sources (Durant's profile, quoted by ISGP; a blog quoting a conference report; an unauthorised biography), and some of these may go back to the same account. No financial record was found. Jacques Vallée's diary for July 1989 reportedly mentions "a meeting on abductions sponsored by Prince Hans-Adam", which fits the first TREAT meeting, but that was seen only as a blog quotation.
- **Two TREATs could both be real.** Fouché, or his source, might have used a real covert unit's name that happened to match Laibow's group. Fouché's TREAT is known from his 1998 talk and book, nine years after Laibow's TREAT began, so it's also possible that he or his source borrowed a name already familiar in UFO circles. Neither can be shown from here.
- **The marriage date is approximate.** Wikipedia gives only "shortly thereafter" after a 1994 divorce, and a blog gives 1994. Either way it's before 1997.

## Already known?

Partly. The pieces have been laid side by side before. ISGP's study of Greer's Disclosure Project notes that Laibow founded TREAT, that she is Stubblebine's wife, and that the Prince financed her TREAT II conference. But no source found links the 1997 letter to Laibow's TREAT, or sets either against Fouché's TREAT. A search for the expansion "Tactical Reconnaissance Engineering Assessment Team" turns up only an acronym listing, with no source behind it.

## What would settle it

The full text of the 31 August 1997 letter, and where Gerb found it. If it says more about what TREAT did (conferences, patients, abduction research), it settles the question. Greer's archives or the papers of C.B. Scott Jones at the University of Colorado, which hold correspondence with Laibow and the Prince, may also show whether the Prince was still funding Laibow's work by 1997.
