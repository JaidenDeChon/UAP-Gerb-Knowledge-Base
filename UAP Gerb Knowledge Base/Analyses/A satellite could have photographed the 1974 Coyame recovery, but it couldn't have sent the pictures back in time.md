The [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval|1974 Coyame crash]] is said to have been a midair collision between a disc and a small plane in the desert of northern Mexico, followed by a secret American recovery. Most of the story comes from one anonymous document, the [[Denb Report]]. In it, the [[CIA]] follows the Mexican army's recovery from the air, using "satellite and recognizance aircraft overflight", and decides from those pictures to send a team in from [[Fort Bliss]], Texas. Gerb suggests the satellite pictures came from the Keyhole spy satellites of the [[NRO (National Reconnaissance Office)|National Reconnaissance Office]] (NRO), and that this may be an example of the classified crash imagery [[David Grusch]] has described.

A US satellite could well have photographed the site in August 1974. What it couldn't do was get the pictures to anyone that week. In 1974 every American spy satellite shot on film, and the film came back to Earth in capsules. The first satellite able to send its images down as they were taken, the KH-11, went up in December 1976. Gerb gives that date himself in his NRO video.

## The finding

The Denb Report has the CIA using satellite pictures as part of a decision it made within about a day of the crash being found. In 1974 no US satellite could deliver a picture that fast. Any satellite photo of the disc being loaded onto trucks would have reached analysts only after its film capsule was dropped, caught and developed, which took days at the very least and often weeks. By then the team had already flown.

This doesn't mean no satellite could have seen the recovery. It means satellite pictures couldn't have shaped the decision the report describes. The aircraft photos in the same passage are a different matter, and they are what the report's timeline actually relies on (see below).

## The evidence

1. In [[Videos/The 1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash/summary|the Coyame video]], Gerb reads the report's timeline. Mexican searchers spot the wreckage at "approximately 10:35 hours" the day after the crash (26 August). The team is "staged at Fort Bliss" by "2100 hours 26 AUST 74". Then: "satellite and recognizance aircraft overflight that day indicated that both the crash disc and civilian aircraft had been removed from the crash sites and loaded onto flatbed trucks later flights confirmed the Convoy had Departed the area heading south at that point the CIA had to make a choice".
2. The report goes on: "high altitude overflights indicated that the Convoy had stopped before reaching any inhabited areas or major roads", and then "at low altitude highspeed overflight was ordered the photos returned by that aircraft showed all trucks and jeeps stopped some with doors open and two human bodies laying on the ground beside the two vehicles the decision was immediately made to launch the recovery team". The helicopters left Fort Bliss at "1438 hours". The report gives no date for the launch. Read in sequence, it falls on 27 August, as the [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval|event page]] infers.
3. Later in the same video Gerb says: "the team was allegedly given intelligence by the CIA gathered from satellite and recognizance aircraft overflight of the disc in plane Collision". He adds: "the satellite imagery as discussed by Noah Torres likely came from the fleet of Keyhole spy satellites put into orbit around 1971 these satellites were primarily operated by the nro perhaps this is one instance of Highly classified highres satellite imagery of crashed UAP David grush has referred to". From the wording, it isn't clear whether the Keyhole idea is Gerb's or comes from [[Noe Torres]], who co-wrote a book on the case.
4. In [[Videos/National Reconnaissance Office - UFO Crash Retrievals, Surveillance, and Legacy Program Gatekeepers/summary|the NRO video]], Gerb explains how satellite pictures came back before 1976. The Corona satellites "took highresolution photos of denied airspace on film and [music] physically ejected capsules to be captured in air by American assets." The NRO's goal was to allow imagery "to be transmitted electronically, not literally collected in the air by airplanes", and "in 1976, they succeeded to accomplish this near realtime photo transfer. In 1976, the NRO launched the Kenon aka KH11 Kenon." He never brings Coyame up in that video, and the Coyame video never mentions how long satellite film took to come back.
5. The outside record agrees with Gerb's NRO video. The first KH-11 Kennen launched on 19 December 1976 and was the first US imaging satellite to send its pictures down electronically, through a relay satellite (NRO; Wikipedia; The Space Review). Before it, the NRO's film-return systems "could not provide finished intelligence data quickly enough to be communicated in the moment global crises developed", in the NRO's own words.
6. The "around 1971" date matches Hexagon, also called the KH-9. The first Hexagon launched on 15 June 1971 and sent its film back in four capsules, which aircraft caught in midair over the Pacific near Hawaii (Wikipedia; Orbit Codex; National Museum of the US Air Force). Its partner, the higher-resolution Gambit or KH-8, also returned film in capsules. A Gambit launched on 14 August 1974 was still in orbit at the end of that month (Wikipedia; Orbit Codex). So a satellite that could have photographed northern Mexico was up there at the time.
7. The space historian Dwayne Day writes that with these film systems, pictures reached "the light tables of photo-interpreters days, or even weeks after the photos had been taken", and that Gambit images reached interpreters in Washington "days or even weeks" late (The Space Review).

## How it fits together

1. The disc wasn't found until about 10:35 on 26 August, so any picture of it being loaded onto trucks was taken after that.
2. The team took off at 14:38, most likely on 27 August. Between the first possible photo and the launch there were roughly 28 hours at most.
3. A satellite photo taken in that window was still sitting on a reel in orbit. It came back only when a capsule was dropped and caught over the Pacific, and then it still had to be flown to be developed and studied. On Day's account, that took days at best.
4. So whatever the satellites saw, it couldn't have been part of the "choice" the report describes, or in the intelligence Gerb says the team was given. The step from the report to NRO satellite imagery of the kind Grusch describes doesn't hold for 1974.
5. The aircraft photos are another matter. Film from a plane also had to be developed, but a plane could land within hours and its film could be processed straight away. One example from 1969: a 7th Air Force newspaper reported that a reconnaissance squadron in Vietnam had cut the time from landing to film under the interpreters' magnifiers to about 30 minutes. The report's final decision rests on aircraft photos ("the photos returned by that aircraft"), and that timing is possible for 1974.

## What cuts against it

- **"Satellite" may be loose wording.** The report's author may have used "satellite" loosely for any overhead surveillance, or added it to make the story sound more impressive. In that case the problem is a detail of the report, and the main casualty is the NRO and Grusch link Gerb draws from it.
- **A secret faster system.** It's possible some faster way of reading out film existed and is still classified. Nothing in the public record points to one. The NRO's own history treats the KH-11 as the first, and the faster-delivery projects studied before it, such as a film read-out version of Gambit, are described as never flown.
- **A lucky capsule.** A capsule could in principle have been dropped soon after the satellite passed over. Even then, catching it, flying it to a lab and developing it would have taken longer than the report's window. No source found gives an exact best-case turnaround in hours, which is why this post is labelled moderate, not strong.
- **Some NRO link may survive through aircraft.** The NRO also helped run the CIA's spy-plane programs (Gerb calls this "Program D" in the NRO video). If NRO-linked aircraft took the photos, the agency could still be involved, just not through satellite imagery.
- **None of this tests whether the crash happened.** It says only that the satellite part of the report doesn't fit what satellites could do in 1974.

## Already known?

Web searches for the Coyame case together with satellites, Keyhole, the KH-11 or real-time imagery found no one making this point. The facts about film-return satellites and the KH-11 are well established. Putting them next to the Denb Report's timeline seems to be new.

## What would settle it

- The full text of the Denb Report, to see whether its author says anything more specific about the satellites than the line Gerb reads.
- Noe Torres and Ruben Uriarte's discussion of the imagery in their books, to see whether they or Gerb first named Keyhole satellites as the source.
- A declassified NRO record of any capsule recovery in late August 1974, or a published best-case turnaround for Hexagon or Gambit film, would show exactly how long the fastest satellite delivery took.
