**Leo A. GeBauer** (rendered "Gabauer" in the captions of Gerb's video) was a Phoenix-based specialist in magnetics and electronic detection equipment and the business partner of the Denver oilman [[Silas Newton]]. In UFO literature he is known mainly as the man journalist [[J. P. Cahn]] named as "Dr. G", the unnamed scientific source of [[Frank Scully]]'s story of a UFO crash retrieval at Aztec, New Mexico, in 1948. He was also Newton's co-defendant in a 1953 fraud trial over his oil-detecting device, a "doodlebug", which in effect discredited the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery|Aztec case]] for decades. Gerb's coverage of him comes from a single video on Aztec.

## Background

GeBauer's work centred on electronic and magnetic detection, the kind of technology used to look for oil, gas and water underground and, during World War II, to detect submarines from the air. Researchers [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]] found that he held senior positions at the AiResearch laboratories around 1946; Cahn's 1952 article had him calling himself chief of laboratories at AiResearch in Phoenix and Los Angeles during the war.

Researcher [[William Steinman]] went further. He claimed that GeBauer had worked at AiResearch's Phoenix and Los Angeles divisions, and that GeBauer and the Denver geophysicist [[Carl A. Heiland]] were research partners who adapted wartime magnetic airborne detection gear to finding oil, gas and water, and had even lived and worked together on a 1935 government project in Antarctica. Gerb notes that Steinman gives no evidence for the Antarctica partnership or the AiResearch claim, though he has no particular reason to doubt it.

## Connection to the Aztec UFO Case

GeBauer's name became permanently linked to the 1948 Aztec crash through *Behind the Flying Saucers*, Scully's bestselling book of 1950. Scully's source, Newton, said he was repeating what "Dr. G" had told him: a group of up to eight scientists with, in Scully's phrase, "more degrees than a thermometer".

In September 1952 Cahn's article "The Flying Saucers and the Mysterious Little Men" named GeBauer as the one and only Dr. G, saying Newton had told him so, and tied both men to oil fraud. GeBauer denied again and again, in writing and under oath, that he was Dr. G. Gerb finds no basis for Cahn's claim, but allows that GeBauer may still have been one of several Dr. Gs. Steinman concluded that Heiland was both a real Dr. G and the original, unintentional leaker to Newton and GeBauer; the Ramseys' leading candidate is the physicist [[John Torrence Tate]].

## The 1952-1953 Fraud Trial

Denver businessman [[Herman Flader]], who had invested heavily in Newton's oil wells and bought an interest in GeBauer's oil-detecting devices, sought charges with the FBI's help over $250,000, encouraged by Cahn. The charges, interstate transportation of stolen property and marketing "worthless machinery", were filed before a Denver magistrate on 10 October 1952, and on the 18th GeBauer was arrested in Phoenix and Newton in Los Angeles.

The trial ran from 10 November to 29 December 1953. Gerb, following the Ramseys and Steinman, presents it as a targeted prosecution meant to suppress the Aztec story rather than a real case of fraud, and points to the following:

- The evidence was dubious. The prosecution's expert, Colorado School of Mines geophysicist Dr. James Meredith, testified that such doodlebugs "couldn't detect submarines, much less oil", but the device he examined was one the district attorney had bought at a war-surplus store as "very similar" to GeBauer's, not GeBauer's own. According to Steinman, Heiland had been asked to testify against the devices first and refused, saying he could not do so with a clear conscience because he and GeBauer had developed them together.
- Most investors were satisfied. The FBI's own inquiry found that 32 of the 33 investors in Newton and GeBauer's ventures were pleased. Flader was the only complainant, and he was still receiving royalties from Newton's oil wells. He also denied signing the contract that gave him an interest in three of the devices, though eyewitnesses testified that they saw him sign it.
- Cahn was involved. According to court records, Cahn helped the prosecution throughout the trial, and he had written to [[J. Edgar Hoover]] about Newton in April 1951.
- Defense evidence was set aside. Ranch owner Horace Steele testified that GeBauer's machine had defined 35 oil wells for him, one of which he sold most of for $1.75 million, and another witness showed photographs of three water wells the device had found.

Both men were found guilty and faced up to 30 years, but they were never sentenced or fined. Newton agreed to pay $18,000 in court costs, "as though this was a civil case", in Gerb's words. Gerb and the Ramseys see the trial as retaliation for leaking the Aztec story; GeBauer, in this reading, was caught up in it as Newton's partner and a possible link to Dr. G.

## The Doodlebug and Magnetic Detection Technology

Gerb argues that GeBauer's doodlebug was probably an early magnetic anomaly detector (MAD) and not the fraud it was portrayed as at the trial. MAD equipment was a real technology, adapted from wartime submarine detection. [[Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)|Geophysical Service Inc.]], which became Texas Instruments in 1951 and still calls its early field crews "doodlebuggers", built airborne magnetic submarine detectors for the Navy under [[Vannevar Bush]]'s Office of Scientific Research and Development. To Gerb, wartime oil-exploration scientists of this kind are the most likely members of Dr. G, and GeBauer's expertise placed him among them.

## Later Reassessment

The conviction discredited both GeBauer and the Aztec case for decades. Steinman's 1986 book reopened the case, and the Ramseys' research followed. Both treat GeBauer and Newton as possible victims of a campaign to bury the Aztec story and to punish those who leaked it.
