**Leo A. GeBauer** (spelled "GeBauer" in some video captions) was a Phoenix-based physical scientist who specialized in magnetics and electromagnetic detection technology. He is primarily known in UFO literature as one of the alleged sources for the 1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO crash retrieval story, and as the co-defendant with [[Silas Newton]] in a controversial 1952-1953 fraud trial that effectively discredited the Aztec case for decades.

## Background and Expertise

GeBauer was an accomplished scientist whose professional work centered on magnetic anomaly detection—technology used both for locating underground oil and gas deposits and, during World War II, for detecting submarines. He was one of the inventors or early developers of devices colloquially called "doodlebugs," electromagnetic sensing equipment designed to locate subsurface resources.

According to researcher [[William Steinman]]'s investigations, GeBauer may have worked at the Phoenix and Los Angeles divisions of the Air Research Corporation around 1946, and potentially consulted with [[Carl A. Heiland]]'s Heiland Research Corporation in Denver on magnetic detection equipment. Steinman theorized—though without conclusive documentary proof—that GeBauer and Heiland may have worked together on a US government research project in Antarctica in 1935, and later collaborated on adapting World War II anti-submarine magnetic aerial detection (MAD) technology for postwar oil and gas exploration.

## Connection to the Aztec UFO Case

GeBauer's name became permanently linked to the 1948 Aztec crash through [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 bestselling book *Behind the Flying Saucers*. In the book, Scully referenced a mysterious figure called "Dr. G"—described as a scientist (or group of scientists) with "more degrees than a thermometer" who provided firsthand information about a recovered flying disc near Aztec, New Mexico.

In 1952, San Francisco Chronicle writer [[J. P. Cahn]] published a debunking article in which he identified GeBauer as the sole person behind the "Dr. G" designation. This claim has been heavily disputed. Both GeBauer himself and subsequent researchers have maintained that "Dr. G" was actually an amalgamation of up to eight scientists, and that GeBauer was—at most—one member of that group, not the singular source. GeBauer continuously denied being "Dr. G" in writing and under oath.

Steinman and other researchers theorized that GeBauer may have been used as a conduit for information from more senior scientists who wished to remain anonymous, possibly including figures like Carl A. Heiland or members of [[Vannevar Bush]]'s wartime scientific networks.

## The 1952-1953 Fraud Trial

On October 18, 1952, GeBauer and Newton were arrested and charged with defrauding Denver businessman [[Herman Flader]] of $250,000 through the sale of interests in oil-detecting devices (the doodlebug). The charges included interstate transportation of stolen property and marketing "worthless machinery."

The trial, which ran from November 10 to December 29, 1953, was highly unusual and has been characterized by researchers as a targeted prosecution intended to suppress the Aztec story rather than pursue legitimate fraud:

- **Dubious evidence**: The prosecution's expert, Colorado School of Mines geophysicist Dr. James Meredith, examined a device the district attorney had bought at a war-surplus store and described only as "very similar" to GeBauer's, not GeBauer's own. Testimony about that substitute was that it could not detect oil or gas.
- **Satisfied investors**: FBI investigators found that 32 of 33 investors in Newton and GeBauer's ventures were pleased with their returns; Flader was the sole complainant, and he was actively receiving royalties from Newton's successful oil wells at the time of filing charges.
- **J. P. Cahn's involvement**: Cahn assisted the prosecution throughout the trial and had previously written to [[J. Edgar Hoover]] and the FBI to build a case against Newton, suggesting coordination between media and law enforcement.
- **Ignored defense evidence**: Multiple witnesses testified that GeBauer's devices had successfully located oil wells and water deposits, including testimony from ranch owner Horace Steele, who stated GeBauer's machine identified 35 oil wells, one of which Steele sold for $1.75 million.

GeBauer and Newton were found guilty on December 29, 1953, but the case evaporated—neither man was ever sentenced, and no fines were levied. Curiously, Newton agreed to pay $18,000 to cover court costs, as though the criminal prosecution had been a civil matter.

Many researchers, including Steinman and [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]], argue the trial was retaliation for Newton's role in publicly disclosing the Aztec crash, with GeBauer caught in the crossfire due to his business association with Newton and possible connection to the "Dr. G" network.

## The Doodlebug and Magnetic Detection Technology

UAP Gerb's video argues that GeBauer's doodlebug was not the fraud it was portrayed as at trial, but probably an early magnetic anomaly detection (MAD) device. MAD equipment was a genuine technology adapted from wartime submarine detection programs. Companies like [[Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)]], which became Texas Instruments in 1951, developed reflection-seismograph and electromagnetic detection equipment for the oil industry before being recruited by [[Vannevar Bush]]'s Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD) during World War II to build submarine-detecting systems.

Testimony during the trial confirmed that GeBauer's devices had successfully located subsurface oil and water on multiple occasions. UAP Gerb's video argues that the prosecution's reliance on a surplus-store substitute rather than GeBauer's actual equipment undermines the verdict.

## The Question of Dr. G

Whether GeBauer was one of the "Dr. G" scientists remains unresolved. What is clear:

- GeBauer repeatedly and consistently denied being the sole "Dr. G"
- J. P. Cahn's identification of GeBauer as the singular source has no supporting evidence
- GeBauer's expertise in magnetic detection and his professional networks placed him in contact with scientists who would have been logical candidates for early UFO crash retrieval analysis
- Steinman theorized that [[Carl A. Heiland]], a geophysicist and alleged MJ-12 member, was the primary "Dr. G" source and potentially leaked information to both Newton and GeBauer

If GeBauer was indeed one member of a larger "Dr. G" network, his role may have been as a technical consultant on magnetic propulsion—consistent with multiple early accounts that described recovered UFO craft as operating via magnetic principles.

## Later Years and Legacy

GeBauer largely disappeared from public UFO discourse following the 1953 trial. The conviction—despite its lack of sentencing and the questionable evidence presented—succeeded in discrediting both GeBauer and the Aztec case for decades.

It was not until the 1980s, with William Steinman's investigative work and the later research of Scott and Suzanne Ramsey, that GeBauer's reputation began to be reassessed. Modern researchers view both GeBauer and Newton as possible victims of an orchestrated disinformation and suppression campaign designed to bury the Aztec crash story and punish those who leaked details of early UFO crash retrievals.
