**Leo A. GeBauer** (spelled "GeBauer" in some video captions) was a Phoenix-based physical scientist who specialized in magnetics and electromagnetic detection technology. In UFO literature he is known mainly as one of the alleged sources for the story of a UFO crash retrieval at Aztec, New Mexico, in 1948. He was also [[Silas Newton]]'s co-defendant in a controversial fraud trial in 1952-1953 that in effect discredited the Aztec case for decades.

## Background and Expertise

GeBauer was an accomplished scientist whose work centered on magnetic anomaly detection, a technology used to locate underground oil and gas deposits and, during World War II, to detect submarines. He was one of the inventors or early developers of the devices informally called "doodlebugs," electromagnetic sensors designed to find resources below the surface.

According to the investigations of researcher [[William Steinman]], GeBauer may have worked at the Phoenix and Los Angeles divisions of the Air Research Corporation around 1946, and may have consulted on magnetic detection equipment for [[Carl A. Heiland]]'s Heiland Research Corporation in Denver. Steinman theorized, without conclusive documentary proof, that GeBauer and Heiland may have worked together on a US government research project in Antarctica in 1935. He also theorized that they later worked together to adapt World War II anti-submarine magnetic aerial detection (MAD) technology for oil and gas exploration after the war.

## Connection to the Aztec UFO Case

GeBauer's name became permanently linked to the 1948 Aztec crash through *Behind the Flying Saucers*, [[Frank Scully]]'s bestselling book of 1950. In it, Scully wrote about a mysterious figure called "Dr. G", a scientist (or group of scientists) with "more degrees than a thermometer" who gave firsthand information about a flying disc recovered near Aztec, New Mexico.

In 1952, San Francisco Chronicle writer [[J. P. Cahn]] published a debunking article that identified GeBauer as the only person behind the name "Dr. G". This claim has been heavily disputed. GeBauer himself and later researchers have maintained that "Dr. G" was a composite of up to eight scientists, and that GeBauer was at most one member of that group rather than the single source. GeBauer consistently denied being "Dr. G", both in writing and under oath.

Steinman and other researchers theorized that GeBauer may have passed on information from more senior scientists who wanted to stay anonymous, possibly including Carl A. Heiland or members of [[Vannevar Bush]]'s wartime scientific networks.

## The 1952-1953 Fraud Trial

On October 18, 1952, GeBauer and Newton were arrested and charged with defrauding Denver businessman [[Herman Flader]] of $250,000 by selling him interests in oil-detecting devices (the doodlebug). The charges included interstate transportation of stolen property and marketing "worthless machinery."

The trial ran from November 10 to December 29, 1953, and was highly unusual. Researchers have described it as a targeted prosecution meant to suppress the Aztec story rather than to pursue a real case of fraud. They point to the following:

- The evidence was dubious. The prosecution's expert, Colorado School of Mines geophysicist Dr. James Meredith, examined a device the district attorney had bought at a war-surplus store, described only as "very similar" to GeBauer's. He did not examine GeBauer's own device. The testimony about that substitute was that it could not detect oil or gas.
- Most investors were satisfied. FBI investigators found that 32 of the 33 investors in Newton and GeBauer's ventures were pleased with their returns. Flader was the only complainant, and when the charges were filed he was still receiving royalties from Newton's successful oil wells.
- J. P. Cahn was involved. Cahn assisted the prosecution throughout the trial and had earlier written to [[J. Edgar Hoover]] and the FBI to build a case against Newton, which suggests coordination between the press and law enforcement.
- Defense evidence was ignored. Several witnesses testified that GeBauer's devices had found oil wells and water deposits. One was ranch owner Horace Steele, who said GeBauer's machine identified 35 oil wells, one of which Steele sold for $1.75 million.

GeBauer and Newton were found guilty on December 29, 1953, but the case then fell away: neither man was ever sentenced, and no fines were imposed. Curiously, Newton agreed to pay $18,000 to cover court costs, as though the criminal prosecution had been a civil matter.

Many researchers, including Steinman and [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]], argue that the trial was retaliation for Newton's part in making the Aztec crash public. In their view GeBauer was caught in the crossfire because he was Newton's business partner and was possibly connected to the "Dr. G" network.

## The Doodlebug and Magnetic Detection Technology

UAP Gerb's video argues that GeBauer's doodlebug was probably an early magnetic anomaly detection (MAD) device, and not the fraud it was portrayed as at the trial. MAD equipment was a real technology, adapted from wartime submarine detection programs. Companies such as [[Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)]], which became Texas Instruments in 1951, built reflection-seismograph and electromagnetic detection equipment for the oil industry. During World War II, [[Vannevar Bush]]'s Office of Scientific Research and Development (OSRD) recruited them to build systems for detecting submarines.

Testimony at the trial confirmed that GeBauer's devices had found underground oil and water several times. The video argues that the verdict is undermined because the prosecution relied on a substitute bought at a surplus store instead of GeBauer's actual equipment.

## The Question of Dr. G

Whether GeBauer was one of the "Dr. G" scientists remains unresolved, but some points are clear:

- GeBauer repeatedly and consistently denied being the sole "Dr. G"
- J. P. Cahn's identification of GeBauer as the singular source has no supporting evidence
- GeBauer's expertise in magnetic detection and his professional networks placed him in contact with scientists who would have been logical candidates for early UFO crash retrieval analysis
- Steinman theorized that [[Carl A. Heiland]], a geophysicist and alleged MJ-12 member, was the primary "Dr. G" source and potentially leaked information to both Newton and GeBauer

If GeBauer was one member of a larger "Dr. G" network, he may have been its technical consultant on magnetic propulsion. That would fit several early accounts, which described recovered UFO craft as working on magnetic principles.

## Later Years and Legacy

After the 1953 trial, GeBauer largely disappeared from public discussion of UFOs. Although no sentence followed and the evidence was questionable, the conviction discredited both GeBauer and the Aztec case for decades.

GeBauer's reputation began to be reassessed only in the 1980s, through William Steinman's investigations and, later, the research of Scott and Suzanne Ramsey. Modern researchers see GeBauer and Newton as possible victims of an orchestrated campaign of disinformation and suppression, meant to bury the Aztec crash story and to punish those who leaked details of early UFO crash retrievals.
