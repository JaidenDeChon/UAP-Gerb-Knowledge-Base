Carl Sagan (1934–1996) was an American astronomer, planetary scientist, cosmologist and science communicator, widely regarded as one of the most influential public scientists of the 20th century. In UFO research he is known for a measured skepticism. He treated the possibility of extraterrestrial life as a serious scientific question and made real contributions to SETI research, but he openly criticized what he considered unfounded claims in UFO culture, including alien abduction stories and the [[Betty and Barney Hill Abduction Incident|Betty Hill star map]] interpretation.

## Scientific Career

Sagan was a professor at Cornell University and a prolific author. His planetary science work included research on the surface conditions of Venus and Mars. He played a leading part in creating the [[Voyager Golden Record]], a message carried on both Voyager spacecraft in case extraterrestrials ever find them. He co-founded the Planetary Society and was a central figure in SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence).

## Cosmos and UFO Skepticism

Sagan's 1980 television series *Cosmos: A Personal Voyage* addressed the [[Betty and Barney Hill Abduction Incident|Betty Hill star map]] in the episode "The Backbone of Night." He argued that the pattern of stars [[Betty Hill]] recalled under hypnotic regression could not be reliably matched to the [[Zeta Reticuli Star System]], as its proponents claimed. With hundreds of visible stars to choose from, he noted, subjective pattern-matching could produce many plausible "matches", depending on which stars were selected. His rebuttal challenged the statistical argument that the odds against the pattern being random were at least 1,000 to one.

Throughout his career Sagan held that the possibility of extraterrestrial life was scientifically compelling. The evidence UFO proponents offered, such as abduction accounts, blurry photographs and anecdotes, did not in his view meet the standard an extraordinary claim requires. His formulation "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" became a widely cited axiom of scientific skepticism.
