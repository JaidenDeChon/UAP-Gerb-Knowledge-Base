Mark McCandlish (1952–2021) was an American aerospace and aeronautical concept illustrator who devoted much of his career to documenting and publicly disclosing the existence of alleged Alien Reproduction Vehicles (ARVs). His clients included the US Air Force, [[Rockwell International]], [[Boeing]], [[Lockheed Martin]], and respected publications such as [[Aviation Week and Space Technology]] and [[Popular Science]]. He is best known for his widely circulated technical cutaway blueprint of the disc-shaped ARV "Flux Liner," which he based on the firsthand testimony of industrial designer [[Brad Sorenson]]. Sorenson claimed to have seen three such craft at a classified exhibit near [[Norton Air Force Base]] in November 1988. McCandlish was found dead on April 13, 2021, from an apparent self-inflicted gunshot wound. His death remains controversial because he had been scheduled to testify before the US Senate on UAP matters in June 2021.

## Early Career and ARV Discovery

Starting in the late 1970s and through the 1980s, McCandlish worked as a contract illustrator for major defense aerospace clients. An early brush with classified aerospace programs came when personnel at [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works]] commissioned him to illustrate a jet design for a Department of Defense pitch. During that meeting, Skunk Works personnel accidentally let slip that aircraft components would tear apart at Mach 17 (approximately 13,000 mph). That speed is roughly double that of the world's fastest manned aircraft, the SR-71 Blackbird (Mach 3.3). McCandlish later described this incident as his first indication that classified programs operated far beyond publicly acknowledged aeronautical capabilities.

In fall 1988, McCandlish planned to attend an air show at Norton Air Force Base in San Bernardino, California. He pulled out at the last moment to finish a design contract for Popular Science, and his friend and fellow designer Brad Sorenson went in his place. The following week, Sorenson called McCandlish, clearly shaken, and said he had witnessed something he "probably shouldn't have seen." At a lunch meeting afterwards, Sorenson described in detail a classified exhibit at [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, California, approximately 30 miles southwest of [[Edwards Air Force Base]]. It included three disc-shaped craft referred to as Alien Reproduction Vehicles, or "Flux Liners."

## The Flux Liner Blueprint

Starting in March 1989, McCandlish turned Sorenson's spoken descriptions and sketches into precise technical blueprints of the ARV. The finished cutaway illustration showed a smooth, dome-topped disc with concentric capacitor plate rings, a central energy column, a crew compartment with ejection seats (off-the-shelf F-4 Phantom components), a synthetic vision system using paired optical cameras and stereoscopic goggles, and a ball-trackball pilot control system. McCandlish concluded that the ARV was essentially a large Tesla coil designed to negate gravity and inertia, drawing its energy from Zero Point Energy, the quantum fluctuations present throughout spacetime.

McCandlish stressed how crudely the ARV was built compared with the craft in reported UAP sightings. It had a thick coat of lead paint (apparently for x-ray radiation shielding), smudged surfaces, chipped paint, messy internal filaments, and off-the-shelf life support and navigation components. He called it a "Model T of anti-gravity vehicles": a human attempt to reproduce technology recovered from non-human craft, built by engineers who did not fully understand the underlying propulsion principles.

Brad Sorenson told McCandlish that his drawing was so accurate it was "probably one of the most dangerous illustrations you've ever done in your life."

## Additional Witnesses and Corroboration

McCandlish sought out other ARV witnesses and received accounts from several sources. One important corroborating witness was a man identified as Kent Sellen (the name is also given as Selin or Sellin). McCandlish met him at a 1992 air show at Edwards, and Sellen told him that in 1973, while working as a crew chief for experimental aircraft, he had inadvertently seen a Flux Liner ARV at [[Edwards Air Force Base]]. His detailed description added features Brad Sorenson had not known about, particularly in how the vehicle was configured and operated. McCandlish compared this sighting with cases in [[Leonard Stringfield]]'s unpublished crash retrieval files, which included a Class A witness who saw a large disc-shaped craft with a transparent dome inside open hangars at Edwards in the 1960s.

In 2001, McCandlish obtained two photographs taken in 1967 by USAF Captain [[Harvey Williams]] while flying a C-47 at 12,000 feet approximately 25 miles southwest of Provo, Utah (within 100 miles of [[Dugway Proving Ground]]). McCandlish argued that these images matched the ARV in proportions and physical features, including the synthetic vision system bubbles on the outside of the craft.

## Public Disclosure and Consequences

McCandlish's most prominent public appearance was at [[Steven Greer]]'s 2001 [[National Press Club]] panel, held as part of the Disclosure Project, where he presented his ARV line art and testified about the Flux Liner. The Disclosure Project also pressured him into naming Brad Sorenson publicly. McCandlish later deeply regretted this, because it brought Sorenson unwanted attention and damaged his career.

After the 2001 appearance, McCandlish reported signs of surveillance, received threatening phone calls, lost all his defense industry clients, and had money and vehicles seized by the IRS. He described lying awake at night with a loaded .9mm handgun under his pillow, wondering why he hadn't yet been killed. He nevertheless kept presenting his research, including a 2015 lecture on interstellar travel at the Secret Space Program Conference. There he argued that back-engineered off-world technology had enabled private corporations and state actors to build a hidden space program and a "Breakaway Civilization."

## Death

On April 13, 2021, at age 69, McCandlish was found dead in his home from an apparent self-inflicted gunshot wound. That day he had told his friend Rick Price that he would call later, and told his girlfriend that he would speak to her when he arrived at her animal shelter. Neither conversation showed any sign of suicidal intent. Researcher [[Robert D. Morning Star]] stated that McCandlish had been in contact with Senator [[Marco Rubio]] and was scheduled to testify before Senate elements on UAP matters in June 2021. Brad Sorenson told Gerb: "Mark McCandlish was a stupid dramatic selfish fool who died because he could not keep his mouth shut. Learn from this."

Other people in the ARV research community have also died in suspicious circumstances. Filmmaker James Allen, for example, died of alleged radioisotope poisoning approximately one month before his ARV documentary was due to be released. These deaths have led researchers to argue that McCandlish's death fits a pattern of foul play aimed at people involved in ARV disclosure.

## Related Figures and Context

In his later years McCandlish also discussed the work of other alleged ARV-related researchers, including [[Edgar Fouché]]'s claims about the triangular [[TR-3B]] craft, Gary McKinnon's alleged discovery of non-terrestrial USAF personnel records, and the activities of the alleged 412th Reverse Engineering Group at Edwards and Nellis Air Force Bases. He believed there were 10 to 15 craft incorporating non-human intelligence technology to varying degrees, including the Flux Liner, the [[Aurora Craft|Aurora]], and the TR-3B.
