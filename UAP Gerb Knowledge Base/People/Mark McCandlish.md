Mark McCandlish (1953–2021) was an American aerospace and aeronautical concept illustrator who devoted much of his later career to documenting and publicly disclosing the alleged Alien Reproduction Vehicles (ARVs). His clients included the US Air Force, Rockwell International, [[Boeing]], [[Lockheed Martin]], and publications such as [[Aviation Week and Space Technology]] and *Popular Science*. He is best known for his widely circulated cutaway blueprint of the disc-shaped [[Alien Reproduction Vehicle (ARV)|ARV]] "Flux Liner," which he based on the firsthand account of industrial designer [[Brad Sorenson]]. Sorenson claimed to have seen three such craft on 12 November 1988, at a classified exhibit at [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, California, to which he was flown from the [[Norton Air Force Base Air Show]]. McCandlish was found dead on 13 April 2021 from an apparent self-inflicted gunshot wound. His death remains controversial because, according to researcher Robert D. Morning Star, he had been due to testify to Senate elements on UAP matters in June 2021.

## Career

From the late 1970s through the 1980s, McCandlish worked as a contract illustrator for major defense aerospace clients. An early brush with classified aerospace work came when [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works]] personnel (then still Lockheed's) commissioned him to illustrate a design for a new high-speed jet. In McCandlish's telling, the meeting grew heated when it slipped out that aircraft components would tear apart at Mach 17 (about 13,000 mph), far beyond any acknowledged aircraft. Gerb notes that this happened even before the Sorenson episode, and McCandlish took it as his first sign that classified programs operated far beyond publicly acknowledged capabilities.

## The Norton Air Show and the ARV

McCandlish had planned to attend the 12 November 1988 air show at [[Norton Air Force Base]] in San Bernardino, California, with his friend and fellow designer Brad Sorenson. He dropped out at the last moment to finish a design contract for *Popular Science*, and Sorenson went with one of his own clients. About a week after the show, McCandlish called Sorenson, who sounded almost depressed and said he thought he had seen something he wasn't supposed to see. Too nervous to talk on the phone, Sorenson met him for lunch and described what had happened: he had been flown from Norton to [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, less than 30 miles from [[Edwards Air Force Base]], where an exhibit for about 30 senior guests included three disc-shaped craft referred to as Alien Reproduction Vehicles, or "Flux Liners."

## The Flux Liner Blueprint

Starting in March 1989, McCandlish turned Sorenson's spoken descriptions and sketches into precise technical blueprints of the ARV. The finished cutaway illustration showed a smooth, dome-topped disc with concentric capacitor plate rings, a central energy column, a crew compartment with ejection seats (off-the-shelf F-4 Phantom components), a synthetic vision system using paired optical cameras and stereoscopic goggles, and a ball-and-laser pilot control system. McCandlish concluded that the ARV was essentially a large Tesla coil designed to negate gravity and inertia, drawing its energy from [[Zero Point Energy]], the quantum fluctuations present throughout spacetime.

McCandlish stressed how crudely the ARV was built compared with the craft in reported UAP sightings. It had a thick coat of lead paint (apparently for x-ray shielding), smudged surfaces, chipped paint, messy internal filaments, and off-the-shelf life support and navigation components. He called it a "Model T of anti-gravity vehicles": a human attempt to reproduce technology recovered from non-human craft, built by engineers who did not fully understand the underlying propulsion principles.

Brad Sorenson told McCandlish that his drawing was so accurate it was "probably one of the most dangerous illustrations you've ever done in your life."

## Additional Witnesses and Corroboration

McCandlish sought out other ARV witnesses and received accounts from several sources. One important corroborating witness was a man identified as Kent Sellen (the name is also given as Selin or Sellin). McCandlish met him at a 1992 air show at Edwards, and Sellen told him that in 1973, while working as a crew chief for experimental aircraft, he had inadvertently seen a Flux Liner ARV at [[Edwards Air Force Base]]. His detailed description added features Brad Sorenson had not known about, particularly in how the vehicle was configured and operated. Gerb sets this sighting beside cases in [[Leonard Stringfield]]'s unpublished crash retrieval files, which include a Class A witness who saw a large disc-shaped craft with a transparent dome inside open hangars at Edwards in the 1960s.

McCandlish also obtained two photographs taken in 1967 by USAF Captain [[Harvey Williams]] while flying a C-47 at 12,000 feet approximately 25 miles southwest of Provo, Utah, and showed them at the 2001 Disclosure Project panel. He argued that these images matched the ARV in proportions and physical features, including the synthetic vision system bubbles on the outside of the craft. Gerb notes that Provo is within 100 miles of [[Dugway Proving Ground]].

## Public Disclosure and Consequences

McCandlish's most prominent public appearance was at [[Steven Greer]]'s 2001 [[National Press Club]] panel, held as part of the [[Disclosure Project]], where he presented his ARV line art and testified about the Flux Liner. People in the Disclosure Project also pressed him into naming Brad Sorenson publicly. McCandlish later deeply regretted this, because it brought Sorenson unwanted attention.

After the 2001 appearance, McCandlish reported signs of surveillance, received threatening phone calls, lost all his defense industry clients, and had money and vehicles seized by the IRS. He described lying awake at night with a loaded 9mm handgun under his pillow, wondering why he hadn't yet been killed. He nevertheless kept presenting his research, including a 2015 lecture, "The Feasibility of Interstellar Travel." There he argued that back-engineered off-world technology had enabled private corporations and state actors to build a hidden space program and a "Breakaway Civilization."

## Death

On 13 April 2021, aged 68, McCandlish was found dead in his home from an apparent self-inflicted gunshot wound; the Shasta County coroner ruled it suicide. (Gerb gives his age as 69.) That day he had told his friend [[Rick Price]] that he would call later, and told his girlfriend that he would see her at the animal shelter where she worked. Neither conversation showed any sign of suicidal intent. Researcher [[Robert D. Morning Star]] stated that McCandlish had been in contact with Senator [[Marco Rubio]] and was scheduled to testify before Senate elements on UAP matters in June 2021. When Gerb contacted Brad Sorenson, Sorenson was hostile from the start and told him: "Mark McCandlish was a stupid dramatic selfish fool who died because he could not keep his mouth shut. Learn from this."

Other people in the ARV and free-energy research community have also died in strange circumstances. Filmmaker [[James Allen]], for example, fell ill and died about a month before his documentary on McCandlish was due to be released; McCandlish said pathology found radioisotopes in his system. Gerb sets McCandlish's death beside these cases while stressing that the pattern is not proven.

## Related Figures and Context

In his video on McCandlish, Gerb also sets the Flux Liner beside other alleged ARV-related accounts: [[Edgar Fouché]]'s claims about the triangular [[TR-3B]], [[Gary McKinnon]]'s alleged discovery of non-terrestrial USAF personnel records, and his own work on an alleged reverse-engineering group of the [[412th Test Wing]] at Edwards and Nellis Air Force Bases. Gerb counts some 10 to 15 alleged craft built with non-human intelligence technology to some extent, among them the Flux Liner, the [[Aurora Program|Aurora]] and the TR-3B. In later videos he notes that the cradled control sphere McCandlish and Sorenson described recurs in other triangle accounts, and that McCandlish reportedly saw a TR-3B-type triangle at a Lockheed Martin facility.
