Ryan S. Wood is an American author and UAP researcher. With his father [[Robert Wood]], a former McDonnell Douglas aerospace engineer, he has worked extensively on authenticating [[Majestic 12]] documents and documenting UFO crash retrieval cases. He wrote *Magic Eyes Only*, a comprehensive catalog of alleged UFO crash retrieval incidents covering cases from 1933 forward. He has also spent decades on forensic analysis of approximately 3,500 pages of alleged Majestic 12 documents, leaked between 1984-1999 from seven different sources.

## MJ-12 Document Authentication Work

Wood has developed a weighted system for rating the authenticity of Majestic documents. It draws on several kinds of evidence, including typography and printing analysis, period-accurate terminology, personnel verification, witness testimony, cross-document corroboration, and forensic ink/paper testing. On the forensic evidence, he gives his highest confidence in authenticity to documents including the [[Special Operations Manual (SOM 1-01)]], the [[Eisenhower Briefing Document]], and [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] reports.

### Document Provenance Investigation

To trace the document control initials "EWL" (MJ-01) and "JRT" (MJ-04) on the control pages of SOM 1-01, Wood consulted Albuquerque phone books for 1954-1957 and cross-referenced them with National Personnel Record Center files. He found that Captain Lewis (EWL) and people matching "JRT" lived on Perimeter Road inside [[Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico|Kirtland Air Force Base]] at the relevant time. After hiring private investigators, Wood visited one person matching "JRT" in person. That person agreed to meet but denied being the one named in the document, despite matching the location and timeline.

### Sources and Leakers

Wood identified the primary source of the leaked documents as "Thomas Cantwheel" (a pseudonym). Near death from cancer, Cantwheel wrote to researcher [[Timothy Cooper]] describing a career as a Counter Intelligence Corps officer in the [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] from 1942-1958. Working with Reddit researcher "Harry is White Hot", Wood identified a likely candidate, Boris Dimitri Tasarov. Tasarov was a CIC officer (1942-1945) stationed at Camp Ritchie MD who later worked for the CIA (1956-1968). He served at the Mexico City CIA station in 1963, transcribing surveillance of Soviet compounds including Lee Harvey Oswald traffic, and lived in San Leandro CA when Cooper conducted his investigation.

## Response to Alexander Disinformation Theory

Wood strongly disputes [[Colonel John B. Alexander]]'s claim that [[SOM 1-01]] was created in 1954 as Soviet disinformation. He argues that exposing detailed crash retrieval infrastructure, facility locations, entity descriptions, and protocols merely to deceive the Soviets makes no sense as psychological warfare. If SOM 1-01 were disinformation aimed at the Soviets, he argues, it would not be part of a 19-year leak campaign of 3,500 pages from seven different sources using multiple delivery methods. He notes that the Soviets already knew UAPs were real from their own recovery operations and had penetrated the Manhattan Project within a year, so they would certainly have intelligence on U.S. UAP activities.

Wood states: "I have no credible evidence that any document listed on [Majesticdocuments.com] is fake" and has found "no credible objections" that withstand forensic scrutiny. He maintains that some documents remain in his "neutral basket" until there is further evidence, but he believes the core documents show overwhelming signs of authenticity.

## UFO DX Research Tool

Wood created UFO DX (UFO-dx.com), a ChatGPT tool trained on 500-800 UFO books, so that researchers can query the accumulated knowledge of ufology. Users can ask natural language questions on any topic in ufology, and the tool bases its answers on Wood's curated library of research materials.

## Coyame Case Assessment

In *Magic Eyes Only*, Wood rates the [[1974 Coyame, Mexico UFO Crash Retrieval]] case as medium to high authenticity based on the quality of the [[Deneb Report]], its use of correct military terminology, operational detail consistent with known crash retrieval protocols, and the distinction between hard evidence and speculation.
