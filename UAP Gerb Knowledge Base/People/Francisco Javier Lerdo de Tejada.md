Francisco Javier Lerdo de Tejada was the captain of [[TAE (Trabajos Aéreos y Enlaces)|TAE]] flight JK 297, the Super Caravelle he diverted to [[Manises Airport, Valencia, Spain|Manises Airport]] in Valencia on the night of 11 November 1979 after two red lights closed on his aircraft over the Mediterranean. The landing, the heart of the [[Manises UFO Incident]], is generally described as the first time a commercial airliner was grounded because of a UFO. In Gerb's video the transcript renders his name "Javier Leo de Taha".

## The Manises incident

Lerdo de Tejada was flying 109 passengers from [[Salzburg, Austria|Salzburg]] to [[Las Palmas, Spain|Las Palmas]] and had left [[Mallorca, Spain|Mallorca]] after a refuelling stop at about 22:47. His cockpit crew were co-pilot [[José Ramón Zuazu]] and flight mechanic [[Francisco Javier Rodriguez]]. At about 23:05, cruising at some 23,000 feet, Rodriguez pointed out two powerful red lights ahead and to the left. [[Barcelona Air Traffic Control]] told the crew that no other aircraft were on or near their route. As the lights drew closer the crew could make out two strong light sources with no visible solid body.

When Lerdo de Tejada changed altitude to avoid a collision, the lights, by the crew's account, mirrored the new trajectory and stayed between half a mile and five miles from the aircraft. Reasoning that flying on beside an unidentified object broke every rule of air safety, and that he had no way to take evasive action if it came to a collision, he abandoned the route and landed at Manises. His decision set off the rest of the night: radar echoes and lights seen from the ground at Manises, and the scramble of a Mirage F1 flown by [[Fernando Cámara]] from [[Los Llanos Airbase, Spain|Los Llanos]].

## The "anxiety attack" explanation

Among the explanations Gerb reviews is that the red lights were the flare stacks of the Escombreras refinery near Cartagena and that the captain landed in the grip of an anxiety attack. Gerb answers that stationary lights cannot close on an aircraft and hold station through its manoeuvres, and that the co-pilot and flight mechanic, who backed the landing, would have had to share the attack.
