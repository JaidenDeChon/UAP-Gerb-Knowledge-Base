Carl Howe is the name Gerb gives, as "Mr. Carl Howe", for the source quoted in the well-known [[Hottel Memo]] of March 22, 1950, for the claim that three flying saucers recovered in New Mexico had come down there because high-powered radar interfered with them. In the memo as the FBI released it, the name is redacted ("According to Mr. [redacted] informant"), and Gerb does not say where the name Howe comes from. Who the source was, what qualified him and how far he can be trusted are all uncertain. Nothing in declassified records has been found to corroborate his claims independently, and it is unclear whether he was a government official, a military officer, a civilian contractor or a private citizen.

## The Hottel Memo Reference

In the March 22, 1950 memo from FBI Special Agent in Charge [[Guy Hottel]] to FBI Director [[J. Edgar Hoover]], Hottel passed on information that a redacted source had furnished to a Special Agent whose name is also redacted. In it, an Air Force investigator stated that three circular flying saucers about 50 feet in diameter had been recovered in New Mexico, each carrying three bodies of human shape about three feet tall, dressed in metallic cloth. Gerb guesses the investigator was from the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center]]. The memo then gives a separate claim, "According to Mr. [redacted] informant": the saucers were found in New Mexico because the government had a very high-powered radar set-up there, and it was believed the radar interfered with their controlling mechanism. This is the claim Gerb attributes to Howe.

In the memo the recovery details are the Air Force investigator's, only the radar explanation is credited to the redacted "Mr." informant, and the information was furnished to a redacted Special Agent; the ATIC link is Gerb's guess.

The radar interference theory, which blames UAP crashes on electromagnetic disruption from ground-based radar, became a recurring idea in early UFO literature. It has been offered as an explanation for the 1947 Roswell crash, the alleged 1948 [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]] and other incidents.

## Identity and Credibility

Nothing about Carl Howe's life has been conclusively established. It is unknown whether he was:

- A military radar technician or engineer with knowledge of electromagnetic effects on aircraft systems.
- A scientist or contractor involved in UAP analysis.
- A civilian informant with secondhand knowledge.
- A fabricator or source of disinformation.

The memo does not say how the informant's claim reached the FBI, beyond its being furnished to a Special Agent by a source whose name is redacted, so it shows nothing about whether he had official contacts. There is no corroborating documentation and no record of a follow-up investigation, which raises questions about how reliable his account is. The Hottel Memo itself does not judge whether the source is credible: it ends, "No further evaluation was attempted by SA [redacted] concerning the above."

## Radar Interference Theory

Howe was not alone in reportedly claiming that high-powered radar caused UAP crashes by disrupting their controls. Similar claims appeared in early UFO literature, including [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 book *Behind the Flying Saucers*, and in accounts of the Roswell and Aztec crashes. Radar installations at [[White Sands Missile Range]], [[Holloman Air Force Base]] and other New Mexico facilities were close by, which made the theory seem plausible on the surface. No technical analysis confirming that radar can cause UAP crashes has been publicly released.

## Assessment

Little is known about Carl Howe, or even whether that is the name behind the memo's redaction, yet the claim Gerb credits to him went into one of the most widely cited FBI documents in UAP research. Until the source's identity or expertise is independently verified, the claim must be treated as unsubstantiated. Still, it reached J. Edgar Hoover's office, which shows that the Washington Field Office thought it worth passing on.
