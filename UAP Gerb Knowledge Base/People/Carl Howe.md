Carl Howe (called "Mr. Carl Howe" in FBI records) is the person named in the well-known [[Hottel Memo]] of March 22, 1950, as the source of the claim that three flying saucers recovered in New Mexico had crashed because high-powered radar installations interfered with them. Who Howe was, what qualified him and how far he can be trusted are all uncertain. Nothing in declassified records has been found to corroborate his claims independently, and it is unclear whether he was a government official, a military officer, a civilian contractor or a private citizen.

## The Hottel Memo Reference

In the March 22, 1950 memo from FBI Special Agent [[Guy Hottel]] to FBI Director [[J. Edgar Hoover]], Hottel passed on information from an Air Force investigator, believed to be from the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center]]. The investigator said that according to "Mr. Carl Howe," three circular flying saucers about 50 feet in diameter had been recovered in New Mexico, each carrying three-foot-tall humanoid figures in metallic suits. Howe allegedly claimed that the discs crashed because high-powered radar in the region interfered with their controls.

The radar interference theory, which blames UAP crashes on electromagnetic disruption from ground-based radar, became a recurring idea in early UFO literature. It has been offered as an explanation for the 1947 Roswell crash, the alleged 1948 [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]] and other incidents.

## Identity and Credibility

Nothing about Carl Howe's life has been conclusively established. It is unknown whether he was:

- A military radar technician or engineer with knowledge of electromagnetic effects on aircraft systems.
- A scientist or contractor involved in UAP analysis.
- A civilian informant with secondhand knowledge.
- A fabricator or source of disinformation.

That Howe's claims reached the FBI through an Air Force investigator suggests he had some official contact. There is no corroborating documentation and no record of a follow-up investigation, however, which raises questions about how reliable his account is. The Hottel Memo itself does not judge whether Howe is credible; it only passes his claims on as information received.

## Radar Interference Theory

Howe was not alone in reportedly claiming that high-powered radar caused UAP crashes by disrupting their controls. Similar claims appeared in early UFO literature, including [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 book *Behind the Flying Saucers*, and in accounts of the Roswell and Aztec crashes. Radar installations at [[White Sands Missile Range]], [[Holloman Air Force Base]] and other New Mexico facilities were close by, which made the theory seem plausible on the surface. No technical analysis confirming that radar can cause UAP crashes has been publicly released.

## Assessment

Little is known about Carl Howe, yet his claims, passed on secondhand through official channels, went into one of the most widely cited FBI documents in UAP research. Until his identity or expertise is independently verified, his statements must be treated as unsubstantiated. Still, they reached the desk of J. Edgar Hoover, which shows that an Air Force investigator took them seriously enough to send them to the FBI.
