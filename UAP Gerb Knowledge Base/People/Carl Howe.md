Carl Howe, spelled Karl Howe by [[The Black Vault]], was a special investigator with the Washington, D.C. Metropolitan Police who gave the FBI the information in the well-known [[Hottel Memo]] of March 22, 1950. His name is blacked out in the copy in the FBI's online vault, under FOIA exemption b7D, and was only released in an unredacted copy in August 2022. Howe was not the source of the flying-saucer story himself: he was passing on what an unnamed informant of his had told him, and the informant has never been identified.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said FBI records name "Mr. Carl Howe" as the source of the radar claim and that nobody knew whether he was an official, an officer or a private citizen. The vault copy (page 2) redacts the name under exemption b7D; the 2022 unredacted release and Gerb's Aztec video identify him as a police special investigator relaying his own informant, and the memo credits the radar claim to that informant.

## The Hottel Memo Reference

The March 22, 1950 memo from FBI Special Agent in Charge [[Guy Hottel]] to FBI Director [[J. Edgar Hoover]] opens: "The following information was furnished to SA [redacted] by [redacted]." The unredacted release names the special agent as Robert H. Kurtzman and the man who furnished the information as Howe. What follows is his informant's account: an "investigator for the Air Forces" had said that three circular flying saucers about 50 feet in diameter had been recovered in New Mexico, each carrying three bodies of human shape about three feet tall, dressed in metallic cloth. Gerb guesses the investigator was from the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center]]. The memo credits the explanation that the saucers came down because high-powered government radar in the area interfered with their "controling mechanism" to "Mr. [redacted] informant," that is, to Howe's informant rather than to Howe, though in his Sarbacher video Gerb credits it to "Mr. Carl Howe" himself. Gerb summarises the chain in his Aztec video: the information was "relayed to special agent RH Curtsman by police special investigator Carl How and his unnamed informant."

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions credited the whole account of the recovered saucers, including the radar claim, to "Mr. Carl Howe" and said the information came to Hottel from the Air Force investigator. In the memo the investigator's story and the radar explanation both come from Howe's informant, Howe passed them to an FBI special agent rather than to Hottel, and the ATIC link is Gerb's guess.

The radar interference theory, which blames UAP crashes on electromagnetic disruption from ground-based radar, became a recurring idea in early UFO literature. It has been offered as an explanation for the 1947 Roswell crash, the alleged 1948 [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]] and other incidents.

## Identity and Credibility

Beyond his police post, little about Howe has been published. The open question is his informant: The Black Vault notes that where the information came from before it reached the police remains unknown. Before the unredacted release, many researchers assumed the source was [[Silas Newton]], who was promoting the Aztec story in the same weeks, and Gerb mentions that assumption in his Aztec video.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions listed guesses about who Howe was (radar technician, contractor, civilian informant, fabricator) and said his claims reached the FBI through an Air Force investigator. The 2022 release identifies him as a police special investigator who brought his informant's account to the FBI directly.

The memo does not say how Howe's informant came by the story, so it shows nothing about whether the informant had official contacts. There is no corroborating documentation and no record of a follow-up investigation, which raises questions about how reliable the account is. The memo itself does not judge whether the source is credible: it ends, "No further evaluation was attempted by SA [redacted] concerning the above."

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said Howe's claims reached the FBI through an Air Force investigator, suggesting official contact, and that the memo passes them on without judging his credibility. The claims in the memo are his informant's, the memo does not tie the radar claim to the investigator, and this paragraph now quotes what the memo says about evaluation.

## Radar Interference Theory

Howe's informant was not alone in claiming that high-powered radar caused UAP crashes by disrupting their controls. Similar claims appeared in early UFO literature, including [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 book *Behind the Flying Saucers*, and in accounts of the Roswell and Aztec crashes. Radar installations at [[White Sands Missile Range]], [[Holloman Air Force Base]] and other New Mexico facilities were close by, which made the theory seem plausible on the surface. No technical analysis confirming that radar can cause UAP crashes has been publicly released.

> [!edit] Attribution clarified 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions credited the radar claim to Howe. The memo credits it to "Mr. [redacted] informant", Howe's informant.

## Assessment

Little is known about Howe, yet the account he brought to the FBI went into one of the most widely cited FBI documents in UAP research. Because it came from an unnamed informant quoting an unnamed Air Force investigator, and neither source's identity or expertise has been independently verified, it must be treated as unsubstantiated. Still, it reached J. Edgar Hoover's office, which shows that a police investigator and the FBI's Washington Field Office took it seriously enough to put it on paper and pass it on.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions treated the claims as Howe's own and said an Air Force investigator sent them to the FBI. Howe relayed an informant's account to an agent of the Washington Field Office, which forwarded it; no Air Force investigator contacted the FBI.
