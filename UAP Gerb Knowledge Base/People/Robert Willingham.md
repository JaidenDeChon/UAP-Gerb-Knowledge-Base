Colonel Robert Burton Willingham was a United States Air Force Reserve officer and civilian pilot who claimed to have witnessed a UAP crash near [[Langtry, Texas]] in spring 1955 while escorting bombers from [[Carswell Air Force Base]] on a Cold War simulation mission. He is the central witness in one of two distinct crash retrieval cases that UFO researchers call the "Del Rio, Texas" incidents. His account is documented mainly in the 2008 book *The Other Roswell: UFO Crash on the Texas Border* by [[Noe Torres]] and [[Ruben Uriarte]], which draws on extensive personal interviews with Willingham near the end of his life. He died in 2015.

## Military Service

Willingham served in World War II and the Korean War, and later as an Air Force Reserve pilot. He claims to have received a Purple Heart for a mortar wound to his leg and head sustained in a Korean War foxhole. The National Personnel Records Service in St. Louis, Missouri, could not retrieve his service record. However, a catastrophic fire at that facility on July 12, 1973 destroyed most records for personnel who served between 1947 and 1963, and the video notes that this weakens the missing record as disqualifying evidence. Co-author [[Noe Torres]] claims to hold over 50 documents that corroborate Willingham's service. Researcher [[Kevin Randle]], in his book *Crash: When UFOs Fall from the Sky*, is among those who have questioned the service record.

## The 1955 Sighting

On a spring day in 1955, Willingham was flying an F-86 Saber on a Cold War simulation mission from [[Carswell Air Force Base]], escorting B-47 and B-52 bombers (mission call sign: Willie Eddie; Willingham's serial: CD1195). The squadron received radio intelligence from Distant Early Warning (DEW) radar installations in Canada that fast-moving unidentified signatures were tracking south toward Texas. Willingham spotted a large, extremely bright object with a surface he compared to "magnesium steel." It was traveling at an estimated 2,000 mph, about four times the F-86's top speed of 685 mph. Pilot George Smithson and other squadron members confirmed the sighting in a debrief afterward. The object made a sharp 90-degree turn southwest toward the Mexican border, then gave off sparks and tilted to a 45-degree angle before falling from view, which suggested the maneuver had damaged its structure.

## The Crash Site Visit

Willingham obtained permission from Denver flight control to investigate the estimated crash site. From an altitude of about 800 feet he saw the wreckage of a disc-shaped craft just south of the Rio Grande, on the Mexican side of the border near [[Langtry, Texas]]. The craft had skidded roughly 300 feet and broken into about three sections. He returned to base, then flew privately to the site in a 1947 Aeronca Champion with [[Jack Perkins]], a Pennsylvania electronics engineer and Civil Air Patrol colleague.

At the site, Mexican soldiers had formed a perimeter but were making no effort to recover the craft. An officer named Lieutenant Martinez noticed Willingham's USAF fatigues and gave him limited access, while keeping Perkins out, saying that "the American Air Force will be here very soon to clean this all up." Willingham described the craft's main body as a flattened disc 20–25 feet in diameter. A dome-shaped section 12–15 feet long lay 50 feet from the main body, and the debris ranged from small fragments to chunks the size of a sofa chair.

Before leaving, Willingham pocketed a small piece of shiny metal. Back at base, he gave oral and written reports to Reserve Commander Colonel Miller, and a USAF intelligence general threatened him not to discuss what he saw "down on the border."

## The Anomalous Material

The fragment Willingham took was described as rigid, extremely light and grayish-silver, with small holes in a honeycomb pattern along one side. He could not cut or deform it with a torch. He sent it to a USMC Metallurgy Lab in Hagerstown, Maryland. When he later tried to follow up, the lab denied any record of the fragment, the tests or the major who had received it.

## The Bodies Contradiction

The most significant problem for Willingham's credibility is that his written testimony directly contradicts his later statements to the media. *The Other Roswell* quotes him specifically: "I never saw bodies. I was not able to get close enough, and I don't know what happened after I left." However, in a July 24, 2012 interview with [[Jeff Rense]], Willingham described sneaking close to the dome section and seeing three to four non-human bodies. He said they were small, with large heads, broomstick-thin arms and no clothing, and "in a lot of different pieces." Author [[Noe Torres]] also repeated the bodies claim in a 2023 presentation. Willingham generally blamed inconsistencies in his story on his Korean War head injury. He used it to explain why the aircraft type changed (an F-94 in the 1977 affidavit, an F-86 in the 2008 book) and why the year of the event changed (1948 in the 1967 article, 1955 in the book). That explanation is difficult to accept for a detail as specific as whether he saw bodies.

## Public Disclosure History

Willingham first went public in 1965, in a weekly newspaper in Mechanicsburg, Pennsylvania, that asked Civil Air Patrol pilots about their UFO encounters (Gerb later calls it the 1967 article). Twelve years later, after researchers found clippings of the article in the files of the [[National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena]] (NICAP), [[Todd Zechel]] tracked Willingham down and obtained a signed affidavit from him in 1977. Gerb documents at length the inconsistencies between this 1967 article, the 1977 affidavit and the 2008 book *The Other Roswell*.

## Independent Corroboration

Former USAF member [[William Drager]], who had served as a Spanish interpreter, was hired to help with a Japanese documentary investigating a UFO crash near the Mexican border that involved a colonel. Drager contacted a Mexican general who commanded the border region. In private the general acknowledged knowing about the incident, but on camera during filming he denied it. Drager provided a formal affidavit describing the exchange.

## Assessment

Gerb rates Willingham's account as fascinating but significantly compromised in credibility. He cites the changing aircraft type, the shifting year and, most critically, the direct contradiction about the bodies. He compares the case with the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval|1953 Kingman crash retrieval]], where witness [[Arthur Stansel Jr.]] gave a single affidavit whose details do not change within it.
