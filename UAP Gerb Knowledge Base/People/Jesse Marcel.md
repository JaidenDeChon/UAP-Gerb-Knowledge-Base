Major Jesse Antoine Marcel Sr. (1907–1986) was the intelligence officer of the [[509th Bomb Group]] at [[Roswell Army Airfield]], New Mexico, and the first military officer sent to investigate the debris field from the alleged [[Roswell Crash]] of July 1947. Within a day the Army Air Forces recast the find as a weather balloon, and Marcel was photographed with substitute material for the press. In 1978 he told the UFO researcher [[Stanton Friedman]] that he believed the debris was not of this Earth and that he had been ordered to take part in a cover-up. His disclosure reignited public interest in the Roswell incident and launched the modern era of crash retrieval research. In Gerb's videos Marcel is the starting point of the paper trail on crash retrievals, and his description of the debris recurs as the standard against which other material is compared.

## Background

Marcel was born on 27 May 1907 in Louisiana. As an Army Air Forces intelligence officer he helped administer Operation Crossroads, the 1946 atomic bomb tests at Bikini Atoll, and in 1947 he was the air intelligence officer of the 509th, the group that had dropped the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. He later became a lieutenant colonel in the Reserve. After leaving the service he worked as a television repairman in Houma, Louisiana, and he died in June 1986. Sources differ on whether he died on the 23rd or the 24th.

## Roswell Investigation

In early July 1947, rancher W.W. "[[Mac Brazel]]" took a box of debris to the local sheriff. He had found a wreckage field on the [[Foster Ranch]], about 75 miles north of Roswell, and Marcel was dispatched to observe and recover it. Gerb stresses that this debris field was not the site where a craft and bodies were allegedly recovered: a second location just north of Roswell was kept far more secret. Marcel recovered materials he described as unlike anything he had encountered in his military career, including an exceedingly light metallic foil that returned to its shape after being deformed, and light "I-beam" structures with markings resembling hieroglyphs. He later said he had handled all manner of aircraft wreckage during his intelligence career and was certain the Roswell material was not of earthly manufacture. On the way back to the base he loaded wreckage into a military jeep and showed pieces to his family, including his son [[Jesse Marcel Jr.]] Both later described the markings. Gerb notes that the marked I-beam has since been claimed by many other witnesses, and he sets it beside the "strange markings" [[Walter Haut]] described and the "symbols of instruction" [[Steven Lovekin]] said he saw on a piece of 1947 New Mexico material. In his [[1965 Kecksburg, Pennsylvania Crash Retrieval|Kecksburg]] video, too, the hieroglyph-like writing reported there reminds Gerb of the writing Marcel described on the Roswell debris.

On 8 July, Colonel [[William Blanchard]] had Haut announce that the base had recovered a flying saucer from a ranch north-west of Roswell and that Marcel would fly the material to the office of [[General Ramey|General Roger Ramey]] at Fort Worth. The same day Ramey ordered a retraction and recast the find as a weather balloon. Marcel was photographed in Ramey's office with aluminum foil and balsa wood rather than the material he had recovered. According to Haut's 2002 affidavit, Marcel told him he had taken pieces of the wreckage to Ramey's office and, returning from a map room, found that balsa wood and foil from a weather balloon and radar kite had been put in their place. Colonel [[Thomas J. DuBose]] later swore that the balloon was a cover story. Afterwards Blanchard went on leave, Brazel dropped from view, Marcel moved to Fort Worth and ordered his family not to talk about the wreckage, and the story stayed buried for decades.

## 1978 Disclosure

In 1978 [[Stanton Friedman]] interviewed Marcel, who broke his silence about the Roswell incident for the first time. Marcel said he believed the recovered material was not of this Earth, and admitted he had been ordered to take part in the weather-balloon cover-up. In 1980 he also admitted his part privately to Walter Haut, who stayed silent until his 1993 affidavit and recorded there his disbelief that Blanchard or Marcel could have mistaken the wreckage for a weather balloon. Marcel's disclosure, along with those of Brazel's son Bill and others, spurred the modern Roswell research movement and the wave of congressional and media attention that followed through the 1990s.

Gerb uses the date of Marcel's admission as a fixed point. Because [[Arthur Stansel Jr.]]'s account of the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval|1953 Kingman crash]] came out in 1973, five years before Marcel spoke, Gerb argues that it cannot have been riding on Roswell's coat-tails. Answering [[Sean Kirkpatrick]]'s claim that the reverse-engineering story was a conspiracy spun up around 2008, Gerb points to a paper trail that begins in the 1940s with Marcel being made to call the Roswell wreckage a weather balloon. In his history of the [[UFO Stigma|UFO stigma]] he notes that the Ramey who briefed the press on the 1952 Washington sightings was the same general who had ordered Marcel to pose with fake wreckage.

## Suppression of Information

Marcel is cited as an example of the broader government pattern of silencing military witnesses to UAP-related events. Marcel and other Roswell witnesses were kept from speaking freely about what they saw by men in military fatigues, in Gerb's words, rather than by the suit-clad [[Men in Black]] of UFO folklore. The suppression parallels what [[Robert Jacobs]] and others experienced.

## Connection to Battelle Nitinol Research

Marcel described the anomalous debris as exceedingly light metallic foil that returned to its original shape after being deformed. That description corresponds to the material properties of [[Nitinol (Nickel-Titanium Alloy)]], a shape-memory alloy not officially discovered until 1961. In 1949 [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] contracted [[Battelle Memorial Institute]] to analyze a nickel-titanium shape-memory alloy. Gerb argues that Marcel's description of the Roswell debris, combined with the material's reported transport to Wright-Patterson and Battelle's 1949 contract research on the same type of alloy, forms a traceable chain from crash retrieval to private-sector material analysis.

The alleged [[MJ-12 Documents|Majestic 12 documents]] also echo Marcel. Gerb notes that the *Special Operations Manual* describes crash materials as extremely strong for their weight, resembling aluminum foil or magnesium sheeting and bearing inscriptions, "identical to the claims" of Marcel, and that the IPU report places "the debris field seen by Brazel and Marcel" at the first of two Roswell sites.
