Jeff Bingaman was a United States Senator from New Mexico from 1983 to 2013. In the early 1990s he and fellow New Mexico Senator [[Pete Domenici]] made critical inquiries to the United States Air Force about [[Project Moon Dust]]. The Air Force at first denied that the program existed, but their inquiries forced it to acknowledge the program.

## 1992 Moon Dust Inquiry

In 1992, Senators Bingaman and Domenici made formal inquiries to the Air Force about Project Moon Dust, the classified crash retrieval program that had operated since at least 1961. The Air Force responded that "there is no Project Moon Dust. These missions have never existed."

The Senators challenged this flat denial with documents proving that Moon Dust existed. Faced with records obtained through Freedom of Information Act requests, the Air Force was forced to revise its statement and acknowledge that Moon Dust existed and what its function was with regard to UFOs.

The exchange is one of the rare cases in which Congressional oversight got past Air Force denials and forced the acknowledgment of a classified UFO-related program.

## Significance of New Mexico Connection

New Mexico has a central role in UFO history, which makes Bingaman's interest in Moon Dust particularly notable:

- [[Roswell]], New Mexico, site of the famous 1947 UFO crash
- Multiple alleged crash retrieval sites within New Mexico
- Dulce Base and other rumored underground facilities
- [[White Sands Missile Range]], [[Sandia National Laboratories]], and other sensitive installations with alleged UAP connections

As a senator for New Mexico, Bingaman would have been aware of his constituents' concerns and of the historical incidents that made the state a focal point for UAP research and alleged government programs.

## Congressional Oversight Limitations

Bingaman's inquiry forced the Air Force to acknowledge Moon Dust, but in the end it also showed the limits of Congressional oversight. When Senator Domenici requested specific Moon Dust documents in 1994, the Air Force claimed that the program no longer existed and that files had been destroyed. That claim was later proven false when Moon Dust documents continued to surface through FOIA requests.
