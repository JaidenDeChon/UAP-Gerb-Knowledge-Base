Senator Barry Morris Goldwater was a five-term United States Senator from Arizona (1953–1965, 1969–1987), a Major General in the US Air Force Reserve, and the Republican Party's nominee for President in 1964. He chaired the Senate Intelligence Committee and later the Senate Armed Services Committee, posts that gave him access to highly classified defense and intelligence programs. Even so, by his own account he was refused entry to a room at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] rumored to hold recovered UFO materials, and General [[Curtis LeMay]] cursed him out for asking. He described the refusal in a 1975 interview and in letters to UFO researchers.

## Career

Goldwater was born in [[Phoenix, Arizona|Phoenix]] in 1909. He took a reserve commission in the Army Air Forces after the United States entered the Second World War, founded the Arizona Air National Guard in 1946, and retired in 1967 as a major general and command pilot. He was elected to the Senate in 1952, gave up his seat to run for President in 1964, and returned in 1969. He chaired the [[Senate Select Committee on Intelligence]] from 1981 to 1985 and the Senate Armed Services Committee from 1985 until he retired in January 1987. He died on 29 May 1998.

## Wright-Patterson Access Denial

Senator Goldwater made several attempts to learn what was kept in a classified area at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base known informally as the "[[Blue Room|Blue Room]]", a secure area rumored to house recovered UFO materials and possibly non-human biologics. His interest came from his military background, his friendship with senior Air Force officials, and his seats on committees with oversight of classified aerospace programs.

By his own account he was turned away each time. In a 1975 interview he described phoning General Curtis LeMay to say he knew there was a room at Wright-Patterson "where you put all this secret stuff" and to ask whether he could go in. LeMay, whom he had never seen angry, "got mad as hell," cursed him out and said, "Don't ever ask me that question again." In his Moon Dust video Gerb sums it up: Goldwater asked Wright-Patterson officials to see the room where "it" was stored and was laughed off and told never to ask again. Gerb dates the refusal differently from video to video: to the early 1960s, perhaps 1963, from Goldwater's own March 1975 letter; to 28 December 1973 in his iceberg video; and to 1975 in his analysis of a Special Forces whistleblower. Years later, in October 1981, Goldwater wrote to UFO researcher [[Lee Graham]] that he knew of no one with access to the Blue Room and that the subject had become impossible to get anything on.

Goldwater was a Major General and later chaired the Senate Intelligence Committee, and either position would normally have let him into virtually any US military installation or program. That he was still refused suggests that whatever is kept at Wright-Patterson sits in a compartment that overrides even senior Congressional and military authority. This matches the "unacknowledged special access program" structure described in more recent UAP whistleblower testimony.

Two documents in Gerb's coverage suggest the rumors and the secrecy were older than Goldwater's requests. A 27 April 1953 [[Air Force Office of Special Investigations]] record has Master Sergeant [[Ralph Brown]] relaying that Wright-Patterson held three flying saucers and the bodies of their pilots. In 1991, answering a FOIA request from [[Brian Parks]], USAF Colonel [[Eddie L. Anderson]] said material relating to a Blue Room, in this case a film, had been destroyed on 9 September 1965, which Gerb notes was close to the time of Goldwater's request.

Gerb also weighs Goldwater's account against the [[Infographic Show Whistleblower|anonymous Special Forces veteran]] who called Wright-Patterson a red herring and said "we faked some stuff going into Wright-Patterson." Gerb suggests the base may have held records of the program rather than craft.

## Connection to Project Moon Dust

Wright-Patterson Air Force Base was a main destination for materials collected under [[Project Moon Dust]], the classified Air Force program that investigated and retrieved UFOs and foreign space vehicles from 1961 through at least the mid-1990s. Several Moon Dust memos and cables were addressed to the Foreign Technology Division at Wright-Patterson, which supports the view that the base was a central point for collecting and analyzing anomalous aerospace objects. Goldwater's refusal fits that picture of Wright-Patterson as a restricted store for crash retrieval materials.

## Roswell Connection

Wright-Patterson also appears in accounts of the [[Roswell Crash|Roswell]] incident. Witnesses and researchers have alleged that debris from the 1947 crash was taken to the base for analysis and storage. Goldwater was refused access decades later, which suggests that if such materials were sent to Wright-Patterson, they were still under the strictest security classifications well into the 1970s.

## Public Statements on UFOs

Unlike many of his Congressional colleagues, Goldwater was willing to discuss UFOs and government secrecy on the record. His letters to researchers and his account of the Wright-Patterson refusal are a rare case of a senior elected official publicly acknowledging that classified UFO-related programs and materials exist beyond normal Congressional oversight. In his Majestic-12 video, Gerb plays the 1975 interview clip beside the alleged [[Special Operations Manual (SOM 1-01)]], which lists the Wright-Patterson Blue Room as the destination for supplies, provisions and deceased entities.

## Documentary Record of Goldwater's Letters

The video "UFO Whistleblowers [Vol.2]" presents four documentary sources on Goldwater's denied access, compiled from [[Leonard Stringfield]]'s personal archives:

- In a letter to Stringfield dated December 3, 1974, Goldwater says he had been agnostic toward UFOs but had tried to get into "the room at Wright-Patterson field where the information was stored" and was denied.
- In a letter to an unknown researcher dated March 28, 1975, Goldwater dates his attempt to get into the building to ten or twelve years earlier (about 1963, Gerb notes) and says the material is still classified "Above Top Secret."
- In a 1975 public interview, Goldwater said on the record: "I think the government does know. I can't back that up, but I think that at Wright-Patterson field, if you could get into certain places, you'd find out what the Air Force and the government knows about UFOs." In the same interview he described the phone call in which LeMay told him, "Don't ever ask me that question again."
- In a letter to Lee Graham dated October 19, 1981, Goldwater says he knows of no one with access to the Blue Room and is unaware of anything being relocated. He describes the subject as "impossible to get anything on" because of its classification level.
