Dr. J. Allen Hynek was an American astronomer and professor who was the scientific adviser to the United States Air Force's [[Project Blue Book]], the official UFO investigation program that ran from 1952 to 1969. He is one of the most significant figures in the history of UFO research. Hynek started as a UFO skeptic tasked with debunking sightings and became a serious researcher who concluded that some UFO cases were genuine unexplained phenomena.

## Career

Hynek was born in Chicago in 1910 and took his doctorate in astrophysics at Yerkes Observatory in 1935. He joined the physics and astronomy faculty of Ohio State University in 1936, and he was still teaching there in the spring of 1948, when the Air Force engaged him as scientific consultant to [[Project Sign]]. He stayed on as adviser through [[Project Grudge]] and [[Project Blue Book]] until Blue Book closed in 1969. In 1956 he went to the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory to work on tracking the first artificial satellites, and in 1960 he became chair of the astronomy department at Northwestern University, where he remained until he retired in 1978. In 1973 he founded the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS). He died on 27 April 1986.

Tired, as he put it, of being a "puppet of the Air Force" who "only says what the Air Force wants me to say", Hynek used the hearing to ask for an independent civilian panel. In "The Origin of the UFO Stigma", Gerb adds that Hynek himself delivered the "swamp gas" and "weather balloon" explanations on a national stage, and that after leaving Blue Book he said openly that it "was not a scientific project" and that the Air Force "did everything they could to keep it down".

## NORAD UFO Tracking Revelation

On January 6, 1967, Hynek took part in a discussion at [[Goddard Space Flight Center]] where he made a significant disclosure. He said that [[NORAD]] (North American Aerospace Defense Command) had been tracking UFOs since 1957, and that every continental United States military UFO case in the [[Project Blue Book]] files was designated as a NORAD case.

This confirmed that NORAD, the military command responsible for aerospace warning and control, was carrying out its own parallel tracking and analysis of unidentified objects in North American airspace, far more serious than Blue Book's. Project Blue Book, meanwhile, was publicly presenting UFO investigation as a largely dismissive scientific exercise. The implication was that the US military had continuous surveillance data on UFOs from its defense tracking systems, and that this data was never fully disclosed to Project Blue Book investigators or the public.

## Disclosure of Parallel Classified Programs

Hynek also said that other classified programs were studying UFOs alongside Project Blue Book. This confirmed what Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]] had disclosed on April 24, 1952: that programs parallel to the official UFO investigations were "conducting a more complete investigation." Gerb identifies one of these parallel programs as [[Project Moon Dust]], a classified Air Force collection program run under the [[USAF 1127th Field Activities Group]] from at least 1961 through the mid-1990s.

In Gerb's reading, Hynek's acknowledgment of these programs suggests he knew Project Blue Book was mainly a public-facing operation, while classified programs handled the actual UFO collection, crash retrieval and technical analysis.

## Critic of the Air Force investigations

In "The Alien and UFO Obscure Oddities Iceberg (Level 2)", Gerb calls Hynek "one of the OG whistleblowers" and gathers his public criticisms of the programs he served:

- **Project Blue Book's record.** Blue Book (1952–1969) analysed 12,618 reports and classed 701 as unexplained. Gerb sees its reputation as overshadowed by the CIA's 1953 [[Robertson Panel]] and the [[Condon Report]], which in his account both stripped UFOs of serious status.
- **"A coverup" (1972).** In a book published in May 1972, Hynek wrote that Blue Book was "a coverup" and that the UFO issue had been "glossed over". He also wrote that Blue Book was a front for a more classified investigation group, possibly one designated "AF-FON-X-SG", and that he had been told to drop certain cases and "not to pursue the matter further".
- **Rumsfeld's "need to know" (1975).** On 13 April 1975, at the White House, [[Donald Rumsfeld]] told Hynek that he did not have a need to know whether a secret UAP study existed after Blue Book.
- **The Robertson Panel's "unwritten law" (1979).** Hynek said the panel, worried about public reaction, handed down the unwritten law of the Air Force: "Don't rock the boat, play it cool, don't get the public excited." He said the Air Force deliberately debunked interesting cases as balloons or planets. Gerb sees the same pattern in the Mars-and-Jupiter explanation of the [[1986 Japan Airlines UFO Incident]].
- **Grudge and Condon.** Hynek called 1949's [[Project Grudge]] "less scientific and more of a public relations campaign", and said the Condon committee ignored key evidence.

## Evolution as a Researcher

Hynek developed the "Close Encounters" classification system (CE-I, CE-II, CE-III), set out in *The UFO Experience* (1972), which became the standard framework for categorizing UFO encounters. In 1973 he founded the Center for UFO Studies (CUFOS) and advocated for serious scientific study of the phenomenon. In "UFOs in the Private Sector - Battelle Memorial Institute", Gerb notes that Hynek raised [[Project Stork]], Battelle's separate analysis of the Air Force's UFO cases, during his attempted testimony to the 1953 [[Robertson Panel]].

## Significance

In "FASTWALKERS – UFOs Outside Earth", Gerb uses Hynek's 1967 statement about NORAD tracking to show that military aerospace surveillance has watched for unidentified objects since the 1950s. He sets it beside the later record of the [[Defense Support Program (DSP)]] early-warning satellites, which an Air Force thesis says have recorded "fast walkers" since 1972, and NORAD's refusals to release that tracking data.
