Boyd Bushman (1936–2014) was an American aerospace engineer who worked as a senior specialist at [[Lockheed Martin]] in Department 60-8, the Special Programs Division, with Top Secret/SCI/SAP clearances. He is known mainly for a video interview released in 2014, the year he died, often called his deathbed testimony. In it he claimed that debris had been recovered from crashes in New Mexico and that Lockheed had worked on anti-gravity, and he showed photographs he said were of alien bodies. An online forum thread had earlier claimed that the creature in those photographs was a toy figurine.

## Career

Bushman was born in Globe, Arizona, in 1936. His career in the defense industry ran for more than forty years, with Hughes Aircraft, General Dynamics, Texas Instruments and finally Lockheed Martin, and his obituary credits him with work on the Stinger missile and the F-16. He retired in 2000 with 28 patents in his name. He died in Tucson, Arizona, on 7 August 2014.

Gerb calls him "a controversial but very verifiable figure": a senior specialist cleared at Top Secret/SCI/SAP who served in Lockheed's Department 60-8, the Special Programs Division. Among his patents, Gerb singles out one filed in 1997 for a device that creates a magnetic monopole beam that emits pulses, levitates, stops electronics and separates materials. Magnetic monopoles (isolated north or south poles of a magnet) have not been shown to exist.

## Deathbed Claims

Bushman's claims of working on UAP research and development for Lockheed were released in 2014 and, in Gerb's words, "have become stuff of legend for UFO researchers". In the video Bushman says:

- Debris and materials were recovered from crash recoveries, plural, in New Mexico: likely the [[Roswell Crash|Roswell crash]] and perhaps the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval|Kingman, Arizona crash]].
- Lockheed carried out work on anti-gravity, which matches what other Lockheed-connected sources allege about the company's black programs.
- His work on UAP craft concluded that they contain three key elements: thoride, germanium and palladium. Gerb notes that these could make up a thermoelectric or photovoltaic generator with a semiconductor and capacitor: pure germanium was the first metallic material to become a superconductor in the presence of an electromagnetic field, palladium is crucial to capacitors, and thoride alloys have thermoelectric or photovoltaic properties. In Gerb's view this materials science lends some credence to the claim.
- He also showed photographs of what he said were alien bodies: small grey creatures.

## FBI Investigation (1999)

In 1999 Bushman came under FBI investigation for suspicious foreign contact. Lockheed had become concerned that he might share sensitive information with what appear to have been contacts in Prague. It is not known whether all of that contact concerned UAP, but Gerb's reading is that Bushman was contacting people in Prague to learn more about anti-gravity. The outcome of the investigation is not publicly documented.

## The Alien Photographs

The difficulty with Bushman's account is the photographs. A thread on the Above Top Secret forum claimed in 2008 that the small grey creature in them was a Kmart Halloween decoration from the late 1990s. Gerb could find no verifiable evidence of the toy's listing, but calls it "not a great look" for Bushman.

## Credibility Assessment

Of the three Lockheed-connected insiders Gerb assesses directly, he ranks Bushman second for credibility, after [[Bernard Haisch]] and ahead of [[Don Phillips]]. Bushman's verified employment and clearance, and the materials science behind the composition he described, count in his favor. The toy alien counts against him, and Gerb says more analysis needs to be done there. In his survey of UFO whistleblowers, Gerb places Bushman with [[Bob Lazar]], [[Mark McCandlish]] and [[Bill Uhouse]], whistleblowers "with astounding claims persisting throughout the decades but with no real legal testimony or evidence to back them up".
