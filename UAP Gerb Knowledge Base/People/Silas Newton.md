**Silas M. Newton** (died 1972) was a Denver-based oil entrepreneur and geophysicist whose disclosure of the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery|1948 Aztec crash]] made him one of the most controversial figures in UFO history. Newton was the primary source for [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 bestseller *Behind the Flying Saucers*, which first brought the Aztec case to public attention.

## Career

Gerb gives Newton's date of birth as 19 July 1897; the FBI's published file on him gives his birth year as 1887. Newton earned a bachelor's degree from Baylor University, studied at Yale, and did postgraduate work at the University of Berlin. He became successful in the oil industry through his background as a geophysicist specializing in petroleum detection.

An FBI report of February 1941 described him as a man of good character with a very substantial income. Through his professional contacts Newton came to know [[Leo GeBauer]] and a group of scientists referred to collectively as "Dr. G," from whom he reportedly received information about the Aztec crash and prototypes of oil-finding devices later called "doodlebugs."

## Role in the Aztec Disclosure

On March 8, 1950, Newton lectured under the pseudonym "Scientist X" at the University of Denver, discussing the Aztec flying saucer crash. He stepped in for the original speaker, probably one of the "Dr. G" scientists, and described a crash that had occurred two years earlier within a 500-mile radius of Denver.

The day after the lecture, [[Air Force Office of Special Investigations|AFOSI]] visited Denver radio station KMYR looking for George Koehler, who had recorded the event. Koehler was interrogated about Newton, flying saucers, and cube-like radios allegedly retrieved from the crash.

Newton then gave [[Frank Scully]] detailed information for *Behind the Flying Saucers*, including claims that he had seen photographs of the craft and handled equipment retrieved from it. Gerb says the items in Newton's personal possession were only replicas, a sensible precaution given the official interest in the case, and that Newton and Scully had probably seen the real collection.

## Government Attention

The lecture drew official interest almost at once. According to Gerb's 2025 video, [[Air Force Office of Special Investigations|AFOSI]] questioned [[George Koehler]] the day after it, and a 31 March 1950 letter to [[J. Edgar Hoover]] reported that Newton, "aka Mr. X," was said to know of a flying disc and recovered bodies. On 30 September 1950 Army CID and the FBI staged a sting in Denver to intercept the sale of photographs of the Aztec saucer. The FBI's files on Newton, 289 pages of which were released to [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]] with more than 100 pages withheld, go back to an 18 February 1941 report finding him of good character, with a substantial income and no sign of espionage. In 1951 journalist [[J. P. Cahn]] wrote to Hoover accusing Newton of preparing "a hoax or swindle of large proportions."

Cahn's September 1952 article, "The Flying Saucers and the Mysterious Little Men", made two charges against Newton that Gerb's video answers.

## The Wire Recording

A 1950 wire recording held at the University of Washington has Newton describing magnetic research in the Mojave Desert in the summer of 1949 with a colleague he called "the top magnetic scientist of the United States." That scientist told him he had passed Aztec on a drive from Denver to Phoenix and wished he could show Newton where the first flying saucer had landed. Gerb believes he found this recording, or one very like it, dated 3 March 1950. In it Newton says two telescopes tracked the ship and Air Force officials flew out of Durango within two or three hours. They found it on a rocky plateau about 16 miles east of Aztec, and about seven of "our magnetic group of scientists" were called in to examine it.

## Fraud Charges and Retaliation Theory

In October 1952, Newton and [[Leo GeBauer]] were charged with defrauding Denver businessman Herman Flader in connection with the doodlebug oil-detecting device. The trial ran from November 10 to December 29, 1953, and ended in guilty verdicts. Neither man was ever sentenced or fined, and Newton agreed to pay $18,000 in court costs.

Researchers including [[William Steinman]] and Scott and Suzanne Ramsey argue that the prosecution was targeted retaliation for Newton's role in leaking information about the Aztec crash. They point out that 32 of 33 investors in Newton's ventures were satisfied with their investments and that questionable evidence was presented at trial.

## Never-Finished Autobiography

A 1954 manuscript found in Scully's archive gives Newton's own view of the case. He described his University of Denver lecture as the focal point of persecution, writing:

> "As to my talk, it wasn't long before the finger of prosecution began pointing my way, and they've scorched my hide already, but I haven't recanted."

Newton maintained that he had not originated the Aztec story and had only repeated what credible scientists told him. He also expressed admiration for [[Wilbert B. Smith]]'s work on flying saucer magnetic propulsion.


## The Pflock Diary Claim

UFO researcher and former CIA officer Karl Pflock claimed to have obtained a diary kept by Newton, which said military officials contacted Newton in the early 1950s, told him they knew the Aztec story was bogus, and nonetheless asked him to spread it widely. Pflock never produced the diary, and after persistent questioning by Scott Ramsey he admitted his source was Newton's nephew.
