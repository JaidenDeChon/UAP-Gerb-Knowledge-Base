Enrico Fermi (1901–1954) was an Italian-American physicist widely regarded as one of the most important scientists of the 20th century. He made foundational contributions to nuclear physics and quantum mechanics, created the world's first nuclear reactor (Chicago Pile-1, in 1942) and played a central role in the [[Manhattan Project]]. In discussions of UAP and astrobiology he is best known for a question he reportedly asked during a lunchtime conversation at Los Alamos National Laboratory in 1950, which became the basis for the [[Fermi Paradox]]. Separately, Gerb's broader research on the legacy programs alleges that, according to family accounts, Fermi and not [[Guglielmo Marconi]] led fascist Italy's secret [[RS-33 (Gabinetto RS 33)]] body, which was set up to study the craft allegedly recovered near [[Magenta, Italy]] in June 1933.

## The Fermi Paradox

During a casual lunchtime conversation at Los Alamos in the summer of 1950, after a discussion about extraterrestrial life, Fermi reportedly asked: "Where is everybody?" The question sums up what became known as the [[Fermi Paradox]]. The universe has hundreds of billions of galaxies, each with hundreds of billions of stars, so extraterrestrial civilizations seem statistically very likely to exist; yet there is no confirmed contact, communication or evidence of any of them.

Fermi's colleagues at the lunch reportedly recalled that he quickly estimated the probability of life and of travel between star systems, and concluded that the absence of any evidence was itself a striking fact that needed explaining.

## Scientific Legacy

Beyond the Fermi Paradox, Fermi's scientific contributions include Fermi-Dirac statistics, which describe how subatomic particles behave, the discovery of nuclear reactions induced by slow neutrons, and the theoretical framework for beta decay. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1938. The element fermium (atomic number 100) is named in his honor.
