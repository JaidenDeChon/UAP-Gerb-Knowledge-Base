Enrico Fermi (1901–1954) was an Italian-American physicist, widely regarded as one of the most important scientists of the 20th century. He did foundational work in nuclear physics and quantum mechanics, built the world's first nuclear reactor, Chicago Pile-1, in 1942, and was one of the central figures of the [[Manhattan Project]]. In discussions of UAP and astrobiology he is best known for a question he asked over lunch at Los Alamos in the summer of 1950, "where is everybody?", which gave the [[Fermi Paradox]] its name. Gerb's coverage also ties him to fascist Italy's alleged study of a crashed craft: in his legacy-program timeline, Gerb says that according to [[Guglielmo Marconi]]'s family it was Fermi, not Marconi, who spearheaded the secret [[RS-33 (Gabinetto RS 33)|Gabinetto RS/33]] set up after the alleged 1933 recovery near [[Magenta, Italy]].

## Career

Fermi was born in Rome on 29 September 1901. He took his doctorate in physics at the University of Pisa in 1922, published the statistics now called Fermi-Dirac statistics in 1926, and in 1927 took up the new chair of theoretical physics at the University of Rome, which he held until 1938. There he developed the theory of beta decay and discovered the nuclear reactions induced by slow neutrons, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1938. That year he left fascist Italy, and in January 1939 he joined Columbia University. On 2 December 1942 his team at the University of Chicago achieved the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in Chicago Pile-1, and from 1944 to 1945 he was an associate director of the Los Alamos laboratory. After the war he returned to Chicago as a professor at the university's new Institute for Nuclear Studies. He died in Chicago on 28 November 1954. The element fermium (atomic number 100) is named in his honour.

## The Fermi Paradox

In his iceberg series, Gerb introduces the [[Fermi Paradox]] as a question the "Italian American physicist" put on a lunch break in the summer of 1950: if the universe is so big, "where is everybody?" The paradox sets the high likelihood of advanced extraterrestrial life against the lack of conclusive evidence for it. Fermi's colleagues later recalled that he quickly estimated the odds of life and of travel between the stars, and concluded that the absence of any visitors was itself a fact that needed explaining.

Gerb says plainly that he "despises" the paradox, calling it "limited" and "ignorant". In his view [[SETI]] searches one conical section of a universe 92 billion light-years across, and listens for radio because humans use radio, when another species might communicate with neutrinos: "using a magnet to find a needle in the ocean." He prefers [[Dysonian SETI (CTI)|Dysonian SETI]], which looks for technosignatures such as a Dyson sphere. "For the sake of this entry" he runs through five proposed answers: the [[Great Filter]], the [[Dark Forest Theory]], mostly aquatic civilizations, humanity being early, and humanity being late.
