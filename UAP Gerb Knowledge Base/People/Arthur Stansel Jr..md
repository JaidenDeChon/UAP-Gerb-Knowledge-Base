Arthur Glenn Stansel Jr. (1924–2006) was an American engineer. A World War II infantry veteran, he worked for twelve years as a civilian engineer for the U.S. Air Force at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]], and in 1953 was an engineer on Air Force contract with the [[Atomic Energy Commission]] for [[Operation Upshot-Knothole]]. He later spent most of his career on the Apollo program. He is the primary known witness to the alleged [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval]], and the only witness to have produced a notarized sworn affidavit describing the incident. He came forward to UFO researcher [[Raymond Fowler]] in 1973, and Fowler published his account under the pseudonym "Fritz Werner" to protect his identity, later confirming who he really was. His credentials can be checked independently against declassified DOD records, his wartime service and his obituary. That makes him one of the more credentialed figures in the literature on UAP crash retrievals.

## Career

Stansel was born in Dayton, Ohio, on 6 November 1924. He enlisted in the [[US Army]] in March 1943 and trained as a combat infantryman. He landed at Normandy on D-Day, where he was wounded for the first of three times, was wounded again at the Battle of the Bulge, and was discharged after recovering; he received three Purple Hearts. He graduated from Miami University of Ohio in 1948 with a degree in mathematics and physics, and later took a master's degree in diesel engineering at North Carolina State.

He began his engineering career at Wright Field (later Wright-Patterson Air Force Base) as an aircraft engineer and test mechanical engineer for the [[Air Materiel Command]]. From 1949 to 1960 he worked in the Air Materiel Command installation division within the Office of Special Studies, headed by Dr. [[Eric Henry Wang]], an Austrian-born scientist who, UAP researchers allege, directed early programs to exploit non-human craft. Gerb also finds him listed as an engineer in the mechanical branch of the [[Air Research and Development Command (ARDC)|Air Research and Development Command]], specialising in lighting devices and landing gear, and in AFSWP documents for Upshot-Knothole as personnel of the Special Study Section. He worked on aspects of the [[Manhattan Project]] and was a project engineer on the GAM-72 guided missile.

In early 1953 Stansel was working at [[Frenchman's Flat, Nevada]], at the [[Nevada National Security Site (Nevada Test Site)|Nevada Proving Ground]], measuring how nuclear detonations affected purpose-built structures. The work was part of [[Operation Upshot-Knothole]], a series of 11 nuclear weapons tests overseen by the Atomic Energy Commission.

In the 1960s he moved to New England to work for [[Raytheon]] in Bedford, Massachusetts, where he spent twelve years as principal engineer on Raytheon's Apollo work, followed by fifteen years as program manager for GTE Sylvania's Apollo software support. He retired from GTE Sylvania in 1987 and died in New Ipswich, New Hampshire, on 3 December 2006. (Gerb, crediting [[Richard Geldreich Jr.]], gives his Apollo service as 16 years; the figures here are the obituary's.)

## The Kingman Incident

On the evening of May 20, 1953, Stansel got a telephone call from [[Ed Doll]], physicist and project director for [[Operation Upshot-Knothole]] at the Nevada Test Site, telling him of a special assignment the next day. Stansel reported to [[Indian Springs Air Force Base]] and handed his valuables to military police. He was flown to Phoenix, then driven for approximately four hours in a bus with blacked-out windows. On the way, a U.S. Air Force Colonel briefed the personnel, saying that a "super secret Air Force vehicle" had crashed and that each specialist was to investigate only within their own area of expertise.

On arrival, two high-intensity spotlights lit up a crashed object surrounded by armed guards. Stansel described it in an affidavit sworn on June 7, 1973, with Raymond Fowler as witness, which Fowler published in *Casebook of a UFO Investigator*:

> "The object was oval and looked like two deep saucers one inverted upon the other. It was about 30 ft in diameter with convex surfaces top and bottom... It was constructed of a dull silver metal like brushed aluminum. The metal was darker where the saucered lips formed a rim around which were what looked like slots. A curved open hatch door was located on the leading end and was vertically lowered."

The craft had sunk approximately 20 inches into the desert sand. It had no landing gear and no structural damage (no dents, scratches or marks), even though Stansel, working from his engineering training, estimated that it had hit the ground at approximately 1,200 mph. A colleague who briefly entered or observed the interior reported two swivel-like seats, an oval cabin and anomalous instrument displays. A single dead occupant lay in a guarded tent nearby. It was a humanoid approximately 4 feet tall with a dark brown complexion, two eyes, nostrils, ears and a small round mouth, dressed in a silvery metallic suit and matching skull cap with no helmet or face covering.

An Air Force Colonel swore the 16 AEC specialists to secrecy and told them to record their findings in longhand only; no typed or reproduced documents were permitted. Stansel wrote his report and was taken back to Phoenix.

Separately from the 1973 affidavit, Stansel kept a diary. Its entry for 20 May 1953, which Gerb reads out, records the call from [[Edward Bushnell Doll|Dr. Doll]], the technical director for the [[Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP)]]'s programs at the Upshot-Knothole tests:

> "Spent most of the day on Frenchman's Flat surveying cubicles and supervising welding of plate girder bridge sensors which cracked after last shot. Drank brew in eve, read, got funny call from Dr. Doll about 1000. I'm going on a special job tomorrow."

(The automatic captions of one video render Doll's name as "Dah".) Gerb reads the entry as independent support for Stansel's testimony, in which Stansel names [[Edward Bushnell Doll]] as the man who coordinated personnel for the Kingman crash site and Dr. [[Eric Henry Wang]] as the scientific lead for studying the recovered disc. Gerb also plays an audio interview in which Stansel, addressed as "Fritz", describes the bus ride and a colleague who said he had seen "two alien-looking bodies" with "brown, leathery skin" in the tent.

## Researcher Investigation

[[Richard Geldreich Jr.]] did archival research, published on Medium, that confirmed Stansel's professional background: his D-Day service, his employment at Wright-Patterson, his work at the Nevada Proving Ground in May 1953 and his involvement in the Manhattan Project. Gerb found further confirmation in Air Research and Development Command and AFSWP records for Upshot-Knothole. More contested figures in UAP disclosure, such as [[Bob Lazar]], lack verifiable biographical records of this kind.

## Connection to Eric Henry Wang

Researchers are still looking into what Stansel's posting under Dr. [[Eric Henry Wang]] at the Office of Special Studies means. Researchers including [[William Steinman]] allege that Wang was brought into the exploitation of UAP material as early as 1949, after the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]], and that he led reverse-engineering efforts on recovered craft. Gerb found that Wang was the technical and scientific monitor for the Air Force program at Upshot-Knothole, the operation on which Stansel worked under him. Wang's department moved from Wright-Patterson to [[Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico|Kirtland Air Force Base]] in 1956, and he died there in 1960, according to the obituary Gerb cites. His papers were reportedly confiscated by military intelligence when he died.
