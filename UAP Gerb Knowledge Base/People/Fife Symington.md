J. Fife Symington III (born 1945) was Governor of Arizona from 1991 to 1997 and is one of the most credible and prominent witnesses to the [[Phoenix Lights]] event of March 13, 1997. A licensed pilot, Symington saw a large, silent, delta-shaped craft move over the Phoenix area that evening. He later pressed for an official explanation and publicly rejected the Air Force's account that the lights were flares, which made him a significant figure in public debate over UAP disclosure.

## Background

Symington served in the US Air Force and later graduated from Harvard University. He worked in Arizona real estate before entering politics. He served two terms as governor before resigning in 1997 after a federal fraud conviction. A federal appeals court later overturned the conviction, and President Clinton subsequently pardoned him.

## Phoenix Lights Witness

On the evening of March 13, 1997, Symington saw the Phoenix Lights himself. He described a large, structured, delta-shaped craft moving silently above Phoenix, with a "very distinctive leading edge" and "enormous lights." When he spoke about it publicly at the time, he famously staged a mock press conference in which an aide dressed as an alien was "arrested". He later said he regretted the stunt: he had meant to defuse public anxiety, but in retrospect it minimized a genuinely significant event.

Symington later asked the US Air Force for an official inquiry, and the request was denied. In a 2007 editorial for CNN he wrote about the sighting directly:

> "I'm a pilot and I know just about every machine that flies. It was bigger than anything I've ever seen. It remains a great mystery."

He said he had seen a structured, solid craft, not flares or aircraft flying in formation. He was openly dissatisfied with the Air Force's official explanation that the lights were flares dropped by A-10 Thunderbolt II aircraft during a training exercise.

## Legacy

Symington's willingness to speak publicly about his sighting, despite the political and professional risks, did a good deal for the credibility of the Phoenix Lights case. Because he was a licensed pilot and the sitting governor at the time of the event, his testimony was hard to dismiss as a misidentification or as unreliable.
