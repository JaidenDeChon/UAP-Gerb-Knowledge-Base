Brad Sorenson is an American industrial designer and inventor who claims to have seen three classified Alien Reproduction Vehicles (ARVs), known as "Flux Liners," at a restricted aerospace exhibit at [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, California in November 1988. His firsthand account is the main evidence behind the ARV disclosure story, and aerospace artist [[Mark McCandlish]] turned his detailed descriptions into widely circulated technical illustrations. On December 3, 1990, Sorenson gave a detailed interview to William B. Scott, senior engineering editor of [[Aviation Week and Space Technology]]. That interview stayed out of public circulation for decades, and it is the only known instance of Sorenson speaking on the record about his experience outside private conversations with McCandlish.

## The 1988 Norton / Plant 42 Exhibit

Sorenson was originally due to attend an air show at [[Norton Air Force Base]] with McCandlish on November 12, 1988. After McCandlish dropped out, Sorenson went with one of his own clients, described as a tall, wealthy white man with glasses and an Italian-sounding name, and a former Secretary of Defense or Under Secretary of Defense. McCandlish later identified this man with high confidence as [[Frank Carlucci]], who served as the 16th US Secretary of Defense from 1987 to 1989.

During the USAF Thunderbirds flyby at the main air show, Sorenson's client led him away from the crowd. They boarded a military version of a Boeing 727, which flew north from Norton to [[Air Force Plant 42]] in Palmdale, about 30 miles southwest of [[Edwards Air Force Base]]. As they entered the Palmdale hangar, the client told Sorenson: "There are some aircraft on exhibit here that you're not cleared to see. Just enjoy the show, walk around, listen, take a look, but don't say anything to anybody."

The exhibit was a private briefing for about 30 senior men aged 45 to 60, among them California Senator [[Alan Cranston]] and Congressman [[George E. Brown Jr.]] The inside of the hangar looked like the deck of an aircraft carrier, tightly packed with aircraft. They included the losing competitor to the B-2 stealth bomber, an advanced hovercraft with hidden thrust mechanisms, an unmanned pulse-detonation Aurora craft ("Lockheed Pulsar"), and, behind a large black curtain that divided the hangar, three disc-shaped ARV craft. The exhibit was designed to attract investor funding for black budget aerospace programs.

## Description of the ARV Craft

Sorenson described three saucer-shaped vehicles of identical design but different sizes, nicknamed "daddy, mommy, and baby bear", which hovered off the floor with no visible means of support. Their approximate diameters were 20–24 feet, 60 feet, and 120–130 feet. He found them crudely built compared with the other aerospace hardware there: "They had been built by scientists who didn't know a hell of a lot about laying up composite materials or whatever." The sphere of the crew compartment appeared to have messy internal filaments, and the whole craft was coated in thick, heavy lead paint. Panels had been removed so that the interior could be inspected.

A three-star general addressed the group, describing the craft as "Alien Reproduction Vehicles," nicknamed "Flux Liners," and said they were capable of light speed or better. The briefers told attendees that the craft used a new fuel source "that was everywhere in the universe," which allowed travel at 500 times the speed of light and a practical operating speed of 5–8 times the speed of light while withstanding 85 Gs of acceleration. The craft made no noise, had no moving parts, and emitted no exhaust.

An easel held cutaway blueprints of the craft, and a video monitor played continuous footage of an ARV hovering over a dry lake bed. The craft made three quick sideways hops, then shot vertically into the sky and vanished in about half a second.

When asked where the craft came from, the exhibit staff said: "While they tried to avoid it, they said something about 1947. They had some sort of a contact telling them that we had no right to use nuclear weapons... Back in 1947 we had just won the war, we felt pretty damned cavalier, and we attacked basically... they just tried to destroy all of their stuff and get away, and they didn't get all of it destroyed... they couldn't really make those things that they found work, so they had to build their own." Sorenson confirmed to interviewer Bill Scott that the craft were a copy of recovered hardware and not the product of a fundamental research breakthrough.

## The Aurora Craft

Sorenson also confirmed the Aurora, described as a large, flattened, black, football-shaped unmanned craft about 100–110 feet long. It used engines inside the fuselage to reach its initial altitude and then external pulse detonation engines to accelerate to Mach 6–8. Officials stated the Aurora could "take out every city over a million in the Soviet Union within one hour and twenty minutes."

## The 1990 Bill Scott Interview

On December 3, 1990, Sorenson gave an on-record interview to [[William B. Scott]] of Aviation Week and Space Technology. Gerb has described the interview as never before publicly discussed. In it, Sorenson confirmed the air show, the Aurora, the losing B-2 competitor, and the ARV craft as hovering saucers capable of faster-than-light speeds. He said he gave the interview out of patriotism and opposition to excessive military spending, and described the exhibit as seeking "appropriations of billions of dollars to develop new craft."

Bill Scott did not publish the parts of the interview about the ARVs. He later said he had difficulty believing claims about flying saucers capable of light speed that had been copied from other beings. The interview documents did, however, include a sketch of the exhibit layout that is believed to be in Sorenson's hand.

## McCandlish's Illustrations

In the week after he returned from the exhibit, Sorenson met McCandlish for lunch, sketched the ARV and described its components in detail. When McCandlish sent him a copy of the finished blueprint in 1989, Sorenson told him: "This drawing is about as accurate as you can get. Every detail, every aspect of it is right on the money. It is so accurate in every way, this has to be probably one of the most dangerous illustrations you've ever done in your life. And if you're smart, you'll tear up the original, you'll throw it away, and you'll never talk about it again."

## Post-Disclosure and Hostile Demeanor

After McCandlish publicly named Sorenson at the Disclosure Project's 2001 National Press Club event, Sorenson became hostile and went silent. When Gerb contacted him for this investigation, Sorenson's first response was: "Mark McCandlish was a stupid dramatic selfish fool who died because he could not keep his mouth shut. Learn from this." He also stated that he could not cover the topic without facing mortal peril, and advised Gerb to say goodbye to his loved ones if he kept investigating the ARV story. Asked why McCandlish had been allowed to speak openly for 20 years before his death, Sorenson replied: "I protected him until I could not."

Gerb has noted that Sorenson's threatening manner may partly be an attempt to warn off amateur investigators rather than a literal death threat. He also acknowledges that the deaths of McCandlish, James Allen, and others suggest there is real danger around ARV disclosure.
