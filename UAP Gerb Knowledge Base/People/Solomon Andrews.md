Dr. Solomon Andrews (1806–1872) was an American physician and inventor who designed and flew the Aereon, a new kind of airship that achieved controlled flight through differences in buoyancy instead of mechanical propulsion. He made successful test flights in the Aereon in 1863, before mechanically propelled airships were widely available, which makes him an early documented pioneer of lighter-than-air aviation in the United States.

## The Aereon

The Aereon (from the Greek *aer*, air) was a cigar-shaped airship filled with hydrogen. It rose and sank by changing its buoyancy, and it combined those changes in altitude with forward speed to produce lift and forward movement without an engine, in a way similar in principle to a glider. Andrews made multiple documented test flights over New Jersey from 1863 to 1866 and attempted to interest the Union Army in the craft as a reconnaissance vehicle during the Civil War.

The Aereon could not sustain powered flight, and weather and atmospheric conditions severely limited its performance. Even so, its documented test record is one of the few confirmed examples of controlled manned airship flight before mechanically driven dirigibles were widely developed in the late 19th century.

## Significance to the Mystery Airship Craze

Andrews comes up in discussions of the [[Mystery Airship Craze]] of 1896–1897 as evidence that some airship technology did exist in the United States before the wave of sightings. The Aereon could do far less than what thousands of witnesses reported during the craze, which rules it out as a direct explanation for the sightings. It does set a baseline for what American inventors of the time had demonstrably achieved.
