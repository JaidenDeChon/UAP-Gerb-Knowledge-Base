Frank C. Conahan was Director of the National Security and International Affairs Division of the US General Accounting Office (GAO). UAP researchers know him from GAO's July 24, 1986 testimony to the House Subcommittee on Oversight and Investigations, which described severe failures of document control and gaps in oversight in a classified special access program at [[Lockheed Martin]] (then Lockheed Corporation). Gerb says Conahan gave that testimony; the August 1986 report that reprints it, which Conahan signed, credits the testimony itself to Martin M. Ferber, Associate Director of the division.

> [!edit] Attribution clarified 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said Conahan testified. That comes from Gerb; the report (GAO/NSIAD-86-191, page 6) names Martin M. Ferber as the witness, and Conahan signed the covering letter to the Secretary of Defense (page 4).

## 1986 GAO Testimony

At the subcommittee hearing on July 24, 1986, GAO presented findings from its review of Lockheed's Burbank facility, which housed the [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works|Skunk Works]] advanced development programs. The findings included:

> [!edit] Attribution clarified 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said Conahan presented the findings. The report credits the testimony to Martin M. Ferber; Gerb names Conahan.

- Lockheed had a "poor document control system" over classified special access documents.
- There was effectively zero oversight from the DOD program office over certain Lockheed special access contracts, which were carve-out programs operating with minimal government visibility.
- The Defense Investigative Service had not been permitted to conduct its usual semiannual inspections of the contract, because special access ("carve-out") contracts are carved out of its inspection program.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said the Defense Investigative Service had been "explicitly denied" permission. The report says carve-out contracts are excluded from its inspection program (page 7) and that problems might not have arisen if it "had been permitted" to inspect (page 4); it describes no specific refusal.

- An inventory of 40,000 classified documents revealed 1,460 discrepancies.
- Of the 235 discrepancies Lockheed had finished investigating by July 19, 1986, it reported 224 resolved and 11 unresolved (7 inadvertently destroyed, 4 not located). The resolved cases included 46 documents destroyed with destruction receipts reportedly on file and 17 transferred out of the company. GAO had not yet verified these results.

> [!edit] Corrected 17 October 2026
> Earlier versions said 46 documents had been destroyed and "could not be accounted for" and 17 transferred out and "never recovered". The report (pages 8 and 9) lists both among the 224 cases Lockheed counted as resolved; Gerb's video says the 17 "were never discovered".

## Significance in UAP Research

Gerb connects Conahan's testimony to the account in the [[Wilson-Davis Memo]] in which [[Thomas Wilson]] is told that the "watch committee" overseeing UAP crash retrieval and reverse engineering programs had to reorganize and improve security on their special access programs (SAPs) after nearly being exposed by an audit. The 1986 GAO audit came only a few years before the early-1990s reorganization Wilson described, so the timing fits. In this reading, the destroyed documents and the unaccounted transfers out of Lockheed's most classified facility are consistent with efforts to conceal the existence of UAP legacy programs housed within Lockheed's Skunk Works structure.
