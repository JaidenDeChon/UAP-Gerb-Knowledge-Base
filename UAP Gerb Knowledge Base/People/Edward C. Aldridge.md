Edward C. Aldridge, known as "Pete" Aldridge, held several senior posts in U.S. defense acquisition and Air Force leadership. He was Under Secretary of the Air Force from 1981 to 1986 and then Secretary of the Air Force from 1986 to 1988, which gave him authority over Air Force Special Access Programs while the black budget was expanding in the 1980s. For the whole of that time he was also Director of the [[NRO (National Reconnaissance Office)|National Reconnaissance Office]]. After leaving government he ran the Electronic Systems Company of McDonnell Douglas, then became president and CEO of the [[Aerospace Corporation]], a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC) spun off from [[TRW]] in 1960. He returned to government as Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition, Technology and Logistics under President George W. Bush from 2001 to 2003. Gerb calls his CV one of the most impressive he has found, rivalled only by [[Donald M. Kerr]]'s, and names him "a cornerstone figure" in how UFO legacy programs reorganized themselves inside special access programs in the 1980s.

## Career

Aldridge was born in Houston, Texas, in 1938, graduated from Texas A&M in 1960 and took a master's degree at Georgia Tech in 1962. After working from 1967 as an analyst in the Pentagon's systems analysis office, he spent a year from 1972 as a senior manager at LTV Aerospace in Dallas, a contractor Gerb notes later became the CIA-linked [[E-Systems]]. He then worked at the Office of Management and Budget and at System Planning Corporation before joining the Reagan administration. As Under Secretary of the Air Force from August 1981 he also had overall direction of Air Force space programs, including launch and on-orbit operations, and he directed the NRO until December 1988. Gerb regards the acquisition under-secretaryship he held from 2001 to 2003 as "paramount to gatekeeping UFO programs".

## Dual-Hatted NRO and Air Force Roles (1981–1986)

While he was Under Secretary of the Air Force, Aldridge was also Director of the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO), a post he held from 1981 to 1988. Gerb says he would "wager with the utmost confidence" that the two roles came with a third: a senior executive role in joint NRO and Air Force UFO legacy programs. In his account those programs included ISR (intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance) support for crash retrieval operations and, he wonders, joint RDT&E on derivative technologies or alien reproduction vehicles able to operate both in the atmosphere and in microgravity. Gerb also says he is aware that, as of 2017, specific funds had been misappropriated and earmarked for joint NRO and Air Force UFO legacy activity efforts.

## SAF/AA Outside Activities

An Air Force History and Museums document titled "A History of the Office of the Administrative Assistant to the Secretary of the Air Force" contains a 2018 interview with retired SAF/AA William A. Davidson. Davidson said that SAF/AA had maintained "several activities that were in other agencies over which the Air Force had administrative control", two of which were started "in Pete Aldridge's day." These outside activities:

- Did not appear in Air Force mission directives
- Operated exclusively under SAF/AA
- Were not subject to oversight by [[SAFAAZ|SAF/AAZ]], the Air Force's own Special Access Program Central Office (SAPCO)

In effect, the programs sat outside both standard Air Force SAP oversight and standard inter-agency accountability. Gerb doubts that the activities were created in Aldridge's day. He theorizes that they began as joint NRO/Air Force programs housed at the NRO, and that Aldridge moved them under SAF/AA, as a cover office, after the mid-1980s audits and SAP reforms that followed Reagan's 1982 Executive Order 12356 and the [[Yellow Fruit]] scandal. That order stripped away the ad hoc security protocols and loose top-secret codeword access the programs had previously exploited. The arrangement let legacy programs bypass even the Air Force's own component-level SAPCO while retaining Air Force administrative and operational control. Gerb adds that Davidson later joined the advisory board of Percepta, a company he links to Veritas Capital's alleged attempt to acquire the Kona Blue material.

It is one example of how UFO legacy programs burrowed deeper into the onion when they came under outside pressure: they hid as "outside activities" inside a cover office instead of using acknowledged SAP channels. Gerb sees this as a root of the siloed, extremely compartmentalized structure he describes today.
