Edward C. Aldridge, known as "Pete" Aldridge, held several senior posts in U.S. defense acquisition and Air Force leadership. He was Under Secretary of the Air Force from 1981 to 1986 and then Secretary of the Air Force from 1986 to 1988, which gave him authority over Air Force Special Access Programs while the black budget was expanding in the 1980s. After leaving the Air Force, Aldridge became CEO of the [[Aerospace Corporation]], a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC) spun off from [[TRW]]. He returned to government as Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition, Technology and Logistics under President George W. Bush from 2001 to 2003.

## Dual-Hatted NRO and Air Force Roles (1981–1986)

While he was Under Secretary of the Air Force, Aldridge was also Director of the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO), a post he held from 1981 to 1988. Holding both jobs matters in the UAP context because it gave Aldridge a senior post in two agencies, the Air Force and the NRO, that allegedly ran joint UFO legacy activities. These allegedly included ISR (intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance) support for crash retrieval operations and joint RDT&E on derivative technologies or alien reproduction vehicles able to operate both in the atmosphere and in microgravity. As of 2017, specific funds have allegedly been misappropriated and earmarked for joint NRO and Air Force UFO legacy activity efforts.

## SAF/AA Outside Activities

An Air Force History and Museums document titled "A History of the Office of the Administrative Assistant to the Secretary of the Air Force" contains a 2018 interview with retired SAF/AA William A. Davidson. Davidson said that SAF/AA had maintained "several activities that were in other agencies over which the Air Force had administrative control", programs set up "in Pete Aldridge's day." These outside activities:

- Did not appear in Air Force mission directives
- Operated exclusively under SAF/AA
- Were not subject to oversight by SAF/AAZ, the Air Force's own Special Access Program Central Office (SAPCO)

In effect, the programs sat outside both standard Air Force SAP oversight and standard inter-agency accountability. That order ended the informal top-secret codeword system the programs had previously exploited. The arrangement let legacy programs to bypass even the Air Force's own component-level SAPCO while retaining Air Force administrative and operational control.
