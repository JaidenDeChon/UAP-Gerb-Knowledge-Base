Captain Edward J. Ruppelt (1923–1960) headed the US Air Force's official UFO investigation from late 1951 to 1953, first as [[Project Grudge]] and then, from March 1952, as its successor, [[Project Blue Book]]. He inherited the files of the earlier [[Project Sign]], and in 1956 published *The Report on Unidentified Flying Objects*, an account of his years on the project. Gerb draws on him throughout his videos: for his ranking of the 1948 [[Gorman Dogfight]] among the cases that convinced Air Force intelligence, for his statement that programs parallel to the official investigation were "conducting a more complete investigation", and for what his book says about Project Sign and [[Project Pounce]].

## Career

Ruppelt was born in Grundy Center, Iowa, in 1923. He was assigned to the [[Air Force Technical Intelligence Center|Air Technical Intelligence Center]] (ATIC) at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]] in 1951 and was put in charge of Project Grudge late that year. In March 1952 Grudge became a separate organization and was renamed Project Blue Book, with Ruppelt still at its head. He left active duty in August 1953. When his book came out in 1956 he was working as a research engineer for Northrop Aircraft. He died in [[Long Beach, California]], on 15 September 1960, at the age of 37.

Gerb's videos sometimes give Ruppelt a longer tenure than this. *MOON DUST* says he ran the first official study under Project Grudge in 1948–1949 and went on to direct Project Sign and Blue Book, and other videos call him the director of Sign, Grudge and Blue Book. Sign (1948–1949) and Grudge's first years predate his time on the project; he wrote about them from its files.

## Parallel Investigations

In Gerb's account, Ruppelt concluded as director that UFOs had an "interplanetary explanation", and on 24 April 1952 suggested that programs parallel to the official investigation were "conducting a more complete investigation". Dr. [[J. Allen Hynek]], Blue Book's scientific adviser, likewise said that other classified programs were studying UFOs alongside Blue Book. In *MOON DUST*, Gerb suggests that one of these could have been an inconspicuous crash retrieval program, "one that unless you look hard enough you'll never know existed": [[Project Moon Dust]], first mentioned in a 1961 Air Force draft policy that proposed giving the [[USAF 1127th Field Activities Group]] collection duties for UFOs.

In his video on unidentified submerged objects, Gerb returns to Ruppelt to identify the sender of a 1952 letter forwarding nine reports of submarine contacts with flying discs. Ruppelt stated that the Air Force Office of Intelligence (AFOIN) was running a more complete UFO investigation parallel to Blue Book, connected to [[Battelle Memorial Institute]]'s later-uncovered [[Project Stork]], and named AFOIN's technical capabilities branch and collection control branch as offices doing UFO work. Gerb concludes that the [[AFOIN Special Group (AFOIN-X-SG)|AFOIN special group]] that sent the letter was likely one of the government's real secret UFO investigation groups. Ruppelt's name also appears in the classified 9 January 1953 Project Stork memo, in which Battelle's Dr. [[Howard C. Cross]] proposed that Stork "assist the Air Force in reassuring the public that everything is well under control with respect to the UFO problem."

## Project Sign and the Interplanetary Explanation

Ruppelt said that Project Sign endorsed the interplanetary explanation for some of its unexplained sightings. In his 1956 book, as Gerb recounts it, he wrote that he saw a 1948 Sign memo concluding that UFOs were of interplanetary origin, and that General [[Hoyt Vandenberg]], whom Gerb calls "one of the original accused members" of [[Majestic 12|MJ-12]], ordered it destroyed. For Gerb, Ruppelt's words show that before 1953 there was "some sort of transparency" on the subject.

## The Robertson Panel

In December 1952, USAF Colonel Donald L. Bower forbade Ruppelt from visiting the CIA's Office of Special Investigations to share compelling UFO data. The CIA's [[Robertson Panel]] sat a month later. Gerb takes the timing as evidence that the panel's conclusions were predetermined, to devalue UFOs and attribute them to misidentified prosaic phenomena.

## Flying Saucer Talk

Representative [[Eric Burlison]] has called out by name and file designation (AF-ATIC-film 0352) a 1952 filmed briefing by Ruppelt titled *Flying Saucer Talk*, held at [[MIT Lincoln Laboratory]], a federally funded research and development center descended from Vannevar Bush's MIT Radiation Laboratory. Gerb asks why Lincoln Laboratory holds the film and why it was never volunteered to the National Archives, and suspects the films there depict sensitive briefings on non-human craft. In his video on [[MITRE Corporation|MITRE]], he notes that Lincoln Laboratory had still not delivered the film to Burlison's office.

## Project Pounce

Ruppelt's book supplies Gerb's clues to [[Project Pounce]], the "outline of investigation of UFOs proposed by Kirtland Air Force Base" that appears among the evidence reviewed by the 1953 Robertson Panel. According to Ruppelt, after saucer maneuvers over [[Albuquerque, New Mexico|Albuquerque]] on 2 August 1952 the local air-defense commander proposed a flight of six stripped-down F-94C interceptors with nose cameras, two of them always on alert, to photograph UFOs. It was shelved only because the aircraft could not be spared. Gerb notes that this was an imagery-intelligence mission of the kind the NRO would take on nine years later.

## Gorman Dogfight Assessment

Ruppelt wrote of the 1 October 1948 [[Gorman Dogfight]] over [[Fargo, North Dakota, USA|Fargo, North Dakota]] as one of three classic 1948 incidents that "proved to Air Force intelligence specialists that UFOs were real." In it, Second Lieutenant [[George F. Gorman]] of the North Dakota National Guard chased a small blinking ball of light in his P-51 Mustang from about 9:07 p.m., when he contacted the tower, until he gave up the pursuit at 9:27 p.m. The object outmaneuvered him with steep vertical climbs his aircraft could not follow.
