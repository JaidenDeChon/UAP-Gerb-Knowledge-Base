George Catlett Marshall was one of the most consequential military and diplomatic figures in American history. As Army Chief of Staff throughout World War II, he built the US Army from a peacetime force into a global fighting organization. He was also a member of the Manhattan Project's Top Policy Group, the very small civilian-military body that oversaw the atomic bomb program and controlled access to it. ## Military Career and Manhattan Project Role

Marshall was Army Chief of Staff from 1939 to 1945. He oversaw the transformation of the US Army and coordinated Allied strategy at the highest levels. In the Manhattan Project he sat on the Top Policy Group, the highest civilian-political oversight body for the atomic program. The group consisted of President FDR (later Truman), Vice President Henry Wallace, Secretary of War Henry Stimson, [[Vannevar Bush]] and James Conant. Through it, the president kept control of the Manhattan Project while bypassing normal government channels.

As Army Chief of Staff, Marshall sat at the top of the extremely short chain of command to which Manhattan Project director [[Leslie Groves]] reported. This narrow oversight let Groves bypass standard acquisition and bureaucratic channels. Gerb identifies it as the model that was copied directly into the organization of UFO legacy programs.

His solution was the Alsos Missions, a compartmented intelligence operation set up under the Manhattan Engineer District (MED). It ran completely outside standard military intelligence channels and reported through Marshall's G-2 (assistant chief of staff for intelligence). [[Leslie Groves]] commanded the Alsos Missions, [[Vannevar Bush]] oversaw them, and Colonel Boris Pash led them on the ground.

Alsos used T-Forces, lightly armed and extremely mobile units drawn from the Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force, to physically secure German scientists, nuclear materials, laboratories and facilities on the war's front lines ahead of Soviet forces. T-Forces operated within Soviet-controlled territory, and their methods were described as sometimes resembling Gestapo tactics: "kidnapping at night by state officials who offered no evidence of identity." In the end Alsos captured Werner Heisenberg and other leading German atomic scientists, along with stores of uranium ore, heavy water and thousands of research documents.
