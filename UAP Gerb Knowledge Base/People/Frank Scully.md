Frank Scully was an American journalist and Variety magazine columnist who in 1950 published *Behind the Flying Saucers*, one of the first books to claim that the US government had recovered crashed extraterrestrial spacecraft. The book described an alleged crash retrieval at Aztec, New Mexico, and drew mainly on claims by oil businessman [[Silas Newton]] and a group of scientists known as "Dr. G." Newton and [[Leo GeBauer|GeBauer]] were later convicted of fraud in an unrelated scam. Even so, the book still matters in UAP history, partly because it came out so early and partly because [[Robert Sarbacher]], a consultant to the DoD Research and Development Board, told [[Wilbert B. Smith]] that its contents were "fundamentally correct."

## Behind the Flying Saucers (1950)

Published in September 1950, *Behind the Flying Saucers* was the first major book about UFO crash retrievals, several decades before the Roswell story became public. Scully presented it as a journalistic investigation based on sources in the scientific community. It described small humanoid occupants, advanced metallurgy and US government efforts to study recovered craft. The book sold well and shaped what the public expected a UFO crash to involve.

The book's credibility suffered badly in 1952, when Silas Newton, one of Scully's main sources, was exposed as a con man in an unrelated oil leasing fraud. The exposure was widely used to write off Scully's claims as fabrication.

## The Aztec Story and Its Sources

Scully first wrote about the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery|Aztec crash]] in two 1949 *Variety* columns. According to Gerb's 2025 video on the case, his source was oilman [[Silas Newton]], who introduced him to "Dr. G," a group of up to eight scientists with, in Scully's phrase, "more degrees than a thermometer." They told Scully the disc had been recovered intact and taken to [[Los Alamos National Laboratory|Los Alamos]], and that it was powered by magnetism. They also showed him a cube that made radio-like sounds, small gears and discs said to come from the craft, and photographs of it. Scully's widow later said she had seen the photographs too. The book reported four such recoveries up to 1950. Scully took the Dr. G names to his grave.

In 1949 journalist [[J. P. Cahn]] tried to buy the saucer story from Scully, who refused. Scully's 15 June 1952 letter to Newton's attorney records Cahn's later threat to name Scully as part of a hoax unless an exposé ran under his name. Cahn's September 1952 article, which named [[Leo GeBauer]] as the sole Dr. G, is the main reason the story came to be regarded as a hoax. In Scully's archive, [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]] later found the recording of Newton's 1950 Denver lecture and Newton's unfinished autobiography. Scully himself was never branded a hoaxer, a point Newton complained about bitterly.

## Sarbacher's Endorsement

Despite the controversy over the book's main sources, [[Robert Sarbacher]] told [[Wilbert B. Smith]] in their 1950 Washington meeting that he had read *Behind the Flying Saucers* and that its contents were "fundamentally correct." Sarbacher was a physicist who served on the DoD Research and Development Board and had access to official UAP reports. Researchers cite his endorsement as evidence that the Aztec story may contain genuine elements that do not depend on Newton and GeBauer.

## Significance in UAP History

Scully's book set the pattern for the crash retrieval stories that dominated UAP discussion for decades: circular craft, small humanoid occupants, recovered materials and government concealment. Whether or not the Aztec case he described is authentic, *Behind the Flying Saucers* shaped how researchers and the public later interpreted and investigated the subject.
