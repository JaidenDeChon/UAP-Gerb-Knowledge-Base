The 1986 Japan Airlines UFO Incident is one of the most thoroughly documented UAP encounters in aviation history. On November 17, 1986, the crew of Japan Airlines cargo flight 1628, a Boeing 747-200F flying the Reykjavik-to-Anchorage leg of a Paris-to-Tokyo route, watched a huge unidentified craft and two smaller luminous objects follow their aircraft at cruising altitude for 31 minutes without a break. The main object was described as about the size of an aircraft carrier, and FAA long-range 3D phased array radar confirmed the encounter independently. An academic physics analysis has calculated that the craft's maneuvers would have put it under forces far beyond any known limit a human could survive.

## Crew and Encounter

[[Captain Kenju Terauchi]] and two other crew members first saw a large round object and two smaller lights approaching from the left at about 35,000 feet, at around 5:00 p.m. on November 17, 1986. The main craft was estimated at roughly the diameter of four Boeing 747s, or about the size of an aircraft carrier. The three objects followed flight 1628 for 31 minutes while the aircraft held its cruising speed.

Terauchi said the main craft kept a standoff distance of about 7.5 miles from the aircraft and periodically circled it, sometimes at a constant speed and sometimes darting in and out of its orbit. The two smaller luminous objects stayed with the larger craft throughout.

## Radar Confirmation

FAA FPS-117 long-range 3D phased array radar systems tracked the encounter. The radar data showed the object staying at a radius of about 7.5 miles from the aircraft the whole time, occasionally switching sides between the radar's 12-second sweeps. This independent support for the crew's account is central to the case's credibility.

## Physics Analysis

Physicist [[Kevin Knuth]] of the University at Albany, with Robert M. Powell and Peter Reali, modeled the forces the craft would have experienced as it circled flight 1628. (Gerb introduces Knuth as a New York University professor.) Their 2019 paper, *Estimating Flight Characteristics of Anomalous Unidentified Aerial Vehicles*, appeared in the journal *Entropy*. They calculated that:

- If the craft crossed the full diameter of its circular orbit, it experienced 68 ± 7 Gs of force.
- If it moved along the circular path (centripetal acceleration), it experienced 84 ± 8 Gs, sustained for 31 minutes.

For comparison, 9 Gs sustained for even one minute is the physiological limit for a trained fighter pilot wearing a G-suit. No known human-piloted aircraft could perform like this.

## Official Response and Debunking

Aviation Week and Space Technology magazine promoted the explanation that the crew had seen the planets Mars and Jupiter. Gerb calls the case severely underrated and rejects that explanation, pointing to the radar track and the modelled G-forces. He also ties it to [[J. Allen Hynek]]'s charge that the Air Force deliberately explained interesting cases away as balloons or planets. Hynek, the former scientific adviser to [[Project Blue Book]], had died in April 1986, months before the sighting, but Gerb offers the Mars-and-Jupiter explanation as an example of the pattern Hynek described. The Japan Airlines incident is often cited as a case in which strong physical and radar evidence was explained away with an implausible official answer.
