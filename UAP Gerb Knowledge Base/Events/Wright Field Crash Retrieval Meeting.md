The Wright Field Crash Retrieval Meeting was an alleged classified gathering at [[Wright Field]] (later Wright-Patterson Air Force Base) around 1949–1950, where US government scientists and military officials discussed recovered UAP craft, materials, and occupants. [[Robert Sarbacher]] and [[Eric A. Walker]] both described the meeting in testimony, and each confirmed that he knew of or took part in discussions about crash retrievals and reverse engineering during this period.

## Participants

According to [[Robert Sarbacher]]'s 1983 testimony to researchers [[William Steinman]] and [[Stanton Friedman]], the following people were involved in the UAP crash retrieval program:

- [[Vannevar Bush]], chairman of the DoD's Research and Development Board. Sarbacher identified him as the head of the small group studying flying saucers.
- [[John Von Neumann]], a mathematician, physicist, and Manhattan Project veteran. Sarbacher named him as "definitely involved."
- [[J. Robert Oppenheimer]], scientific director of the Manhattan Project. Sarbacher also named him as "definitely involved."
- [[Eric A. Walker|Dr. Eric A. Walker]], Executive Secretary of the Defense Research Board (1950–1951). In later interviews with Steinman, he confirmed that he attended the Wright Field meeting.
- [[Robert Sarbacher]], a physicist and consultant to the Research and Development Board. He said he was invited to the discussions but did not attend them himself.

In his 1983 recorded conversation with [[Stanton Friedman]], Sarbacher mentioned someone from Philadelphia who attended "all the meetings" and "acted very smug about it." Researchers later identified this person as [[Eric A. Walker]]. Walker's career had placed him in Philadelphia, and his post as Executive Secretary of the Defense Research Board gave him access to the program.

## Topics Discussed

According to Sarbacher's accounts, the Wright Field meeting or meetings covered these subjects:

1. Recovered UAP craft: the physical characteristics, materials, and propulsion systems of downed flying saucers.
2. Recovered occupants: analysis of biological entities described as having insectoid anatomy. They were lightweight, lacked traditional skin, and were built to withstand extreme inertial forces.
3. Materials analysis: study of materials reported to be "extremely light and very tough," which matches accounts of debris recovered from crash sites.
4. Reverse engineering: concentrated attempts to understand how the recovered craft operated.

Sarbacher said the program focused on flight characteristics that appeared to violate known physics, especially the craft's ability to make high-speed maneuvers and change direction instantly without slowing down.

## Timing and Historical Context

The meeting took place amid these events:

- July 1947: the Roswell crash and a wave of UFO sightings across the United States.
- 1948: the alleged [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery]].
- 1949–1950: [[Wilbert B. Smith]] met with Sarbacher and recorded Sarbacher's claim that the study of flying saucers was classified higher than the hydrogen bomb.
- March 1950: the [[Hottel Memo]] was written. It records claims of three recovered saucers in New Mexico.

The Wright Field meeting or meetings were likely high-level deliberations on how to manage, analyze, and exploit recovered non-human technology, held as Cold War tensions escalated and the US nuclear weapons program grew.

## Eric Walker's Confirmation

In interviews from 1987–1990 with [[William Steinman]] and researcher Dr. Henry Victorian, [[Eric A. Walker]] directly confirmed the following:

- He attended the Wright Field meeting described by Sarbacher.
- The meeting concerned crash retrievals and recovered bodies.
- [[Vannevar Bush]] was involved in the program.
- A group resembling or identical to [[Majestic 12]] existed, and Walker had known about it for 40 years.

Walker's confirmation came decades after Sarbacher's first disclosure to [[Wilbert B. Smith]] in 1950, and it independently corroborates that the meeting took place.

## Significance

The meeting is significant in early UAP history because it brought the US government's most prominent wartime scientists, the people who had built the atomic bomb and shaped postwar defense science, together around the question of recovered non-human technology. It put UAP analysis where nuclear weapons policy, advanced propulsion research, and national security meet. Those concerns would define the secrecy and compartmentalization that continues to surround the subject.

Several independent sources (Sarbacher, Walker, Smith's contemporaneous notes) refer to the same meeting and participants. That lends substantial credibility to the claim that such discussions occurred, even though no documentation has been publicly released.
