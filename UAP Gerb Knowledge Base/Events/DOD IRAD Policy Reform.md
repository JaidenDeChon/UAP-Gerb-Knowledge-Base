The DOD IRAD Policy Reform was a series of sweeping changes to Department of Defense Independent Research and Development (IRAD) regulations, made around 1991. The changes removed the requirement for defense contractors to submit IRAD plans for government approval and eliminated the ceilings on reimbursement spending. Researchers and whistleblowers, including [[David Grusch]] in sworn congressional testimony, argue that these changes made it possible for defense contractors to begin funding covert UAP-related reverse engineering work outside standard government oversight.

## Prior System

Under the earlier IRAD rules, defense contractors that wanted to recover research and development costs through government reimbursement had to submit detailed IRAD plans to the Defense Technical Information Center (DTIC). The government reviewed these plans, which in theory limited what contractors could pursue with reimbursed funds. Reimbursement ceilings capped how much contractors could claim back from the government.

## The 1991 Changes

The 1991 reforms removed both the submission requirement and the spending ceiling. From then on, contractors could run IRAD programs of any scale and kind without prior government review and recover the costs through overhead charges on other contracts. This dramatically widened their control over their own research portfolios. The stated reason for the change was to reduce bureaucratic overhead and encourage innovation.

## Alleged Impact on UAP Programs

[[David Grusch]] testified under oath before Congress that UAP legacy programs are partly funded through IRAD overcharging. In this scheme, contractors submit lowball figures to DTIC while running far larger undisclosed programs, and the surplus pays for clandestine work on recovered non-human materials. The 1991 reforms are argued to have made this mechanism possible and difficult to detect.

[[Northrop Grumman]]'s annual IRAD spending rose from approximately $331 million to over $500 million following its 2002 acquisition of [[TRW]], an increase tracked in a 2015 University of Maryland report. Researchers argue this increase reflects, at least in part, the expansion of inherited TRW UAP legacy programs under Northrop Grumman ownership, funded through the regulatory gap created in 1991. A parallel mechanism involved SDI contract back-channels, in which contract payments to TRW for programs such as the [[Brilliant Pebbles Initiative]] were allegedly used to route funds to covert UAP research through classified Air Force "outside activities."

Representative Moskowitz questioned Grusch about IRAD misappropriation and related funding mechanisms during the 2023 congressional UAP hearings.
