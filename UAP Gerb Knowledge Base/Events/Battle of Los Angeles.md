The Battle of Los Angeles, also called the Great Los Angeles Air Raid, was a military incident in the early morning hours of February 24–25, 1942, when US Army anti-aircraft artillery units opened fire on unidentified targets over [[Los Angeles, California]]. No enemy aircraft were confirmed and none were shot down. The incident was officially attributed to war nerves and possibly a misidentified meteorological balloon. Five civilians died: three in traffic accidents during the blackout and two of heart attacks. UFO researchers still discuss the event as a possible early case of the military responding to an unidentified craft.

## Background

The incident occurred fewer than three months after the [[Attack on Pearl Harbor]] (December 7, 1941), when American war anxiety on the Pacific Coast was at its peak. On February 23, 1942, a Japanese submarine had shelled an oil refinery near Santa Barbara. It was the first attack on the continental US since the War of 1812, and it raised alert levels across the region.

## The Incident

At approximately 2:25 a.m. on February 25, 1942, air raid sirens sounded across Los Angeles and a blackout was ordered. Anti-aircraft batteries began firing around 3:16 a.m. and fired approximately 1,400 rounds over about one hour, while multiple searchlights tracked targets in the air. No enemy aircraft were found, and none were shot down. The all-clear was sounded at 7:21 a.m.

Secretary of War Henry Stimson initially suggested the incident may have involved up to 15 aircraft. The Navy Department later attributed the firing to war nerves. The War Department's formal explanation cited a misidentified weather balloon.

## The LA Times Photograph

The Los Angeles Times published a photograph on February 26, 1942, showing searchlight beams converging on an apparent saucer-shaped object in the sky. The photograph is widely believed to have been heavily retouched to make the bright point where the beams meet stand out, and the original does not clearly show a structured craft. The image still became iconic in early UFO research.

## Historical Reception

The case entered UFO culture as a possible early example of the military engaging an anomalous craft. Gerb credits it with introducing him to UFO research as a child. He does not find it compelling evidence of non-human craft on its own and says a prosaic explanation is likely.
