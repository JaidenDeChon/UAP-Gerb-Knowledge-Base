The Frasier Island USO Sighting was reported in the July–August 1966 issue of *Flying Saucer Review*. Private pilot C. Adams and television cameraman Les Hendy, flying in an aircraft, saw four or five anomalous objects approximately three miles east of [[Frasier Island, Australia]]. The objects stood still in the water, then submerged without disturbing the surface as the aircraft approached. Gerb explicitly connects this behavior to the HMNZS Southland case discussed by [[Kevin Knuth]] at the [[Sol Foundation]], and more broadly to [[Trans-Medium Vehicle|transmedium]] behavior, in which craft enter water without creating a surface disturbance.

## Incident Description

C. Adams, a private pilot, and Les Hendy, a television cameraman, were watching from an aircraft when they saw the objects in the water approximately three miles east of Frasier Island. There were four or five of them: two dark cigar-shaped objects, described as narrow and up to 100 feet long, with three smaller objects alongside. At first they stood still in the water. When the aircraft closed to within two miles, they began to submerge. They did not disturb the surface of the water as they went under and, as described, appeared not to interact with the water at all.

The men first wondered whether the objects might be whales or submarines. The lack of any surface disturbance as the objects submerged was the detail that ruled out conventional explanations.

## Cross-Case Connections

Gerb draws two explicit connections for this case:

1. The HMNZS Southland case, which Knuth discussed. It is a New Zealand naval case from waters near New Zealand, a region next to this one, and a similar lack of surface disturbance was noted as the object submerged. Both cases come from the same broad region of the southwestern Pacific.

2. Transmedium craft that do not interact with the water they enter. An object that enters water without disturbing the surface must either be moving at a speed and angle that leave the surface undisturbed, which does not fit objects that were standing still in the water, or have some mechanism that suppresses the usual hydrodynamic effects of moving through a fluid.
