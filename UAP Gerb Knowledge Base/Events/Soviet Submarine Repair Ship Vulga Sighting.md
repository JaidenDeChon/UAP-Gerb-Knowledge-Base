The Soviet Submarine Repair Ship Vulga Sighting took place on October 7, 1977, in the Barents Sea. Nine unidentified disc-shaped objects approached the Soviet submarine repair ship Vulga and circled it for 18 minutes while its communications systems malfunctioned. After the incident, [[Fleet Admiral Nikolai Smirnov]] issued a formal directive requiring Soviet hydrographic, scientific research and reconnaissance ships to report UFO sightings. It was the Soviet equivalent of the American [[OPNAV 3820]] directive. The case is documented in *Russia's USO Secrets* by [[Paul Stonehill]] and [[Philip Mantle]] and in Jacques Vallée's *UFO Chronicles of the Soviet Union* (pages 29–30).

## Incident Description

The Vulga's radar detected an unknown object approaching from about 60 miles away. Captain Tanton went to the bridge, where he and the crew saw nine separate bright, disc-shaped objects coming in from the northeast. The discs reached the ship and began circling it, and the crew watched them directly for 18 minutes.

The ship's communication links malfunctioned for the whole encounter. The same pattern appeared in the [[1976 Tehran UFO Incident]], when communications were disrupted during a UAP encounter. All communications and operations returned to normal after the discs left. This loss of communications, and their return once the objects had gone, is treated as an example of the electromagnetic interference documented in many UAP encounters.

## Institutional Response

The Vulga incident prompted a formal response from the Soviet Navy. [[Fleet Admiral Nikolai Smirnov]] issued a directive that made UFO reporting mandatory for Soviet hydrographic, scientific research and reconnaissance ships. It was authorized by naval officer and UFO researcher [[Vladimir Azhazha]] and signed by Naval Deputy Chief of Staff P. Noitov. The directive covered research and hydrographic vessels, not combat ships, which suggests the navy recognized that scientific survey work made USO phenomena more likely to be encountered and documented.

UAP Gerb explicitly compares this Soviet mandatory reporting directive with the American [[OPNAV 3820]]. He notes that both were issued in response to significant encounters, and that both sent reports through intelligence channels instead of public-facing programs.
