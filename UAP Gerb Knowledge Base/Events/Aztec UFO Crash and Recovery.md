The **Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery** is the alleged crash of a large disc-shaped craft on a mesa off Hart Canyon Road, about 12 miles northeast of [[Aztec, New Mexico]], on the morning of 25 March 1948, and its recovery by US military personnel and scientists. According to the accounts collected by researchers, the disc was 99.9 feet across, came to rest almost undamaged, and held between 12 and 16 small humanoid bodies. Local civilians saw it before the military sealed the site, and the craft was dismantled and trucked to [[Los Alamos National Laboratory|Los Alamos]]. The story was first published by [[Frank Scully]] in 1950 and branded a hoax after its main source, oilman [[Silas Newton]], and his associate [[Leo GeBauer]] were convicted of fraud in 1953. Later researchers, among them [[William Steinman]], [[Leonard Stringfield]] and [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]], revived it with new witnesses. It is also sometimes called the Hart Canyon crash.

## The Crash

New Mexico in 1948 was ringed with high-powered radar protecting Los Alamos, [[White Sands Missile Range|White Sands]] and [[Sandia National Laboratories|Sandia]], and Steinman's sources said three Southwest radar sites tracked the object. Several researchers suspect radar interference brought it down, a theory also advanced for the [[Roswell Crash]] eight months earlier and echoed in the 1950 [[Hottel Memo]]. Witnesses described a low, wobbling disc: rancher [[Valentine Archuleta]] watched it pass within 200 yards and strike a 150-foot cliff in a shower of sparks, and policeman [[Manuel Sandoval]] of [[Cuba, New Mexico]] followed it north, "fluttering like a leaf."

## The Scene on the Mesa

Oil workers of the El Paso company, among them 19-year-old [[Doug Noland]] and his supervisor Bill Ferguson, were among the first to arrive, having been told of a brush fire near their drip tanks. They described a seamless disc of dull, brushed aluminium about 100 feet across and 18 to 20 feet high, with no rivets, bolts or welds, three gold-coloured rings on its rim, and portholes that looked like mirrors until seen up close. Through a quarter-sized hole in one porthole they saw two small bodies, charred dark brown, slumped over a control panel. Ferguson poked at the craft with a fire pole until "a door or walkway appeared." Local ranchers Mr. and Mrs. Knight warned the men off, [[Ken Farley]] and a friend arrived after following the dust of other cars, and a helicopter circled overhead.

The military arrived quickly "in a very big way": seasoned, well-trained men in Army-style vehicles who separated everyone for interviews and told them the event was vital to national security and never to be discussed. A USAF airman known by the pseudonym "George," interviewed by Scott Ramsey in 2001, said the retrieval was coordinated from Walker Air Force Base (the renamed [[Roswell Army Airfield]]), with personnel flown to [[Durango, Colorado]]. He said it involved about 200 people and was designed to avoid Roswell's mistakes: "From that time on, no more screw-ups."

## The Recovery

In Scully's version, relayed from Newton's sources, investigators pushed a rod through a punctured porthole until a panel opened. Inside were two bodies at a panel about four feet high and 12 to 14 more in a separate cabin. Steinman's 1986 account says General [[George C. Marshall]] was notified and called in the [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] from Camp Hale, Colorado, and [[Vannevar Bush]]. Bush's scientific team reportedly included [[Detlev Bronk]], [[John Von Neumann]], [[J. Robert Oppenheimer]], [[Carl A. Heiland]] and the cryogenics specialist Paul A. Scherer. The team found glyph-covered screens and a plastic pamphlet that cryptologists William F. Friedman and Lambros D. Callimahos could not decipher. Bronk had the bodies preserved on dry ice and later described autopsies of beings of about 40 pounds with no digestive or reproductive organs.

The recovery took up to two weeks. Torches and diamond tools could not mark the hull until a scientist found interlocking keys that split it along seams, and the craft came apart as a crew cabin and three equal sections. Concrete pads were poured for M25 Dragon Wagon transporters, and 1948 [[Bureau of Reclamation]] records show heavy equipment in the area. The convoy took the wreckage to Los Alamos, where it reportedly stayed for over a year; the bodies likely went to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]]. [[Eric Henry Wang]] of the Wright Air Development Center was reportedly brought onto the project in November 1949. In 1994, former state representative [[Andrew Kissner]] said his sources described two to four discs recovered in 1947–1948 and held at Los Alamos and Sandia under the [[Atomic Energy Commission]].

## Leak, Exposé and Trial

Newton said he heard the story in 1949 from "the top magnetic scientist of the United States," one of a group of scientists known as "Dr. G." He lectured on it as "Scientist X" at the University of Denver on 8 March 1950. The next day [[Air Force Office of Special Investigations|AFOSI]] sought out [[George Koehler]], the radio man who recorded the lecture, and on 30 September 1950 Army CID and the FBI staged a sting in Denver to intercept photographs of the disc. Scully's *Behind the Flying Saucers* appeared in September 1950. [[Robert Sarbacher]] told [[Wilbert B. Smith]] that its facts were "substantially correct."

Journalist [[J. P. Cahn]], who had failed to buy the story, published "The Flying Saucers and the Mysterious Little Men" in September 1952, naming GeBauer as the sole Dr. G and tying both men to oil fraud. Denver businessman [[Herman Flader]] pressed charges over the pair's oil-finding "doodlebug," and they were convicted on 29 December 1953 but never sentenced. Defenders argue the prosecution was retaliation for the leak. They point to 32 of 33 investors being satisfied, to the prosecution's expert testing a war-surplus stand-in rather than GeBauer's device, and to Cahn's close work with the prosecution and with [[J. Edgar Hoover]].

## Revival

[[Robert Spencer Carr]] reopened the case in October 1974, claiming a senior Air Force source, and Mike McClellan's 1975 article called it a hoax again. Steinman's *UFO Crash at Aztec: A Well Kept Secret* (1986) named the alleged recovery team, and Stringfield's 1991 Status Report VI reproduced a report on a crash near Farmington, New Mexico, with a similar punctured porthole. The Ramseys' *The Aztec UFO Incident* (2015) collected the witness testimony and archival records. Former Navy chief R&D scientist Nat Kobitz reportedly told [[Ross Coulthart]] that Roswell, Aztec and Kingman were all real recoveries.

## Significance in Legacy Program Research

UAP Gerb treats Aztec as a window into the first year of organised crash retrieval: a recovery run with new people and procedures after Roswell, material placed in Atomic Energy Commission custody at Los Alamos, and a coordinated effort to discredit those who leaked it. Other videos trace the craft exploitation that researchers associate with Aztec-era material through Atomic Energy Commission-connected personnel, then through [[TRW]] Systems Group, and ultimately to [[Northrop Grumman]] after TRW's acquisition in 2002. The same recovery methods and personnel, notably Eric Henry Wang, recur in the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval]].
