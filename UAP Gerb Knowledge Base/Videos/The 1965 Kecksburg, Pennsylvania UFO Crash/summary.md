## Overview

This video is UAP Gerb's case study of the [[1965 Kecksburg, Pennsylvania Crash Retrieval|Kecksburg crash]]. Early on the evening of 9 December 1965, thousands of people across six US states and Canada watched a fireball cross the sky, and around 4:45 p.m. something came down in the woods outside [[Kecksburg, Pennsylvania]]. Local witnesses who reached it first describe a seamless bronze object shaped like an acorn or a bell, 10 to 12 feet long, with a raised band of hieroglyph-like markings around its base. Soldiers, airmen in blue berets, men in civilian clothes and men in white "moon suits" arrived within the hour. By the early morning of 10 December the object had been hauled out of the woods under a tarp on an Army flatbed.

The host leans mainly on the work of the case's longest-serving investigator, [[Stan Gordon]], and on [[Leonard Stringfield]], with whom Gordon shared witnesses. He sets the witness testimony against the official accounts. [[Project Blue Book]] called the object a meteor and said its three-man team found nothing. Sceptics such as [[James Oberg]] proposed the Soviet probe [[Cosmos 96]], but NASA's [[Nicholas Johnson]] later said Cosmos 96 could not have come down in Pennsylvania and that no man-made object did. Journalist [[Leslie Kean]] then sued NASA for its Kecksburg files, only to learn that the National Archives had listed them as lost since 1987.

The second half follows the object after it left the woods. An Air Force guard says it passed through [[Lockbourne Air Force Base, Columbus, Ohio|Lockbourne Air Force Base]]. A trucker known as "[[Myron]]" and his cousin say they saw it in a warehouse at [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]], and Myron later added a small body on a workbench. The video then turns to two figures it thinks were close to the case. The first is [[Eric A. Walker]], president of Penn State and founder of its Navy-funded [[Applied Research Laboratory]], who admitted going to the crash site. The second is [[Lyndon B. Johnson]], who spent the day after the crash in meetings at his Texas ranch with the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary, NASA's administrator, his science adviser and the chairman of the Atomic Energy Commission. The host concludes that he leans toward Kecksburg being a genuine retrieval of a non-human craft. He notes that the one thing the case lacks is official documents, and that those are the documents NASA says it lost.

## The Night of 9 December 1965

### The fireball

In the early evening, residents of Michigan, Indiana, Ohio, West Virginia, Pennsylvania, New York and Canada watched a brilliant ball of fire streak across the sky. It left a smoke trail that stayed visible for about 20 minutes. Pilots and people on the ground first took it for an aircraft in trouble, especially when pieces seemed to break off over Lapeer, Michigan and Elyria, Ohio. The video says Blue Book's own report later recognised and studied that material. Researcher Clark McClelland, a former member of NASA's Apollo programme, and an astronomy professor calculated that the object changed course by about 25 degrees over Elyria and was 40 to 60 miles up when first seen over Michigan. From the spread of the sightings, McClelland put its speed at about 17 miles a minute, or roughly 1,020 mph. Meteors travel at 25,000 to 144,000 mph, so the host argues the object was far too slow to be one even if the estimate were ten times too low.

Around 4:45 p.m. the object turned between [[Greensburg, Pennsylvania|Greensburg]] and Latrobe and headed south, where local communities reported it. Further south it turned again, tracking north-east toward Kecksburg, where it reportedly came down in the woods. [[Project Blue Book]] sent a three-man Air Force team from the 662nd Radar Squadron, which was stationed at the Oakdale Army installation near Pittsburgh and worked under Air Defense Command. The host presumes the orders came from Major [[Hector Quintanilla]], who had run Blue Book since 1963 and whose name appears throughout its 33-page Kecksburg report. That report called the object a meteor, said the team found no material, and filed the case under Acme, Pennsylvania. Researchers are not sure why; the host suggests the team may have interviewed witnesses who lived in Acme. To show what Quintanilla's word was worth, the host points to 5 April 1966. That day an Air Force major told the House Armed Services Committee that Blue Book had no unexplained radar cases, and Blue Book's scientific adviser [[J. Allen Hynek]] contradicted him with three.

### The first on the scene

WHJB Greensburg news director [[John Murphy]] took a call from a mother who said she and her children had watched a fiery object fall into the woods and a cloud of blue smoke rise afterwards. Murphy judged her sincere and drove to the woods. There, State Police fire marshal [[Carl Mets]] went in with another investigator, came out, and told him to get his information from the Army. At the Greensburg barracks Murphy found Army and Air Force men swarming the building. It was the first time, he said later, that anyone had mentioned the military to him.

Volunteer firemen from the Kecksburg company, sent to search for what was thought to be a downed aircraft, heard by walkie-talkie that the site had been found. Among them was machinist [[Jim Romansky]]. They found an object half-buried in the ground at the end of a 25-foot trench. It had cut through the treetops at about 30 degrees, and there was no sign of fire. According to Romansky, after 10 or 15 minutes two ramrod-stiff, crew-cut men in trench coats came down through the woods, declared it a restricted area and ordered the firemen out. The firemen had gone only 15 or 20 feet when uniformed troops came marching the other way with flashlights. The nearest base Romansky knew of was at Pittsburgh, about 45 miles west. The military had taken over a farmhouse at the top of the hill, and by the time the firemen had walked the mile back to the fire hall it was "wall to wall" with military trucks, jeeps and guards. The men were told to shut up, get in their vehicles and keep quiet.

[[Bill Bully Bush|Bill "Bully" Bush]] got there before the firemen. As far as researchers know, he had no contact with Romansky. He watched the object hesitate, turn and head for Kecksburg, and in a clip he says it moved as if radio-controlled and made a figure eight before it "belly-landed". He drove to the top of what is now Meteor Road, saw blue lights flashing in the woods and walked in. He found the object embedded a foot to a foot and a half deep. It smelled of rotten eggs, and blue sparks arced off it at intervals as though it were cooling. The host knows of no other witness who mentions a smell, and compares it with the ammonia smell reported in the Varginha, Brazil case. Bush fled when he heard the firemen coming.

### The recovery

Witnesses describe several kinds of military men: Army soldiers with M1 rifles, Air Force men in blue fatigues and blue berets, men in civilian clothes giving orders, and men in hazmat suits that they called moon suits. Blue Book and the Air Force said no more than three men were there, and the host notes that Eric Walker would later contradict that. The Pittsburgh Press reported the military presence on 10 December. At a later town meeting, Kecksburg residents reached a majority consensus that something big had happened and that soldiers had been on the site.

Pittsburgh jazz musician [[Jerry Betters]], then 41 and living near Kecksburg, came forward in the early 1990s. He and some friends drove toward the crash and, on a private lane, ran into a military roadblock. Behind it he saw officers, well-dressed civilians, jeeps and personnel trucks. He also saw a flatbed Army truck struggling up through a field with an uncovered acorn-shaped object on the back, "a little bigger than a Volkswagen". A tarp was thrown over it, an officer shouted to get the civilians out, and soldiers levelled their guns at them. Betters says he saw the hieroglyphic markings round the back before they were chased away. Local resident David Newhouse says a soldier pointed an M1 rifle straight at him. The teenager Bill Weaver watched four men in white moon suits get out of a light-coloured box truck and carry a box, four or five feet square, on a four-handled stretcher down to the object. A soldier then threatened to confiscate his car if he did not leave. Fourteen-year-old Mike Slater says an Army officer told him and a friend they could serve their country by sending curious crash-seekers the wrong way.

## The Object

Romansky, Bush and Betters never compared notes, and the trucker Myron and his cousin JS saw the object days later in Ohio. Their descriptions line up closely.

## Silencing the Story

John Murphy taped interviews with many residents and assembled a radio documentary, *[[Object in the Woods Radio Documentary|Object in the Woods]]*. According to his wife and other WHJB employees, men visited the station, and the programme that aired was heavily cut. It opened with a disclaimer that no official agency had contacted the station or put any pressure on it. On air, Murphy said a woman who had described the object on tape had called back afraid of getting into trouble, and that others no longer wanted their accounts used for fear of the State Police or the Army. Stan Gordon says station employees gave him notarised statements that government men had confiscated news tapes of eyewitness accounts. The video says Murphy died in a hit-and-run not long afterwards, without ever saying publicly that he had entered the woods. In 2003 his widow said he had told her he did go in and saw a strange metallic object on the ground.

## What Came Down?

The host weighs four explanations.

::wiki-grid{cols=2}
::wiki-panel{title="A meteor (Project Blue Book)"}
Blue Book's verdict, reached under Major Quintanilla: a meteor, and no material found at the site. But Blue Book did receive fragments seen falling from the fireball, sent them to Wright-Patterson and called them chaff, the radar-confusing material aircraft drop during interdiction exercises. The host asks how chaff fits a meteor, and notes that the object's speed, turns and controlled descent do not fit one either.

::wiki-panel{title="Soviet probe Cosmos 96 (James Oberg)"}
Early sceptics proposed the failed Soviet Venus probe [[Cosmos 96]]. In a television clip, space engineer [[James Oberg]] suggests the US secretly recovered a Soviet capsule to study its heat shield and so gauge the size of Soviet ICBM warheads. A 1965 NASA release said Cosmos 96 had come down over Canada about 13 hours earlier, and US Space Command and Naval Surveillance Center records obtained by Stan Gordon put its re-entry at about 3:18 a.m. that morning.

::wiki-panel{title="A US capsule or satellite"}
Others, including an assistant chief of the Kecksburg fire department who got within 75 yards but never had a clear view, wondered about a man-made capsule such as a Gemini. At Leslie Kean's May 2000 conference, NASA orbital-debris chief scientist [[Nicholas Johnson]] said no man-made object came down over Pennsylvania that day. He said his databases would have shown even a secret experiment, and he also ruled out the film canisters dropped by [[Project Corona]] spy satellites.

A seamless bronze bell big enough for a person to stand in, with no propulsion, doors or seams, marked with unknown writing, sparking and smelling of sulphur. It was reached by firemen and civilians, then taken away under armed guard. This is the host's own leaning, on the weight of the testimony.

## The Fight for the Files

[[Leslie Kean]], later co-author of the 2017 *New York Times* article that brought the Pentagon UAP videos and the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program to public attention, took up the case in the early 2000s. Her May 2000 conference, titled "Combating Media Ridicule and Searching for Evidence on the 1965 Kecksburg Crash Case", aired Nicholas Johnson's analysis. Working with the Coalition for Freedom of Information, she then sued NASA for withholding its Kecksburg files. The October 2007 settlement required NASA to search for the records and pay all legal fees. NASA's search ended in August 2009 with 20 pages of [[Project Moondust]] documents, none of them about Kecksburg. Moondust was the Cold War programme, alongside Operation Bluefly, for recovering foreign space vehicles that had come down. A NASA public affairs statement explained what had happened to the real files. NASA had sent them to the National Archives for safekeeping two years after the incident, and in 1996 the Archives told NASA they had been marked as lost since 1987. The host calls it a very strange convenience.

The Lockbourne guard called Stan Gordon before the 1990 *Unsolved Mysteries* broadcast on Kecksburg. He said he was on the base's Air Force security team when the object arrived by truck from Pennsylvania early on 10 December and was backed into a hangar. Security was greatly tightened, and the object stayed only a short time that morning before leaving for Wright-Patterson.

[[Myron]] contacted Gordon after the broadcast. He said he felt it was now all right to talk because the story had been on television, and he was interviewed at length by Leonard Stringfield. Stringfield had his testimony videotaped by his research associate Dr. Brian Thompson and wrote in *UFO Crash Retrievals: The Inner Sanctum* (Status Report VI) that he was convinced of Myron's sincerity. The host notes Stringfield's balance: he also recorded an insider known as "Uncle", who told him Kecksburg was a crashed spy plane, yet he pursued the case for more than a decade.

Myron's account runs as follows. He and his cousin JS worked for a family-owned shale and tile brick business in Ohio. Two or three days after the crash it received a large order for "double glazed engineered brick" for Wright-Patterson. The customer was the US Navy, which sent a representative to select and inspect the bricks, and Myron says the receipts were lost when the business was sold. At the gate, a two- or three-star general in blue led the trucks to a brick warehouse reached by a narrow blacktop drive among offices and a power plant. Beside it stood an Army 35-foot tri-axle lowboy with a tarp still over its middle. Four or five personnel in white coveralls, visors, rubber gloves and boots, wearing Navy insignia and Colt .45 pistols, were "running around like chickens with their heads cut off". During a break in the unloading, Myron slipped to the door. He saw the bell under scaffolding and parachute silk, lit by a single floodlight. He asked a man with an acetylene torch what he was doing, and the man, apparently assuming Myron was cleared, said he was trying to get inside. Torches, diamond-tipped drills and acid had all failed, and he added that if there were bodies inside they might be too hot for the mortician to handle. A guard then found Myron and told him to forget what he had seen or they would lock him up and throw away the keys.

Stringfield reached JS on 3 October 1990. JS was reluctant for family reasons: he confirmed the brick delivery, the guards in protective clothing and a technician with a blowtorch, but said his partner knew more. Interviewed again from 30 March to 1 April 1991, he told a new story. He had made a solo delivery the day before Myron's and clearly remembered the Liberty Bell-shaped object upright under a tarp on the lowboy. On that first trip the gate was normal and he saw men in fatigues and armed guards, but nobody with a rifle or machine gun, unlike the next day.

## The Body Claims

The host calls bodies the most controversial part of the case. At the 2003 Crash Retrieval Conference, Stan Gordon said he had heard little-known rumours that two small bodies were found at the impact site, but that he treated them as hearsay for lack of evidence.

In 1998, in poor health, Myron sat down with Gordon for the documentary *Kecksburg: The Untold Story* and added something new. Beside the bell, on a workbench about 10 to 12 feet long, lay a body under a white sterile sheet. He put it at about 4 to 4.5 feet tall and about 80 pounds. Only a left hand showed: three fingers, with dark green or brownish, lizard-like skin. He said he had kept quiet because "Uncle Sam" had threatened to lock him up, and was speaking now because, with a bad heart, he might not live to tell it.

In late 2002, after local news covered Kean's lawsuit, a witness called Joel contacted Gordon. Gordon watched him go back and forth over whether to go on. Joel said he had hidden in the woods watching Army and Air Force men, civilians, and men in suits and hats around an object that gave off flashes of colour like electricity. An Army general climbed on top of it and struck it with a probe like an officer's billy club, and the tool flew out of his hand. A hatch at the front then swung open from right to left with a whirring, hissing sound, and inside Joel saw what looked like two fingers and an unusually long arm before it closed. The host links this to Bill Weaver's moon-suited men carrying a box on a stretcher.

> [!note] The host's doubts about Myron
> In his closing remarks the host says he first found it a red flag that Myron spoke to Gordon and Stringfield for years before mentioning a body. Until then he had repeated only the worker's remark about bodies being too hot for the mortician. He distinguishes the case from [[Robert Willingham]], who first said he saw no bodies at his 1955 crash and later changed his story, because Myron gave fear as his reason for waiting. He also leaves out witness Don Sebastian's account of a scream from the forest, since no one else in the area reported hearing it.

## Eric Walker and Penn State

[[Eric A. Walker]] recurs across the channel, and the host covers him briefly here because he did so at length elsewhere. Physicist [[Robert Sarbacher]] of the [[Research and Development Board]] had named Walker, in an interview with [[Stanton Friedman]], as a central figure in UFO crash retrieval and study alongside [[Vannevar Bush]]. Walker was president of Penn State from 1956 to 1971, chairman of the Institute for Defense Analyses, and from 1950 to 1951 executive secretary of the Research and Development Board. In the 1980s researchers including [[William Steinman]] and Armen Victorian (the pseudonym of Habib Henry Azadehdel) contacted him. He confirmed attending meetings in 1949 and 1950 about UFO crash and body retrieval, discussed the validity of [[Majestic 12]], and remarked on how hard breakthroughs in reverse engineering had been.

Speaking with Victorian, Walker said he went to the Kecksburg site with two military men who were not on duty and with a colleague. Asked whether a UFO was recovered, he said he could not comment. He would not say how long he stayed, but said none of them prepared a report. Gordon had heard of someone matching Walker's description at the scene. The host reasons that off-duty military men cannot have been the three-man Blue Book team, and asks whether Walker's party were the men in civilian clothes the witnesses saw, or even the trench-coated men who ordered the firemen out.

Walker's colleague leads the host to Penn State itself. A witness the host calls "TB" said, in alleged emails to [[Steven Greer]], that UFO unacknowledged special access programs sit inside the Defense Department's University Affiliated Research Centers. Penn State has one, the [[Applied Research Laboratory]], which Walker helped found for the Navy in 1945 by moving about 100 engineers from Harvard. According to the video, it has since won the largest research contract in Penn State's history, $2.1 billion from the Navy, and specialises in novel power and energy systems, electro-optics and directed energy. The host asks whether Walker and his colleague went to Kecksburg for a programme run from that laboratory.

## Lyndon B. Johnson and 10 December

In his 2024 book *Imminent*, [[Luis Elizondo]] named [[Lyndon B. Johnson]] among the presidents briefed on the UFO subject. Johnson's ties to Walker were close. Walker lunched at the White House with Johnson and other university presidents on 3 August 1964, and was the only university president invited to the 1967 Clean Air Act signing. The two also exchanged personal letters. On 10 September 1964 Johnson thanked Walker for his service on the Board of Visitors to the US Military Academy, and on 24 September 1964 he asked Walker to recommend a student to be honoured as a young leader. On 18 May 1966, after the crash, he thanked Walker for his work on the National Science Board and for "the unstinting manner in which you served your government in other areas". As a senator, Johnson had also tried to set up a military subcommittee to report UFO sightings to him personally, and he wrote to thank Major Donald Keyhoe, author of *The Flying Saucer Conspiracy*, for documented evidence on UFOs.

[[Grant Cameron]]'s 2024 release of his research files includes Johnson's schedule for 9 to 11 December 1965. The evening of the 9th, at the Texas White House, is leisure: dinner, a drive, the evening news. At 9:50 the next morning, about an hour after Johnson woke, a large party of guests arrived for a full day of meetings. None of the meetings mentions Kecksburg, Soviet satellites or ICBMs. The host argues that every name matters and that the roster may mean a day of briefings on the crash.

## Echoes in Other Cases

Crash accounts mostly describe saucers or eggs, so the host looks for other bells. In the "Infographics" crash-retrieval operator video, the whistleblower says the craft that crash are almost always the eggs and the bells, smaller ones he takes to be remotely operated drones. Project Blue Book has several: an acorn with coloured lights over Quincy, Louisiana on 2 December 1952; a green-glowing acorn over Massachusetts on 10 November 1956, filed as Venus; a bell that climbed out of sight over New Jersey on 23 August 1957, filed as a weather balloon; a bell over Manhattan during the November 1965 blackout; and a 7-to-8-foot bell that changed colour over Bunkie, Louisiana in 1965.

A now-deleted Reddit post by someone claiming to have worked on non-human technology until 2018 describes a stored craft "shaped like a dreidel without the handle", with a large exterior piece that went missing when it was recovered. The poster says every shape is "custom molded in a metallic material". The host sets these beside the Kecksburg bell, the pieces that fell from the fireball, and Romansky's liquid-metal mould. He also recalls [[Steven Lovekin]]'s claim that a classified part of Blue Book recovered Roswell debris. He finds three parallels for the hieroglyphs: the writing [[Jesse Marcel]] described on the Roswell debris, the writing on the lip of a crashed craft in Blue Book photographs [[Daniel Sheehan]] saw under orders from President Carter, and the markings on the recovered weapon a witness described at the [[Off-World Technologies Division]]. The failed attempts to cut into the bell remind him of "RB", who said he guarded a hamburger-shaped saucer for two weeks in 1963 while a science team tried and failed to get inside.

## What the Host Left Out

The host deliberately leaves three threads for other videos. The first is [[Die Glocke (The Bell)|Die Glocke]], the alleged Nazi "bell" wonder weapon with the same shape, and [[Nick Cook]]'s work on it. The second is Clifford Stone's affirmation of Kecksburg at [[Steven Greer]]'s 2001 National Press Club event, which he wants to assess on its own merits first. The third is Don Sebastian's scream from the woods. He says his lean is toward a genuine UAP retrieval. The case has something tangible that crashed, a controlled flight, and a wealth of testimony, and it lacks only official documents.

## Key Claims

- On 9 December 1965 thousands across six states and Canada saw a fireball that, by researchers' calculations, changed course by about 25 degrees and moved at around 17 miles a minute, far too slowly for a meteor, before coming down near Kecksburg at about 4:45 p.m.
- Independent witnesses, including Jim Romansky, Bill Bush and Jerry Betters, described a seamless bronze acorn- or bell-shaped object 10 to 12 feet long, with no doors, rivets or propulsion and a raised band of hieroglyph-like markings that Romansky said were neither Russian nor Polish.
- Soldiers with M1 rifles, airmen in blue berets, civilians giving orders and men in moon suits secured the site within the hour and took the object out under a tarp by the early morning of 10 December, despite Blue Book's claim that only three men attended.
- Project Blue Book called the object a meteor and said nothing was recovered, yet it received fragments it labelled chaff. NASA's Nicholas Johnson said Cosmos 96 could not have landed in Pennsylvania and that no man-made object came down there that day.
- John Murphy's radio documentary was censored after men visited WHJB, witnesses withdrew their accounts for fear of the Army and State Police, and Romansky's children in the military were made to sign statements about Kecksburg.
- An Air Force guard says the object passed through Lockbourne Air Force Base on 10 December, and the trucker Myron and his cousin JS say they saw it at Wright-Patterson days later. According to them, the Navy had ordered bricks for a lead-lined enclosure there, and technicians could not breach the object with torches, drills or acid.
- Myron later said he saw a three-fingered, lizard-skinned body about 4 feet tall on a workbench, and the witness Joel said he saw a hatch open to reveal fingers and a long arm.
- Eric A. Walker admitted going to the crash site with two off-duty military men and a colleague and would not say whether it was a UFO; the video asks whether Penn State's Applied Research Laboratory was involved.
- Lyndon B. Johnson, close to Walker and interested in UFOs, spent 10 December in meetings with the Joint Chiefs, the Defense Secretary, NASA's administrator, his science adviser and the AEC chairman, which the host suggests were briefings on the crash.
- After Leslie Kean's 2007 settlement, NASA produced only unrelated Moondust documents and said its Kecksburg files, sent to the National Archives in about 1967, had been lost since 1987.
