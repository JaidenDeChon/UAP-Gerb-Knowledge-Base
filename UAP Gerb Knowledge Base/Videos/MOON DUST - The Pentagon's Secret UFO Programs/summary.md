## Overview

This video follows the documentary trail of [[Project Moon Dust]], a US Air Force program whose official remit was to "locate, recover and deliver descended foreign space vehicles", and which Gerb presents as one possible Department of Defense UFO crash retrieval program. He opens with a clip of [[David Grusch]] describing the intelligence officers who confided in him, and with the claim that such programs have gone from conspiracy to "outrageous fact" through witnesses like Grusch and legislation like the [[Schumer Amendment (NDAA)|Schumer amendment]] to the 2024 NDAA. Moon Dust, he argues, is a case where the paper trail itself can be followed.

The argument has four movements. First, the official investigations were not the whole story: Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]] of [[Project Grudge]], [[Project Sign]] and [[Project Blue Book]], and Blue Book's scientific adviser Dr. [[J. Allen Hynek]], both said parallel classified programs were studying UFOs. Second, Moon Dust's own charter, in a 1961 Air Force draft policy and a 1967 review of the [[USAF 1127th Field Activities Group]], shows real collection duties with a large redacted category beside the Soviet and American space debris. Third, the field record: a 1961 sighting over Karachi, a crashed disc in Nepal in 1968, a 1976 request to Canada, pressure-vessel fragments in New Zealand in 1978 and, as Gerb's "favorite case", the [[1978 Bolivia UFO Crash]], with memos repeatedly addressed to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]]. Fourth, the denials: in 1992 the Air Force told Senators [[Jeff Bingaman]] and [[Pete Domenici]] that Moon Dust had never existed, then, faced with documents, acknowledged it, and in 1994 said its files had been destroyed.

Gerb reads those denials through the [[Wilson-Davis Memo]], in which Admiral [[Thomas Wilson]] is said to describe special access programs being reorganized in the early 1990s to hide crash retrieval. He calls the years from Blue Book's closure in 1969 to AATIP in 2008 the "Dark Ages" of officially invisible UFO investigation, and closes by rejecting the view that Moon Dust only ever collected foreign and domestic space vehicles.

## Parallel Programs

Gerb begins with the official investigations. By his account, Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]] ran the first official US government study of UFOs under [[Project Grudge]] in 1948–1949, and went on to direct [[Project Sign]] and [[Project Blue Book]]. As director, Ruppelt concluded that UFOs had an "interplanetary explanation", and on 24 April 1952 he suggested that programs parallel to the official investigation were "conducting a more complete investigation". Dr. [[J. Allen Hynek]], Blue Book's scientific adviser (whom Gerb covered on layer two of his iceberg video), said the same: alongside Blue Book, other classified programs were studying UFOs. One of them, Gerb suggests, could have been an inconspicuous crash retrieval program that "unless you look hard enough you'll never know existed".

## Moon Dust's Charter

The first mention of Moon Dust is in a 1961 US Air Force intelligence document that Gerb reads as the "AFC 1e draft policy" (the Air Force's AFCIN-1E memo, dated 3 November 1961). It proposes that the [[USAF 1127th Field Activities Group]] receive collection duties for the USAF's established program for investigating "reliably reported unidentified flying objects" within the United States, and names one project that had historically held that duty: Moon Dust, with authority to "locate, recover and deliver descended foreign space vehicles".

A 1967 review for the Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence, detailing the 1127th's functions, shows that Moon Dust was more than a proposal. That year it collected 49 fallen Soviet space objects and 17 US ones, and the tally is followed by a large redacted portion of the memo. So Moon Dust, Gerb concludes, had real collection responsibilities for Soviet, American and *redacted* space vehicles.

In Nepal the paper trail runs longer than anywhere but Bolivia. A defense intelligence memo monitors the crashed disc; four months later a State Department cable titled "Moon Dust", sent to the [[Defense Intelligence Agency|DIA]], the 1127th and Wright-Patterson, calls a diplomatic visit to Kathmandu a "fishing expedition for info on space objects"; further cables show the Royal Nepalese Army showing US officers photographs of the objects. A cover story of a crashed satellite followed, which Gerb sets against [[NORAD]] and [[NASA]] data showing a 100% successful satellite launch rate in 1968. "Unfortunately the trail stops here," he says: he cannot say whether US entities recovered the disc. See [[1968 Nepal Circular Disc Crash]].

The Canadian memo asked for photos on behalf of the USAF [[Air Force Foreign Technology Division|Foreign Technology Division]] at Wright-Patterson and the CIA's Office of Weapons Intelligence. Gerb notes in passing that Canada has historically been open about investigating UFOs and sharing analysis with its Five Eyes partners, a subject he leaves for another video. The New Zealand fragments' shape reminds him of Commander [[David Fravor]]'s [[2004 Nimitz UAP Encounter (Tic Tac)|Tic Tac]], which Fravor likened to a propane tank or pressure vessel with no inlets or outlets.

### The Wright-Patterson connection

The Nepal cable and the Canadian memo were both written to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]], and Gerb asks viewers to notice it. Wright-Patterson, he says (recalling another entry on layer two of his iceberg), is often seen as the hub where UFO crash retrievals are stored: the [[Roswell Crash|Roswell]] materials were allegedly sent there, and Senator [[Barry Goldwater]] asked Wright-Patterson officials to see the room where "it" was stored and was laughed off and told never to ask again.

## The 1978 Bolivia Crash

Most countries, Gerb says, have their own Roswell story; he cites Canada's [[Shag Harbour, Nova Scotia|Shag Harbour]]. Bolivia's is his favourite Moon Dust case, brought to his attention by the work of the [[UAP Caucus]], whose members include Representatives [[Tim Burchett]], [[Anna Paulina Luna]], [[Eric Burlison]] and [[Representative Moskowitz|Jared Moskowitz]].

On 6 May 1978 at 4:15 p.m., thousands of people near El Taire, Bolivia, saw a cylindrical object crash into a mountainside. Its sonic boom was heard up to 150 miles away and cracked window panes within a 30-mile radius. Army Corporal [[Natalio Ruiz]] saw it fly over his head: it resembled "a gigantic wine container emitting a trace of white smoke". Bolivian soldiers and three jets went to check the wreckage, and the local military and police were quoted as saying they had found and inspected the object but had no further instructions: "a dull metallic cylinder 12 feet long with a few dents", contents unknown, with technical commissions and a NASA expert expected the next morning.

## The Dark Ages

Gerb's explanation for the denials comes from his earlier video on the [[Wilson-Davis Memo]]. There, Admiral [[Thomas Wilson]] is said to describe special access programs being reorganized in the early 1990s to hide crash retrieval and reverse engineering inside "SAP-X" compartments, making them inaccessible to everybody. The senators asked for Moon Dust's records in the mid-1990s, so Gerb asks whether the denials and the destruction of records followed from that reorganization, and answers: "I think so." He finds it quite plausible that the Air Force destroyed most of Moon Dust's files to hide a UFO crash retrieval program.

He frames the whole stretch from 1969 to 2008, from Project Blue Book to [[AATIP (Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program)|AATIP]], as the "Dark Ages": a completely hidden era of UFO investigation with no official US government investigation group, in which the Air Force kept denying Moon Dust. "That is where our paper trail ends."
