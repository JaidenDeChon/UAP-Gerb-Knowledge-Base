## Overview

This video is UAP Gerb's full investigation of the [[Aztec UFO Crash and Recovery|1948 Aztec, New Mexico UFO crash retrieval]], a case Gerb describes as "debunked, labeled as a hoax several times over, but yet still refuses to die." According to the accounts he assembles, on the morning of 25 March 1948 a disc 99.9 feet across came down on a rocky mesa off Hart Canyon Road, about 12 miles northeast of [[Aztec, New Mexico]], only eight months after the [[Roswell Crash]]. Oil-field workers, ranchers and two policemen reached it first and saw small, charred bodies through a punctured porthole before a seasoned military team sealed the site, swore everyone to silence, and spent up to two weeks dismantling the craft and trucking it to [[Los Alamos National Laboratory]]. The video opens on [[Ross Coulthart]]'s account of former Navy chief R&D scientist Nat Kobitz, who reportedly implied that Roswell, Aztec and Kingman were all real recoveries.

Gerb builds the case on six sources: [[Frank Scully]]'s 1950 bestseller *Behind the Flying Saucers*, which introduced the story; [[William Steinman]]'s rare 1986 book *UFO Crash at Aztec: A Well Kept Secret*, which named an alleged recovery team; [[Scott and Suzanne Ramsey]]'s 2015 *The Aztec UFO Incident*, which found and interviewed new witnesses; and [[Leonard Stringfield]]'s *UFO Crash/Retrieval Status Reports*. Against them he weighs the two main debunks: [[J. P. Cahn]]'s 1952 exposé "The Flying Saucers and the Mysterious Little Men" and Mike McClellan's 1975 "The UFO Crash of 1948 Is a Hoax."

Most of the video re-examines why Aztec came to be called a hoax. Scully's source was the Denver oilman [[Silas Newton]], who never claimed to have been at the site and said he was repeating what a group of scientists, "Dr. G," had told him. Cahn cast Newton's associate [[Leo GeBauer]] as the one and only Dr. G, and the two men's 1953 fraud conviction over an oil-finding "doodlebug" buried the story for decades. Gerb argues, following the Ramseys and Steinman, that the prosecution looks like targeted retaliation for the leak. He points to [[Air Force Office of Special Investigations|AFOSI]] questioning the man who recorded Newton's 1950 lecture, an Army CID and FBI sting to intercept photographs of the disc, and a trial in which 32 of 33 investors were satisfied and the prosecution's expert tested a surplus-store stand-in rather than GeBauer's device. He then tries to name the Dr. G scientists: Denver geophysicist [[Carl A. Heiland]], whom Steinman calls the original leaker and an alleged [[Majestic 12]] member, and wartime magnetic-detection scientists tied to [[Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)|Geophysical Service Inc.]] and [[Vannevar Bush]]'s wartime research office.

The final hour turns to the named eyewitnesses; [[Robert Sarbacher]] and [[Eric A. Walker]], whom Scully's book indirectly exposed as insiders; the [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] that Steinman says recovered the disc; and the case's revival by [[Robert Spencer Carr]] and Stringfield. Gerb ends convinced that "this case likely happened," while allowing that, as with Roswell, details of more than one crash may have been blended together.

## The Crash and the Retrieval

### New Mexico in 1948

Gerb sets the scene in a state thick with atomic weapons work and the radar that guarded it. [[Los Alamos National Laboratory|Los Alamos]], [[White Sands Missile Range|White Sands]] and [[Sandia National Laboratories|Sandia]] were all ringed by high-powered installations against Soviet bombers, and Gerb places the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]'s green-fireball study, [[Project Twinkle]], in the same period. Steinman's sources said three separate Southwest radar sites tracked the Aztec object, about 60 miles from Los Alamos. Why it came down is unknown. Like [[Philip J. Corso]], who blamed radar for the Roswell crash, several researchers suspect radar interference. The Ramseys' contact Lee Crane recalled Air Force radar men at a local diner in 1950 bringing in half-cooked ducks they said had been knocked out of the sky by the station's microwave radar. Gerb links the idea to his earlier claim that [[Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI)|SDI]] weapons were secretly turned against UFOs.

### The craft and its crew

The accounts agree on an enormous disc, 99.9 feet across, resting almost undamaged at an angle on the mesa. It had no rivets, bolts, welds, landing gear or visible propulsion. At its centre sat a crew cabin about 18 feet across and 6 feet high. A helicopter circled overhead, and uniformed men in Army-style jeeps soon arrived. In Scully's version, as told by Newton, investigators pushed a rod through an already punctured porthole of semi-translucent material and prodded the instrument panel until a panel opened. Inside, two small bodies were slumped over a panel about four feet high, and 12 to 14 more lay in a separate cabin. Reports vary between 12 and 16 bodies in all.

Steinman adds names. According to his sources, the impact area was relayed to General [[George C. Marshall]], who called in the [[Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit]] from Camp Hale, Colorado, and Vannevar Bush, the alleged head of Majestic 12. Bush's team is said to have included [[J. Robert Oppenheimer]], [[John Von Neumann]] and [[Detlev Bronk]]; four of its members have been named as original Majestic 12 members. Wall screens carried glyph-like writing, and a plastic "book" of the same glyphs was passed by Marshall to the cryptologists William F. Friedman and Lambros D. Callimahos, who could not decipher it. Bronk had the bodies put on dry ice, with freezing equipment from the AiResearch company, whose R&D director, cryogenics specialist Dr. Paul A. Scherer, was a Bush colleague. Gerb notes that Scherer later joined a 1954 AAAS board that argued against scientific secrecy. Bronk's autopsies, supposedly published in the elusive Project Sign/Blue Book Report 13, described beings of about 40 pounds with large heads, webbed fingers, no digestive or reproductive systems and a colourless, ozone-smelling fluid for blood. Gerb notes that this closely matches "EBE-1" in the disputed Majestic 12 *Special Operations Manual*. Scully's beings, by contrast, were near-perfect humanoids with perfect teeth.

### Taking the disc apart

With no helicopter able to lift a 99.9-foot disc, the recovery took up to two weeks. The scientists found that the outer ring rotated around a stabilised central cabin and that the craft was built in segments that fitted into grooves and were pinned together around the base. It came apart as the crew cabin and three equal sections. In Steinman's telling, cutting torches, diamond drills and diamond saws failed to mark the metal until a scientist found an interlocking key inside; pulling it opened a seam from top to bottom, and more keys at equal intervals gave them the method. Concrete pads, which Gerb says are still there, were poured for three M25 Dragon Wagon tank transporters. The Ramseys found 1948 [[Bureau of Reclamation]] records of heavy earth-moving equipment, cranes and lowboys in the Aztec area. The convoy avoided roads on its way to Los Alamos, where the craft stayed for over a year; the bodies likely went to [[Wright-Patterson Air Force Base]].

## Scully, Newton and "Dr. G"

*Behind the Flying Saucers* (1950), which grew out of Scully's 1949 *Variety* columns, reported the Aztec recovery, four recoveries in all up to 1950, and magnetic propulsion. It also started a separate chain of events: Wilbert B. Smith's questions to Robert Sarbacher (see below). Scully's source was Silas M. Newton of the Newton Oil Company, who claimed no first-hand role in the recovery. His information came from "Dr. G," a group of up to eight scientists with, in Scully's phrase, "more degrees than a thermometer." Dr. G told Scully the craft was recovered intact, taken to Los Alamos and ran on magnetism. They showed him a cube that made radio-like sounds, small gears and discs said to come from the craft, and photographs of the disc, which Scully's widow said she had also seen.

Newton had spoken publicly before the book appeared. On 8 March 1950, billed as "Scientist X," he took the place of the original speaker at a University of Denver lecture. Radio salesman and engineer [[George Koehler]] of KMYR helped set it up, and the Ramseys found the recording and Newton's chalkboard drawings in Scully's archive. The reaction was swift. ### In Newton's own words

Newton's 1954 unfinished autobiography, found by the Ramseys in Scully's archive, shows a man with a deep interest in the history of magnetism, convinced saucers ran on magnetic principles and full of admiration for Wilbert B. He insisted he had always said the story was told to him and could produce "dozens of people" who had heard it from the same source, yet he, not Scully, had been branded its author and then "framed up" in court. A separate 1950 wire recording in the University of Washington archive has Newton describing magnetic research in the Mojave in summer 1949 with "the top magnetic scientist of the United States." That scientist said he had passed Aztec on a drive from Denver to Phoenix and wished he could show Newton where the first saucer had landed. Gerb believes he found this recording, or one very like it, dated 3 March 1950, and plays Newton's voice: the ship was tracked, Air Force officials flew out of Durango within two to three hours, and about seven of "our magnetic group of scientists" were called in to examine it.

## The Doodlebug and the Search for Dr. G

### Steinman's theory: Heiland

Steinman went further. He concluded that Dr. [[Carl A. Heiland]], the Hamburg-born geophysicist who built the Colorado School of Mines geophysics department and founded a Denver instrument company, was both a real Dr. G and an MJ-12 member who was at the Aztec site. Heiland worked with magnetometers for oil exploration and for wartime submarine detection. On Steinman's account, he and GeBauer were research partners who adapted wartime magnetic airborne detection gear to finding oil, gas and water, and had even lived and worked together on a 1935 government project in Antarctica. Heiland was the original, unintentional leaker to Newton and GeBauer. Once MJ-12 found out, Heiland was pulled from the March 1950 lecture, where Newton stood in for him. He was later asked to testify against GeBauer's devices and refused, saying he could not do so with a clear conscience because they had developed the devices together; his colleague Meredith testified instead. Researcher Richard Ogden independently arrived at Heiland in an unpublished 1960s manuscript, reasoning from Scully's remark that one Dr. G lived in Denver and could fly to the site in two or three hours. Gerb notes that Steinman gives no evidence for the Antarctica partnership, nor for his claim that GeBauer worked at AiResearch in Phoenix and Los Angeles, although the Ramseys separately found that GeBauer held senior positions at the AiResearch laboratories around 1946.

### The GSI thread

Gerb's own contribution is to follow the doodlebug into wartime science. [[Geophysical Service Inc. (GSI)|Geophysical Service Inc.]] was founded in 1930 by reflection-seismograph inventor J. Clarence Karcher and Eugene McDermott. After Pearl Harbor it built airborne magnetic submarine detectors for the Navy and worked under Bush's Office of Scientific Research and Development, and in 1951 it became Texas Instruments, which still calls its early field crews "doodlebuggers." Scully had written that some of the Dr. G scientists worked out how Japanese submarines were detected magnetically, and Newton's manuscript says he knew pre-war oil scientists who moved into secret wartime work for Bush. To Gerb, GSI is the kind of workplace that fits one or several Dr. Gs, and GeBauer's device was probably an early magnetic anomaly detector (MAD) rather than a fraud.

## Steinman's Reconstruction and Eric Henry Wang

Gerb admires Steinman's 1986 book, bought a copy of the 1,000-copy printing for the video, and stresses that its account came from sources unconnected to Scully and Newton, so it is not circular reporting. It differs mainly in how the craft was opened: a scientist saw a knob-shaped button through a porthole, the team beat the porthole glass out with hammers, and then used a long pole to open the ship.

> [!warning] Gerb's reservation about Steinman
> Steinman states as fact that the IPU recovered the disc, that Marshall got the call and that Bush, Oppenheimer, Bronk, Scherer and Heiland were on site, but he rarely gives his sources or any corroborating evidence. Gerb calls this his "biggest critique" of an otherwise excellent book.

The book's most lasting lead, for Gerb, is Dr. [[Eric Henry Wang]]. Steinman wrote that Wang joined the project in November 1949, a year and a half after the recovery. An Austrian-born engineer who had taught in Cincinnati, Wang consulted for the Wright Air Development Center on aerodynamic and metallurgical design and later headed its Office of Special Studies. In 1956 his department moved to the Sandia complex at [[Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico|Kirtland Air Force Base]], where he stayed until his death (his obituary ran in *Mechanical Engineering* in April 1961). [[Arthur Stansel Jr.]] placed Wang at the head of the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval|1953 Kingman]] recovery during [[Operation Upshot-Knothole]]. Gerb plays a clip in which a Kingman source says, of a door on a recovered craft, "Oh, yeah. That's the one from Kingman." An Albuquerque contact of Steinman's, "MW," said Wang's papers were seized by military intelligence at his death and locked in a secret section of the Kirtland library. MW never answered Steinman's letters, and Steinman found his mail tampered with and a tracking device on his van.

George's account also explains why records do not help: people on such projects were officially placed elsewhere, "in Florida" or "in the infirmary for a sprained ankle," so that anyone who talked later could be contradicted from his own file. Gerb notes one awkward point: if Heiland really was at Aztec, George's record-keeping failed, because the Ramseys could place Heiland in the Southwest at the time.

## Sarbacher and Walker: Insiders Found Through the Book

Gerb argues that one of the book's most important effects was to expose two insiders. In 1950 [[Wilbert B. Smith]] wrote a top-secret memo to Canada's Department of Transport saying that saucers exist, that the subject was classified higher than the H-bomb, and that a small group under Vannevar Bush was studying them. The memo was downgraded in 1969 and found by [[Arthur Bray]] and [[Stanton Friedman]] in 1978. Smith's source was [[Robert Sarbacher]], a physicist and consultant to Bush's Research and Development Board, whom Smith interviewed on 15 September 1950 precisely to ask how much of Scully's book was true. "The facts reported in the book are substantially correct," Sarbacher replied. Smith went on to lead Canada's Projects Magnet and Second Story. Decades later Sarbacher told Steinman that recovery meetings were held at Wright-Patterson with Bush, von Neumann and Oppenheimer, and that the materials were "extremely light and very tough." He said he had the impression the occupants were "constructed like certain insects." He told Friedman in 1983 that one man had attended every meeting: an RDB member who wrote a book on electrical engineering and headed the electrical engineering department at a university in Pennsylvania.

Steinman traced that man to [[Eric A. Walker]], Penn State president, founder of its Applied Research Laboratory, and the RDB's executive secretary from 1950 to 1952. Friedman found an early-1950s Army document on "indoctrination for special intelligence for Mr. Eric Arthur Walker," and Walker later worked on Project Azorian. His replies to Steinman were strange: a cut-up letter reading "Stop. Don't try and find me," then, in a 30 August 1987 phone call, "Yes, I attended meetings concerning that subject matter," along with the admission that he had known of MJ-12 for 40 years. He told Henry Azadehdel he had been at the [[1965 Kecksburg, Pennsylvania Crash Retrieval|1965 Kecksburg recovery]]. Gerb believes Walker, who joined the Research and Development Board a year after Aztec, knew a great deal about it.

## The Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit

The IPU is central to Steinman's reconstruction, so Gerb reviews the evidence for it, drawing on [[Robert Wood]] and Ryan Wood and on researcher Anthony Bragalia.

Richard Hall (1980), Steinman (1984) and Timothy Good (1987) all got the same answer from the Army. An IPU of the scientific and technical branch of the counterintelligence directorate had existed, and was disestablished in the late 1950s. Its records were "surrendered" to AFOSI with Project Blue Book. It was an "in-house project" and interest item for an assistant chief of staff for intelligence, with no formal mission, and is known only through "institutional memory." AFOSI later told CUFON the material had been destroyed, which contradicts the Army's statement that there were no records.

Timothy Good and the Woods point higher than MacArthur, to George C. Marshall, whom Steinman says mobilised the IPU for Aztec. Majestic documents include a 4 July 1947 IPU field order and a 22 July 1947 IPU report on Roswell, both delivered to [[Tim Cooper]] in the mid-1990s.

The 22 July 1947 report, if genuine, closely matches Steinman's Aztec account, which appeared nine years before it surfaced. It describes multiple objects recovered on 4–6 July 1947, a Sandia radiobiological team with an AFSWP security detail, AEC scientists including Oppenheimer, Bronk in charge of autopsies, recovered craft stored at Los Alamos under a Lieutenant General Collins, and Marshall as the only cabinet member who knew. It also mentions three Sandia technicians killed in a containment accident, which Gerb has linked to the 1974 Coyame case. Gerb concludes that the unit existed but its records have been destroyed. He cannot lean on the disputed documents for anything definitive, but the parallels between Roswell and Aztec, eight months apart, are striking.

## Stringfield, Carr and the Case's Revival

[[Leonard Stringfield]] wrote the foreword to Steinman's book and admitted he too had been conditioned to see Aztec as a hoax, taken in by Cahn and by [[Bill Moore]]'s debunk at a 1985 MUFON symposium. Moore later admitted, at the 1989 MUFON symposium, to working with the intelligence community, with [[Richard Doty]] of AFOSI at Kirtland as his go-between. Stringfield's interest had first been revived by Robert Spencer Carr, who went public in October 1974 with information he said came from five sources, including a senior Air Force officer involved in the retrieval. Carr said the craft and bodies were flown to Edwards and then Wright-Patterson. He got several details wrong, and Mike McClellan's 1975 debunk knocked the case down again. On 6 February 1982 Carr privately named his source to Stringfield. By Status Report 5 (1989) Stringfield was mentioning Aztec alongside Roswell and Kingman, and Status Report 6 (1991) reproduced the Farmington report, whose witness had seen classified files with a photograph of an intact, brushed-aluminium disc lying tilted in the desert, with no crater.

## Gerb's Assessment

Speaking to camera at the end, Gerb explains why he took up a case he had once doubted in his Sarbacher video: people he respects, and Coulthart's account of Nat Kobitz, persuaded him there was more to it than Cahn's debunk. He wagers that the crash "likely happened" but is complicated, and suggests that, as with Roswell, details of several cases may have merged. Carr and the Farmington report both describe a saucer of about 30 feet rather than 99.9. He singles out what he considers tangible: GSI's documented path from oil-finding instruments to Bush's submarine-detection work, the 1950 CID/FBI sting, and Hoover's interest in Newton. He also points to Meredith, from Heiland's own department, testifying on surplus-store parts. He says the Steinman–Heiland thesis would be far stronger with proof that GeBauer worked for Heiland's company or for Scherer, and he recommends the Ramseys' book above all.

> [!note] The 2017 document Gerb set aside
> Gerb holds up a 45-page Majestic document, presented as a DIA report and released in 2017 through a radio host from a "trusted source," which tells the Aztec story almost exactly as Steinman does, IPU and all, alongside strange material about Nikola Tesla. He left it out of the analysis. His working theory is that it was released as UFO interest revived in 2017 to muddy the waters, by making it look as though Steinman had copied an old document. He also floats, without support, the idea that Steinman may have seen the IPU documents privately in the 1980s.

## Key Claims

- A disc 99.9 feet across allegedly crashed almost intact on a mesa off Hart Canyon Road, about 12 miles northeast of Aztec, New Mexico, on 25 March 1948, with between 12 and 16 small humanoid bodies aboard.
- Named civilian witnesses (Valentine Archuleta, Doug Noland, Manuel Sandoval and Ken Farley) described the same seamless, dull-aluminium craft, a punctured porthole, bodies at a control panel, and a fast, seasoned military response that swore them to silence.
- A USAF airman known as "George" said the retrieval was run from Walker Air Force Base (formerly Roswell Army Airfield), took two weeks, involved about 200 people, and applied lessons from the Roswell "screw-up."
- According to Steinman, George C. Marshall mobilised the Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit and Vannevar Bush's scientific team, including Detlev Bronk, John von Neumann, J. Robert Oppenheimer and Carl A. Heiland. Eric Henry Wang joined in November 1949.
- The craft was allegedly dismantled into a crew cabin and three sections using interlocking keys, trucked to Los Alamos, and held there for over a year. Gerb compares this to Sumner Shapiro's account of craft disassembled in an exact sequence.
- AFOSI questioned George Koehler the day after Newton's 8 March 1950 lecture, and in September 1950 Army CID and the FBI ran a sting to intercept photographs of the Aztec disc.
- J. P. Cahn, who had failed to buy the story, named Leo GeBauer as the sole "Dr. G" without evidence, corresponded with J. Edgar Hoover about Newton, found the complainant Herman Flader and assisted the prosecution.
- Newton and GeBauer's 1953 fraud trial relied on a surplus-store stand-in for GeBauer's device while 32 of 33 investors were satisfied. They were convicted but never sentenced, and Gerb and the Ramseys see the trial as retaliation for the leak.
- Steinman theorised that Carl A. Heiland was the original leaker and a Majestic 12 member who refused to testify against GeBauer. Gerb traces the doodlebug to Geophysical Service Inc.'s wartime magnetic anomaly detection work under Vannevar Bush.
- Scully's book led Wilbert B. Smith to Robert Sarbacher, who called it "substantially correct" and, indirectly, to Eric A. Walker, who confirmed attending crash-retrieval meetings at Wright-Patterson.
- The US Army has acknowledged through FOIA that an Interplanetary Phenomenon Unit existed and was disestablished in the late 1950s, with its records surrendered to AFOSI.
