The **Atomic Energy Act of 1954** is the main U.S. federal statute governing civilian and military uses of nuclear energy and materials. It replaced the Atomic Energy Act of 1946 and set the legal framework for nuclear weapons development, nuclear power, and the control of nuclear information. The Act carried over the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC), which the 1946 Act had created and which was later reorganized into the [[Department of Energy]] and the [[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]]. It also defined the federal government's authority over "special nuclear material" (enriched uranium and plutonium) and related technologies.

> [!edit] Corrected 16 October 2026
> Earlier versions said the 1954 Act created the Atomic Energy Commission. The Commission dates from the 1946 Act; the 1954 Act (68 Stat. 919, section 21) continued it.

## Restricted Data Classification

For national security and oversight, the Act's most important feature is the "Restricted Data" classification category it created. Most national security information, across all other agencies, is classified under presidential Executive Orders. Restricted Data classification under the Atomic Energy Act works differently:

- It is established by statute rather than executive decree, so a president cannot unilaterally declassify it.
- It is "born classified": information about nuclear weapons design, production processes and special nuclear materials is classified automatically by operation of law, even if no one has ever formally reviewed or stamped it.
- It is exempt from the standard FOIA processes that apply to other federal records.
- It stays Restricted Data until the Commission (today the Department of Energy) declassifies it: section 142 gives that power to the agency, not to Congress. For data relating mainly to the military use of atomic weapons, the Commission and the Department of Defense decide jointly, and the President decides if they disagree (section 142 c.).

> [!edit] Corrected 16 October 2026
> Earlier versions said Restricted Data could leave the classification system only by specific congressional action. Section 142(a) of the Act has the Commission itself determine what can be declassified.

> [!edit] Corrected 16 October 2026
> Earlier versions said section 142 gives the declassification power to the agency and not to the President. Section 142 c. has the Commission and the Department of Defense decide jointly on Restricted Data relating primarily to the military utilization of atomic weapons, with the President deciding when they do not agree.

A related category, Transclassified Foreign Nuclear Information (TFNI), covers information about nuclear programs that foreign governments originally classified and that has been incorporated into U.S. classified holdings.

President Eisenhower signed the Act into law on August 30, 1954. In UAP Gerb's macro-timeline of the "UFO Legacy Programs," this signing is treated as arguably the single most consequential moment in the history of the alleged secrecy apparatus. The timeline presents the Act as the primary legal container that has allowed UFO-related material to be hidden from the federal government's own executive-order classification system since that date.

## Significance in UAP Research

The Atomic Energy Act's Restricted Data provisions come up often in UAP research because they are one of the only statutory mechanisms that can place government programs and materials beyond the reach of both presidential oversight and standard congressional access. Researchers argue that materials from alleged UAP crash retrievals, particularly those with anomalous radiological properties or involving nuclear-adjacent technologies, could have been absorbed into DOE classification under the Act's "born classified" provisions from the late 1940s onward. In their view, this created an oversight gap that has persisted for decades.

The [[Department of Energy]]'s ability to classify UAP-related activities as Restricted Data, together with the operational presence of the [[Office of Intelligence and Counterintelligence]] (OICI) at DOE national laboratories, is described as the core legal mechanism that lets alleged UAP legacy programs at [[Sandia National Laboratories]], [[Oak Ridge National Laboratory]], and other [[Federally Funded Research and Development Center (FFRDC)|DOE FFRDCs]] operate outside standard congressional intelligence oversight.

## Special Nuclear Material and UFO Classification

Section 51 of the Act lets the Commission add to "special nuclear material" (plutonium and enriched uranium) any other material it finds "capable of releasing substantial quantities of atomic energy", with the President's written assent. Researchers argue that this is broad enough to cover recovered non-human materials with radiological properties. [[David Grusch]] has said that legacy programs appear to have exploited this vague definition to classify UFO materials through nuclear secrecy channels: "They're basically treating this as nuclear secrets because it gives off nuclear radiation... what legal gymnastics are you saying this stuff... is a US nuclear secret? You're transclassifying it into a nuclear secret." The Schumer UAP disclosure amendment (UAPDA) states that UAP records have been withheld under exemptions in the Atomic Energy Act and through "an over-broad interpretation of 'transclassified foreign nuclear information'" (TFNI).

## Origins in Manhattan Project Practice

The restricted data concept is older than the 1946 Atomic Energy Act. It began as an informal wartime practice of [[Leslie Groves]], who held that certain nuclear physics information was classified by its very nature, whether or not anyone had formally reviewed or stamped it. Groves' informal "born classified" approach was first codified in the 1946 Atomic Energy Act and expanded in 1954. The classification framework protecting UFO legacy programs therefore traces directly to a wartime security improvisation by the Manhattan Project's director.

## Presidential Executive Orders Cannot Override Statutory Classification

Gerb's investigation emphasizes a practical consequence of the dual-track US classification system, in which some information is classified by executive order and some by statute under the Atomic Energy Act. A presidential executive order to declassify or release UFO files has no legal force over materials covered by DOE restricted data or TFNI. Former analyst [[Dylan Borland]] has stated that while working near DTRA (Defense Threat Reduction Agency), he saw old Atomic Energy Commission files, protected under this statutory authority, that concluded some UFOs are of extraterrestrial origin. In Gerb's account, an executive order alone could not declassify these materials.
