"Now You See It, Now You Don't!" is a short classified article by Captain Henry S. Shields of US Air Forces in Europe headquarters (HQ USAFE/INOMP), retelling the [[1976 Tehran UFO Incident]] for a military readership. It recounts how two F-4 Phantom crews of the Imperial Iranian Air Force chased a brilliant object over Tehran in September 1976, and lost their instruments, weapons and radios as they closed on it. It follows the defense attaché's report of the night, the [[DIA Tehran UFO Report (1976)]], paragraph by paragraph. The [[NSA]] released it with its UFO files.

## What it says

The article opens with a short preface: sometime in his career "each pilot can expect to encounter strange, unusual happenings which will never be adequately or entirely explained by logic or subsequent investigation." This is one such episode, it says, reported by two F-4 crews "during late 1976". No more information or explanation has come, and the story "will be filed away and probably forgotten, but it makes interesting, and possibly disturbing, reading."

The account that follows tracks the attaché's report closely, in plainer language:

- **The calls.** At 0030 on "a clear autumn morning", the Imperial Iranian Air Force (IIAF) command post in the Tehran area received four calls from one of the city's suburbs about strange airborne objects, "bird-like" or like brightly lit helicopters, though none were airborne. Unable to convince the callers they were seeing stars, "a senior officer" went outside, saw an object to the north like a star, "only larger and brighter", and scrambled an F-4. The article doesn't name him; the attaché's report identifies him as Brigadier General Yousefi.
- **The first F-4.** The pilot could see the object 70 miles away. At about 25 nautical miles he lost all instrumentation and UHF and intercom communications. When he broke off and turned for home, everything came back, "as if the strange object no longer regarded the aircraft as a threat."
- **The second F-4.** Scrambled ten minutes after the first, its backseater got a radar lock at 27 nautical miles, 12 o'clock high, closing at 150 knots. At 25 nautical miles the object moved away to keep its distance. Its radar return was comparable to a KC-135's (the attaché's report says a 707 tanker). It looked like strobe lights in a rectangle, flashing blue, green, red and orange so fast that all the colours showed at once.
- **The second object.** South of Tehran, a second bright object, a half to a third the size of the moon, detached and came straight at the jet. The pilot tried to fire an AIM-9 but his weapons control panel lost power, and his UHF and internal communications failed with it. He turned into a negative-G dive; the object fell in behind him at 3 to 4 nautical miles, turned inside him, then went back to the main UFO "for a perfect rendezvous".
- **The third object.** Another object then dropped straight toward the ground. The crew, their systems restored, expected an explosion, but it "landed gently and cast a bright light over a two-three kilometer area." The pilot flew as low as he could to fix its position.
- **The landing.** Both crewmen struggled to adjust their night vision for landing. Passing a magnetic bearing of 150 degrees from the base, they had heavy UHF interference and lost all communications, and the inertial navigation system swung between 30 and 50 degrees. A civil airliner nearby lost communications too but saw nothing. On long final, a cylinder-shaped object about the size of a T-33 trainer, with steady lights at each end and a flasher in the middle, passed directly over the F-4. The tower had no other traffic, but saw it when told where to look.
- **The next day.** A helicopter took the crew to where they thought the object had landed, a dry lake bed with nothing unusual. Circling west, they picked up "a very noticeable beeper signal" and traced it to a house, whose occupants spoke of loud noises and "a very bright light, like lightning". Radiation checks and other tests were arranged near the house, but "the results of such tests have not been reported."

Where the attaché's report gives names, places and times (Shemiran, Shahrokhi, Mehrabad, take-offs at 0130 and 0140), the article mostly drops them. It adds no facts the report lacks, and it says nothing about satellites.

## The copy

The NSA's file is three pages, scanned in October 1997, with the article's own page numbers 32 and 33 on the first two, so it was printed in a periodical. The copy has no masthead or date. The office symbol in the byline is printed in a typeface whose N and H look alike; the NSA lists it as INOMP, "IN" being the usual Air Force symbol for an intelligence directorate. Later accounts identify the journal as the *MIJI Quarterly*, a journal on meaconing, intrusion, jamming and interference, in its October 1978 issue. The article was classified Confidential: the marking at the top and bottom of each page is struck through, and each paragraph's portion marking is blacked out. Each page is stamped "Declassify on: 4 Dec 81", by the Assistant Chief of Staff, Intelligence, HQ USAF (written "ACS/I, HQ USAF"). The scan's own text layer was unreadable, so the hosted copy has been OCR'd afresh, and a checked [[Now You See It, Now You Don't (Transcription)|transcription]] gives the full text.

## In Gerb's videos

In [[Videos/FASTWALKERS – UFOs Outside Earth/summary|FASTWALKERS – UFOs Outside Earth]], Gerb says that after "a long and fruitless venture" to confirm [[Lee Graham]] and [[Ron Regehr]]'s claim that a DSP satellite tracked the Tehran object, he found nothing besides "a declassified paper often referenced by" Regehr, titled "Now You See It, Now You Don't", by "USAF Captain Harry S Shields". The byline is Henry S. Shields. Gerb doesn't show or quote the article. It retells the jets' encounter and doesn't mention DSP or any satellite, so it gives no support to the printout claim.
