*DOD Special Access Programs: Administrative Due Process Not Provided When Access Is Denied or Revoked* (GAO/NSIAD-93-162, B-247246) is a 38-page report that the General Accounting Office issued in May 1993; its covering letter is dated May 5, 1993. It went to three House chairmen who had asked for it: William L. Clay of the Committee on Post Office and Civil Service, Frank McCloskey of its Subcommittee on the Civil Service, and Don Edwards of the Judiciary Committee's Subcommittee on Civil and Constitutional Rights. Donna M. Heivilin, GAO's director of Defense Management and NASA Issues, signed it.

The report is about personnel security, not program budgets or contracts. It asks whether the Army, Navy and Air Force gave people a fair hearing when they took away, or refused to grant, someone's access to a [[Special Access Programs (SAPs)|special access program]] (SAP) or to sensitive compartmented information (SCI). It followed a May 1992 GAO report on due process for security clearances.

## What it says

- **SAP access.** DoD's Personnel Security Program Regulation, DoD 5200.2-R, generally requires that a person be told in writing, with reasons, that a denial or revocation is being considered, be allowed to answer, and be allowed to appeal. GAO found the Navy and Air Force gave none of this in any SAP case they showed it: 30 Navy cases drawn at random from a list of 70, and all 26 the Air Force supplied. The people affected were not even told their access had been removed. The Army followed the regulation in the three of its six cases it had been able to adjudicate, but GAO said six cases were too few to conclude it complied overall.
- **DoD's answer.** DoD officials told GAO the regulation was never meant to cover covert or unacknowledged SAPs. GAO replied that the regulation contains no such limit.
- **Unacknowledged programs kept back.** DoD was reluctant to hand over cases from unacknowledged programs, which it said made up about 10 to 20 percent of all SAPs. GAO agreed to review only acknowledged programs, and the services said unacknowledged program cases would be left out.
- **"Letters of compelling need."** What the Navy and Air Force called an appeal was a letter from a supervisor or contractor saying an employee was critical to a program and should keep access despite being a potential security risk. The employee was told about neither the letter nor the unfavorable decision. Some accesses were reinstated this way.
- **Contractor employees.** DoD held that the due process rules of Executive Order 10865 did not apply to contractor employees in SAPs. The Army gave them due process anyway; the Navy and Air Force did not. A 1990 industry study group, surveying contractors on 235 programs, found that most contractor employees were never told they had been denied access, given reasons or offered an appeal.
- **SCI access.** All three services gave due process for SCI denials and revocations. The Army used DoD's procedures; the Navy and Air Force used the thinner minimum procedures of Director of Central Intelligence Directive 1/14, which notify the person only after the decision. The Navy's and Air Force's appeal officials often sat in the same command as those who made the decision.
- **Size of the system.** In fiscal year 1992 DoD granted over 68,000 SCI accesses and had about 214,500 in force. It kept no central count of SAP access, but believed it to be between 200,000 and 250,000.
- **Recommendations.** GAO asked the Secretary of Defense to make the Navy and Air Force follow the regulation for military and civilian personnel, to appoint independent appeal officials, to set up oversight to enforce the rules, and to propose similar procedures for contractor employees in the new National Industrial Security Program. DoD answered in a letter of April 29, 1993, to Assistant Comptroller General [[Frank Conahan|Frank C. Conahan]], signed by Maynard Anderson, Assistant Deputy Under Secretary of Defense (Security Policy), and reprinted as Appendix VI. It partly concurred with three recommendations and rejected the first, arguing that DoD could use the Director of Central Intelligence's procedures for programs approved for "upgraded adjudicative criteria". GAO reviewed cases from the first six months of fiscal year 1992, and did its work from May 1992 to February 1993.

## In Gerb's videos

Gerb cites the report in three videos, each time by its full title and as a May 1993 GAO report. In [[UFO Legacy Programs - Science Applications International Corporation (SAIC)|his SAIC video]] he raises it while discussing the [[Wilson-Davis Memo|Wilson-Davis notes]]' claim that legacy programs were reorganized under the [[SAPOC (Special Access Program Oversight Committee)|SAPOC]]'s Senior Review Group after an internal audit almost revealed them, a reorganization that began on January 5, 1994. He offers the report as a possible piece of that background: the Navy and Air Force had not complied with the regulation, while the Army had in some cases. That fits his view that Army material exploitation programs ran under tighter oversight than the Navy's and Air Force's.

He returns to it in [[Lockheed Martin - UFO Reverse Engineering, Material Exploitation, & Legacy Programs|his later Lockheed Martin video]] and in his [[National Reconnaissance Office - UFO Crash Retrievals, Surveillance, and Legacy Program Gatekeepers|National Reconnaissance Office video]], recalling the SAIC video's theory that the need to avoid more exposure through audits drove the SAPOC restructuring. In the Lockheed video he then looks further back, to the [[GAO Report on Special Access Contracts (1986)|1986 GAO report]] on Lockheed's Burbank carve-outs.

Gerb names the regulation as "DoD regulation 62000.2-R" in all three videos. Its number is DoD 5200.2-R. The report does not deal with access for auditors or Congress. Its subject is due process for the individuals whose SAP or SCI access was removed. It mentions GAO's own access only once, where DoD declined to show it unacknowledged program cases.
