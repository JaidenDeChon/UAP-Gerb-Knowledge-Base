Project Grudge was the second official United States Air Force program to investigate UFO reports. It replaced the terminated [[Project Sign]] in 1949 and came before [[Project Blue Book]]. Sign had been tasked with genuine investigation and was at first open to the phenomenon. Grudge, by contrast, was explicitly designed to explain UFO sightings away and so ease public anxiety. It was the first institutionalized effort by the US government to use an official-seeming investigation to manage public perception, in place of scientific inquiry. Although Grudge was officially terminated in 1949, it was later revived under Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]], who went on to direct Project Blue Book, and it carried the Air Force's UFO investigations into the early 1950s. Its report, the [[Project Grudge Report]], is dated August 1949. Gerb says Grudge's records were updated until 1999 and that a full report was published in 1960.

> [!edit] Corrected 16 October 2026
> Earlier versions said as fact that Grudge's records were updated until 1999 and that its full report appeared in 1960. Both are Gerb's claims in [[The Origin of the UFO Stigma]]; the report itself, Technical Report No. 102-AC 49/15-100, is dated August 1949.

## Mandate and Operations

Gerb says Grudge's investigators were tasked with attributing UFO sightings to conventional explanations: balloons, conventional aircraft, optical illusions, planets, mass hallucinations, or similar prosaic causes. The [[Project Grudge Report]] quotes a similar list from its consultant the RAND Corporation, which had found nothing to "seriously controvert simple rational explanations of the various phenomena in terms of balloons, conventional aircraft, planets, meteors, bits of paper, optical illusions, practical jokers, psychopathological reporters, and the like". The program's approach mirrored what [[J. Allen Hynek]] later described as "less science and more of a public relations campaign." Hynek was describing the whole course of Air Force UFO investigations, but the phrase fits Gerb's account of Grudge especially well.

> [!edit] Clarified 16 October 2026
> Earlier versions credited this list of explanations to Grudge's operating directive. No such directive has been sourced; the list is Gerb's, and the closest wording in the record is RAND's comment quoted in the report's summary.

## Leadership

After its official 1949 termination, Grudge continued in reduced form and was reactivated with Captain Edward J. Ruppelt as director. Under Ruppelt the program was reorganized into Project Blue Book in 1952, and Ruppelt continued as Blue Book's first director.

## The Lubbock Lights Case

The Lubbock Lights case shows how Grudge worked. In Lubbock, Texas, four engineering professors witnessed multiple flights of 20 to 30 lights traveling at extraordinary speeds in a perfect semicircle formation. Grudge officially attributed the sightings to a flock of migrating birds. The professors themselves did not accept that explanation, and no corroborating evidence was ever found to support it. The case is frequently cited as an example of the program reaching dismissive conclusions decided in advance.

## Grudge Report

The project's report, "Unidentified Flying Objects – Project GRUDGE" (Technical Report No. 102-AC 49/15-100), is dated August 1949. Its title page names Lt H. W. Smith and G. W. Towles, and it was approved by Lt Col A. J. Hemstreet Jr., chief of the Technical Analysis Division, and, for the Commanding General, by Col Harold E. Watson, chief of the Intelligence Department. It covers incidents 1 to 244, reported up to January 1949, leaves the New Mexico green fireballs aside, and finds that the reports pose "no direct threat to the national security of the United States". It recommended that the investigation be reduced in scope. Its classification was cancelled on 1 August 1952 over Ruppelt's signature. The National Archives' scan, appendices included, runs to 412 pages. See [[Project Grudge Report]].

Gerb says the report ran to 707 pages of case analysis and was published in 1960, and that the records were updated continuously until 1999, long after the program had nominally ended.
