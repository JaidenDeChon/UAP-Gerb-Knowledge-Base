Project Grudge was the second official United States Air Force program to investigate UFO reports. It replaced the terminated [[Project Sign]] in 1949 and came before [[Project Blue Book]]. Sign had been tasked with genuine investigation and was at first open to the phenomenon. Grudge, by contrast, was explicitly designed to explain UFO sightings away and so ease public anxiety. It was the first institutionalized effort by the US government to use an official-seeming investigation to manage public perception, in place of scientific inquiry. Although Grudge was officially terminated in 1949, it was later revived under Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]], who went on to direct Project Blue Book, and it carried the Air Force's UFO investigations into the early 1950s. Gerb says Grudge records "were updated up until 1999," featuring "a full published report in 1960."

## Mandate and Operations

Under Project Grudge's operating directive, investigators were to attribute UFO sightings to conventional explanations: balloons, conventional aircraft, optical illusions, planets, mass hallucinations, or similar prosaic causes. The program's approach mirrored what [[J. Allen Hynek]] later described as "less science and more of a public relations campaign." Hynek was describing the whole course of Air Force UFO investigations, but the phrase fits Grudge's explicit brief especially well.

## Leadership

After its official 1949 termination, Grudge continued in reduced form and was reactivated with Captain Edward J. Ruppelt as director. Under Ruppelt the program was reorganized into Project Blue Book in 1952, and Ruppelt continued as Blue Book's first director.

## The Lubbock Lights Case

The Lubbock Lights case shows how Grudge worked. In Lubbock, Texas, four engineering professors witnessed multiple flights of 20 to 30 lights traveling at extraordinary speeds in a perfect semicircle formation. Grudge officially attributed the sightings to a flock of migrating birds. The professors themselves did not accept that explanation, and no corroborating evidence was ever found to support it. The case is frequently cited as an example of the program reaching dismissive conclusions decided in advance.

## Grudge Report

Although the program was officially terminated in 1949, Gerb says the Grudge records were "updated up until 1999," featuring "a full published report in 1960" that runs to 707 pages of case analysis. If so, the cases kept receiving institutional attention long after the program had nominally ended.
