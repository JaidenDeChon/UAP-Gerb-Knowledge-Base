The Glomar Explorer Operation (also known as Project Azorian or Project Jennifer) was a highly classified CIA operation conducted in 1974 to recover a portion of the sunken Soviet K-129 submarine from the floor of the Pacific Ocean, approximately 1,560 miles northwest of Hawaii, at a depth of about 16,500 feet. The operation used the Hughes Glomar Explorer — a specially constructed deep-sea salvage vessel built at an estimated modern equivalent cost of $1.68 billion — and was conducted under the [[National Underwater Reconnaissance Office]] (NURO), a joint CIA–Navy black agency. The operation publicly recovered approximately one-third of the K-129, including two nuclear torpedoes and the remains of six Soviet sailors. The full extent of what was recovered remains classified.

## Background

The Soviet submarine K-129 sank in the Pacific Ocean in March 1968. The [[National Underwater Reconnaissance Office]] was established in 1969, the year after the sinking, with recovery of the K-129 as one of its founding objectives. Under CIA director Richard Helms, the CIA's Directorate of Science and Technology and the Office of Naval Intelligence worked together through NURO to develop a recovery capability. The CIA commissioned the Hughes Glomar Explorer from Global Marine, with cover provided by industrialist Howard Hughes.

## The Hughes Glomar Explorer

The Glomar Explorer was built under the cover of a deep-sea manganese nodule mining operation. The vessel incorporated a massive claw-like capture vehicle (the Clementine), capable of lowering to the seafloor on a pipe string. The ship included a large central well with a submersible barge (HMB-1) containing the capture vehicle, allowing the entire operation to be concealed within the vessel. The ship launched in 1972 and completed the K-129 recovery operation in 1974. Its modern equivalent construction cost has been estimated at $1.68 billion.

## Alleged UFO Retrieval Connection

In 1978, Ocean Minerals Company — with Lockheed Missiles and Space Company (now [[Lockheed Martin]]) as prime contractor — leased the Glomar Explorer for ostensible deep-sea mining operations that continued for nearly two decades. UAP Gerb argues these operations were a continuation of NURO clandestine missions under Lockheed oversight, potentially including retrieval of non-human craft.

A more specific alleged UFO connection is presented through the testimony of witness RK, documented by [[Leonard Stringfield]]. A San Diego sailor told RK that in June 1973 — the year before the publicly acknowledged K-129 recovery — a UFO was shot down between Hawaii and the mainland by a Navy destroyer and retrieved by the Glomar Explorer. The craft was allegedly shipped to Hawaii, then sent stateside and ultimately to the Great Lakes Naval Base near Chicago, where RK observed a matching craft on a wooden platform inside a Quonset hut in September 1973. [[Bobby Ray Inman]] separately stated he became aware of the Glomar's activities in Hawaii while serving as assistant chief of staff for intelligence there during 1972–1974.

## Legacy

The Glomar Explorer is also the origin of the "Glomar response" — the legal precedent established in *Phillippi v. CIA* that allows government agencies to neither confirm nor deny the existence of records when confirming or denying would itself reveal classified information.
