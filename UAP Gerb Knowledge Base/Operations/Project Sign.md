Project Sign was the first official United States Government program to systematically investigate unidentified flying objects. It ran from 1948 to 1949 under the [[US Air Force]]. It was set up after Lieutenant General [[Nathan Twining]] assessed that flying discs were "real and not visionary or fictitious," and it was the earliest formal acknowledgment at the highest levels of the Air Force that UFO reports deserved serious investigation. Sign investigated military and civilian UFO reports in the late 1940s, including the well-known [[Gorman Dogfight]] of October 1, 1948. General [[Hoyt Vandenberg]] shut the program down in 1949, despite, or because of, its preliminary conclusions supporting the reality of unexplained aerial objects.

## Establishment

Project Sign was set up after General Twining's 1947 memo asserting that flying discs were real, physical phenomena. The memo was addressed to the Commanding General of the Army Air Forces, to the attention of Brigadier General George Schulgen of Air Force intelligence. (The name is garbled in the channel's videos, which render it as "George Shugen" and "George Lugan.") The project was first named "Project Saucer" before it received its official designation.

Sign had a high-level mandate and oversight. All Project Sign reports had to be sent to three major organizations: the [[Army and Navy Research and Development Board]], the [[USAF Scientific Advisory Group]] (the Air Force's civilian scientific advisory body, renamed the USAF Scientific Advisory Board in the late 1940s; the two videos use the two names interchangeably), and the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]. The distribution list shows that concern about UFOs reached across agencies at the highest levels of government and military leadership. It also shows a link to nuclear-adjacent institutions that continued through later programs.

## Gorman Dogfight Investigation

Project Sign investigated the [[Gorman Dogfight]] in detail. On October 1, 1948, Second Lieutenant [[George F. Gorman]] chased an unidentified luminous object over [[Fargo, North Dakota, USA|Fargo, North Dakota]] for 27 minutes. Investigators interviewed Gorman and other witnesses, including air traffic controller [[L.D. Jensen]].

As part of the investigation, Project Sign tested Gorman's P-51 Mustang for radiation. The aircraft was measurably more radioactive than other fighters, and investigators at first concluded that it had flown close to an "atomic powered object." Further analysis attributed the radiation to reduced atmospheric shielding at the 14,000-foot altitude Gorman reached during the pursuit.

Project Sign first put forward conventional explanations such as weather balloons and jets, but found them inconsistent with the evidence. In the end, the program concluded that Gorman had chased either a lit weather balloon or the planet Jupiter, and that the object's fantastic maneuvers were an illusion arising from his frame of reference. That conclusion contradicted sworn testimony from both Gorman and Jensen.

## Findings

Project Sign's written report concluded that some UFO reports describe actual objects but that their origins cannot be determined. Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]], who later directed Sign's successors ([[Project Grudge]] and [[Project Blue Book]]), stated that Sign had internally endorsed the interplanetary hypothesis as the best explanation for some of its unexplained cases. Ruppelt also wrote that the Gorman dogfight was one of three classic UFO incidents in 1948 that "proved to Air Force intelligence specialists that UFOs were real."

## Termination

General [[Hoyt Vandenberg]] shut down Project Sign in 1949, officially citing insufficient proof for the extraterrestrial interpretation. Its replacement, [[Project Grudge]], was commissioned the same year Sign was terminated. Unlike Sign, Grudge was not tasked with investigation: its explicit job was to explain UFO sightings away to "alleviate public anxiety." Replacing Sign with Grudge was the first major documented shift in the US government's stance on UFOs, away from investigation and toward suppression.

## Legacy

Brief as they were, Project Sign's findings left an official record that some UFO reports were genuine unknowns. Ruppelt's assessment of the 1948 cases reflects the Air Force's stance while Sign was running, before it took more dismissive positions under later programs. That finding was overruled administratively, and the programs that followed worked under a different mandate. UAP researcher [[David Grusch]] has cited programs like Sign, Grudge, and [[Project Blue Book]] as part of the "sophisticated disinformation campaigns" he referenced in his congressional testimony.
