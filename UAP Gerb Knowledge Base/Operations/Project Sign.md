Project Sign was the first official United States Government program to systematically investigate unidentified flying objects. It ran from 1948 to 1949 under the [[US Air Force]]. It was set up after Lieutenant General [[Nathan Twining]] assessed that flying discs were "real and not visionary or fictitious," and it was the earliest formal acknowledgment at the highest levels of the Air Force that UFO reports deserved serious investigation. Sign investigated military and civilian UFO reports in the late 1940s, including the well-known [[Gorman Dogfight]] of October 1, 1948. In the autumn of 1948, according to [[Edward J. Ruppelt]], Sign's Top Secret [[Project Sign Estimate of the Situation (1948)|Estimate of the Situation]], which concluded that UFOs were interplanetary, was rejected by General [[Hoyt Vandenberg]] for lack of proof. In February 1949 the project was renamed [[Project Grudge]].

## Establishment

Project Sign was set up after General Twining's 1947 memo asserting that flying discs were real, physical phenomena. The memo was addressed to the Commanding General of the Army Air Forces, to the attention of Brigadier General George Schulgen of Air Force intelligence. (The name is garbled in the channel's videos, which render it as "George Shugen" and "George Lugan.") The project was first named "Project Saucer" before it received its official designation.

Gerb says all Project Sign reports had to be sent to three major organizations: the [[Army and Navy Research and Development Board]], the [[USAF Scientific Advisory Group]] (the Air Force's civilian scientific advisory body, renamed the USAF Scientific Advisory Board in the late 1940s; the two videos use the two names interchangeably), and the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]. Those names come from Twining's letter, which recommended that the study's data go for comment to the Army, the Navy, the AEC, the JRDB, the Air Force Scientific Advisory Group, NACA, and the RAND and NEPA projects. Gerb reads that list as concern about UFOs reaching across agencies at the highest levels of government and military leadership, and as a link to nuclear-adjacent institutions that continued through later programs.

## Gorman Dogfight Investigation

Project Sign investigated the [[Gorman Dogfight]] in detail. On October 1, 1948, Second Lieutenant [[George F. Gorman]] chased an unidentified luminous object over [[Fargo, North Dakota, USA|Fargo, North Dakota]] for 27 minutes. Investigators interviewed Gorman and other witnesses, including air traffic controller [[L.D. Jensen]].

As part of the investigation, Project Sign tested Gorman's P-51 Mustang for radiation. The aircraft was measurably more radioactive than other fighters, and investigators at first concluded that it had flown close to an "atomic powered object." Further analysis attributed the radiation to reduced atmospheric shielding at the 14,000-foot altitude Gorman reached during the pursuit.

Project Sign first put forward conventional explanations such as weather balloons and jets, but found them inconsistent with the evidence. In the end, the program concluded that Gorman had chased either a lit weather balloon or the planet Jupiter, and that the object's fantastic maneuvers were an illusion arising from his frame of reference. That conclusion contradicted sworn testimony from both Gorman and Jensen.

## Findings

Project Sign's written report concluded that some UFO reports describe actual objects but that their origins cannot be determined. Captain [[Edward J. Ruppelt]], who later directed Sign's successors ([[Project Grudge]] and [[Project Blue Book]]), wrote in his 1956 book that Sign had gone further in private. In the late summer or early autumn of 1948 it sent up a Top Secret [[Project Sign Estimate of the Situation (1948)|Estimate of the Situation]] concluding that UFOs were interplanetary. No copy of the Estimate has been made public, and its existence rests on Ruppelt's account and on later ones. Ruppelt also wrote that the Gorman dogfight was one of three classic UFO incidents in 1948 that "proved to Air Force intelligence specialists that UFOs were real."

## Termination

According to Ruppelt, the Estimate reached General [[Hoyt Vandenberg]], the Air Force Chief of Staff, who "wouldn't buy interplanetary vehicles" and sent it back because "the report lacked proof". Some months later, he writes, it was declassified and burned, though a few copies were kept. He doesn't say who ordered that. Gerb tells this as Vandenberg shutting Sign down in 1949 for "lack of proof", and in another video says Vandenberg ordered the Estimate destroyed. By Ruppelt's account the rejection took the heart out of the project, and an order of 11 February 1949 renamed it [[Project Grudge]]. Its replacement, [[Project Grudge]], was commissioned the same year Sign was terminated. Unlike Sign, Grudge was not tasked with investigation: its explicit job was to explain UFO sightings away to "alleviate public anxiety." Replacing Sign with Grudge was the first major documented shift in the US government's stance on UFOs, away from investigation and toward suppression.

## Legacy

Brief as they were, Project Sign's findings left an official record that some UFO reports were genuine unknowns. Ruppelt's assessment of the 1948 cases reflects the Air Force's stance while Sign was running, before it took more dismissive positions under later programs. That finding was overruled administratively, and the programs that followed worked under a different mandate. UAP researcher [[David Grusch]] has cited programs like Sign, Grudge, and [[Project Blue Book]] as part of the "sophisticated disinformation campaigns" he referenced in his congressional testimony.
