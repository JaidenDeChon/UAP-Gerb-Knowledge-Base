**Operation Upshot-Knothole** was a series of 11 nuclear weapons test shots conducted at the [[Nevada National Security Site (Nevada Test Site)|Nevada Proving Ground]] (also known as the Nevada Test Site) between March 17 and June 4, 1953. The [[Atomic Energy Commission]] ran the operation to develop and test tactical nuclear weapons and to prepare the U.S. military for atomic combat. An estimated 18,000 to 21,000 Department of Defense personnel took part, and the tests were a significant expansion of the U.S. nuclear weapons program in the early Cold War.

## Test Program

The 11 shots tried out various nuclear device designs and delivery methods, including the first use of artillery cannons to fire nuclear payloads. The tests took place at several locations within the Nevada Proving Ground and were designed to evaluate:

- Tactical nuclear weapons for battlefield use
- Blast effects on military structures and equipment
- Nuclear artillery capabilities
- Personnel and equipment survivability in nuclear environments

Engineers such as [[Arthur Stansel Jr.]] were assigned to measure blast effects on different types of buildings and structures built specifically for the tests, and specialized teams analyzed how the structures held up under nuclear detonations. Several of the 11 shots produced yields in the low-kiloton range. Troops and equipment were deliberately positioned near the blast zones to judge readiness for nuclear combat, both how well personnel survived and how well equipment performed. The test area lay roughly 65 miles northwest of Las Vegas.

## Connection to UAP Activity

UAP research cites Operation Upshot-Knothole as background to the [[1953 Kingman, Arizona Crash Retrieval]], which occurred on May 21, 1953, while the tests were under way. Intensive nuclear weapons testing at the Nevada Proving Ground was going on close to the alleged Kingman crash location, and AEC personnel working on the test series were immediately diverted to investigate the crash. For these reasons researchers treat the incident as part of the broader [[UFO-Nuclear Connection]], the documented pattern of UAP activity near nuclear test sites, weapons facilities, and missile installations.

[[Arthur Stansel Jr.]], who was working at [[Frenchman's Flat, Nevada]] as part of Operation Upshot-Knothole, received a phone call on May 20, 1953, from [[Edward Bushnell Doll|Ed Doll]], the physicist and project director for the test series. Doll told him to report for a special assignment, which turned out to be the Kingman crash investigation. Stansel's diary from that period records: "Spent most of the day on Frenchman's flat surveying cubicles and supervising welding of plate girder bridge sensor... Drank brew and eve read funny call from Dr. Doll about 1000. I'm going on a special job tomorrow." The speed with which AEC personnel were assembled and transported to the site suggests an established protocol for handling unusual recoveries within the nuclear testing infrastructure.

Independent testimony from [[Bill Uhouse]] about the Kingman craft also supports a nuclear connection. He described the dead occupants as reportedly taken to Los Alamos, the AEC's primary nuclear research facility.

## Organizational Framework

The operation ran under AEC authority, with significant DOD participation. Key personnel included:

- [[Edward Bushnell Doll|Ed Doll]] ([[Edward Bushnell Doll]]): physicist and project director. During the test series he was technical director for two special weapons programs for the [[Armed Forces Special Weapons Project (AFSWP)|Armed Forces Special Weapons Project]].
- [[Eric Henry Wang]]: chief of the special studies office at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base under Air Research and Development Command. By 1956 his department had moved to Sandia Laboratory Complex at [[Kirtland Air Force Base, New Mexico|Kirtland Air Force Base]].

Because Operation Upshot-Knothole personnel later turn up in UAP reverse-engineering allegations, the operation has become a focal point in legacy program research. Much of that research concerns the use of AEC classification authority under the [[Atomic Energy Act of 1954]] to shield UAP-related materials from oversight. Doll's later career at [[TRW]] (from 1955) and TRW's absorption into [[Northrop Grumman]] in 2002 are cited as one path by which custody of Kingman-derived material may be traced into the 21st century. That path includes a theorized connection to the 2011 [[Kona Blue]] technology transfer attempt.
