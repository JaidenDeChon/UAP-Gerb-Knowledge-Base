The Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program (AATIP, also rendered ATIP) is a U.S. Department of Defense program name publicly associated with [[Luis Elizondo]], who has stated he directed it before leaving government service in 2017 and becoming a prominent UAP whistleblower and advocate. Whether AATIP was a formally funded DIA program or an informal, unfunded working group has been publicly disputed since a December 2017 *New York Times* article first revealed its existence. The *Times* reporting is widely credited with conflating AATIP with [[AAWSAP]] (Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications Program), a separate program whose funding is verifiable.

## Program Overview and the AAWSAP Distinction

[[AAWSAP]] was a DIA program that ran from 2008 to 2012 with a documented budget of approximately $22 million. It was funded at the request of then-Senate Majority Leader [[Harry Reid]] and awarded through a sole-source contract to Bigelow Aerospace Advanced Space Studies (BAASS). Its director on the DIA side was James T. Lacatski. How AATIP relates to AAWSAP is still disputed, even in mainstream reporting. AATIP may simply have been AAWSAP's program name within the DoD, a distinct follow-on effort with no funding of its own, or something else. The dispute includes a public disagreement between Senator Reid and DoD spokesperson Garry Reid over Elizondo's actual role and authority. However its institutional status is described, AATIP's work is associated with investigating UAP encounters by military personnel and analyzing UAP flight characteristics. It is also associated with physics-based assessment of reported anomalous flight capabilities, including research into advanced propulsion and metamaterials.

UAP Gerb's *Special Access Required Vol.2* takes a position on this dispute. It argues that ATIP was only an unfunded, informal working group with no appropriations or administrative structure of its own, and that AAWSAP was the program the DIA actually funded. The video alleges that ATIP instead worked as a National Security Council cover vehicle championed by [[James Clapper]], which let officials discuss parts of the alleged legacy program apparatus "outside" that apparatus's usual secrecy. It also connects Elizondo's use of the ATIP platform to a separate role he describes himself: director of the [[National Program Special Management Staff (NPMS)]] from 2013–2017, under Clapper's former USD(I&S) office. [[Luis Elizondo]] has publicly described doing his UAP investigation work "together" with [[Jay Stratton]] and a wider team during this period.

UAP Gerb's *80 Years of UFO Crash Retrieval and Reverse Engineering* states that AATIP ran from roughly 2009 to 2017, though elsewhere the same video says it began in 2012. According to the video, AATIP was partly a cover for several National Security Council activities, with top cover blessed by [[James Clapper]] for what the video calls a partial-disclosure initiative. The video alleges this effort led to attempts, involving [[John Podesta]], to position Hillary Clinton as a "disclosure president," and to the formation of [[To The Stars Academy]]. Those involved in the academy, according to the video, were Podesta, a general referred to as "McAlant," Lockheed [[Lockheed Martin Skunk Works|Skunk Works]] personnel, and possibly other intelligence agency figures. The video says the alleged effort sought to conduct psychological operations, coordinate narratives and disseminate partial information on the UAP topic. It names "equity protection" as the underlying purpose, meaning keeping program secrecy from both the American public and the wider US government, but says it does not know with confidence why the effort was undertaken.

## Five Observables Framework

Under Elizondo's leadership, AATIP developed a framework for identifying and categorizing anomalous aerial phenomena by five observable characteristics that set UAP apart from conventional aircraft:

1. Instantaneous acceleration: sudden changes in velocity far beyond known aerospace capabilities
2. Hypersonic velocity: extreme speeds without sonic booms or visible propulsion
3. Low observability: the ability to avoid radar detection or conventional observation
4. Trans-medium travel: seamless movement between air, water, and space
5. Anti-gravity/No visible propulsion: flight characteristics that suggest gravity manipulation or unknown propulsion systems

The framework has become a standard analytical tool for assessing UAP footage and encounters. The Metapod footage, for example, shows three of these five characteristics.

## Notable UAP Evidence

In a 2021 GQ interview, Elizondo described the three publicly released Pentagon videos (Gimbal, Go Fast, Tic Tac) as "some of the least compelling" UAP footage the government holds. He said far more dramatic evidence exists, including footage of UAPs within 50 feet of aircraft cockpits and a 23-minute-long video. His statements suggest AATIP either had access to, or knew of, much more compelling UAP documentation than what has been publicly released.

## Public Disclosure and Controversy

AATIP came to public attention in December 2017, when the New York Times published an exposé revealing its existence, along with the release of three Pentagon UAP videos (FLIR1, Gimbal, GoFast). Elizondo's resignation letter, which expressed frustration at the lack of resources and attention given to the UAP issue, helped spark wider public and congressional interest in the topic.

Elizondo has stated that while he was at AATIP, the program found evidence of a black UAP program within the DoD, which suggests compartmentalized activities beyond AATIP's official scope. Some sources have described AATIP as a limited disclosure vehicle or "cover program" that hid deeper classified UAP legacy activities, though this is still debated within UAP research circles.

## Legacy and Successor Programs

AATIP's work laid the groundwork for later official UAP investigations, including the [[UAP Task Force]], established in 2020, and the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), created in 2022. Its framework and methods still influence how military and intelligence agencies approach UAP encounters.
